Description

Multiple simulates the Newtonian gravitational motion of two or more point masses in three dimensions. Every massive body is integrated dynamically and exerts gravity on every other body; the optional massless test particles of Beat 9 are the deliberate exception; unlike MercPert, no primary orbit is prescribed and no body is dynamically privileged.

The default example is a planar three-body encounter: three equal-mass stars approach one another, exchange energy and angular momentum, and can form a temporary or long-lived binary while the third body moves onto a much wider trajectory. Longer integrations reveal repeated encounters and binary hardening.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: one pairwise force law, summed over every pair, gives every body's acceleration; energy, momentum, and angular momentum are exactly conserved by the motion that law implies, and the printed conservation diagnostics are how you tell how far to trust a step-by-step calculation of several bodies at once, especially once their mutual gravity makes the motion chaotic.

Reading the output

Every run prints a summary at the console and then opens a plot.

The first line of the summary gives version, build, accepted steps and simulated days. Initial and final E, E_internal, P, L are expressed per solar mass in input coordinates. The maximum drift line scans the accepted history, not just the endpoints. Eleven time-fraction samples show E, E_internal, K, P, L. The display-frame line describes plotting only.

A run with test particles (Beat 9) also prints a table with one row per particle, using astronomical units and years from here on (the summary above it uses days). For each particle: its starting distance r0 from --test_center; a0 and e0, the two-body semi-major axis and eccentricity it would have about a point mass equal to the centre's mass (a starting diagnostic, not proof of a circular orbit in a binary's field; unbound means it starts at or above escape speed in that point-mass picture, and - means undefined, for a particle exactly at the centre); its starting angle phi0 from a reference body (the massive body farthest from that centre); the lowest and highest values that angle reached, followed continuously round the circle; the angle path those values describe; its fate; and when it was removed. The last line gives the fewest dt steps in any particle's starting orbit. It counts the massive bodies' steps, not the smaller steps a particle may take inside them, and it is a quick screen for a timestep that is too coarse, not a guarantee of accuracy.

The fates are working definitions, not physical verdicts. survived means still present when the run ends; on the survival plot an upward triangle marks the run length, a lower limit on how long that particle lasts, not a removal time. escaped means farther than --escape_radius from the massive bodies' centre of mass, which a bound particle on a wide excursion can also do. encounter means it came within a body's --removal_radii value, a close approach; it does not model a collision, a capture or what would happen next. numerical means the program could not follow that particle accurately even with its smallest steps, so it stopped following it.

The angle path of a survivor describes what its angle from the reference body did during this run, followed through every small step the particle took. circulating: it went all the way round. passed body: it crossed the reference body's direction without going all the way round. A swing of that angle alone does not show that a particle shares the planet's orbit: an eccentric orbit speeds up and slows down, so its angle from the planet can swing back and forth while it steadily drifts ahead. The co-orbital names therefore rest on a different angle, printed as dlambda: the particle's mean longitude minus the planet's, both measured about the heavier body. In an unperturbed Kepler orbit the mean longitude advances uniformly even while the actual motion speeds up and slows down; here it is recalculated from the particle's instantaneous orbit, so the planet's pull can change its rate. For a true tadpole or horseshoe, dlambda itself swings back and forth. It is worked out only for orbits within 5° of the x-y plane that go round the same way as the planet; for any other particle (for example one launched out of the plane with --test_velocities_init), or once a particle's orbit leaves that range, the column shows -, the particle is still integrated normally, and it gets no tadpole or horseshoe name. The names also need the two massive bodies to be on a nearly circular orbit in the x-y plane, the ring to be about their centre of mass, the reference body to be the lighter one with less than 3.85% of their total mass (the condition for its L4 and L5 points to be stable), and the particle to start with a semi-major axis within two of the body's Hill radii of the body's own. If all that holds and dlambda has turned back at both ends of the range it finally reached (by at least 1°), a range that stays ahead of the body in its direction of travel is tadpole L4, one that stays behind is tadpole L5, and one that passes the point opposite the body but never the body itself is horseshoe. Anything else is unfinished: this run cannot name it. These names describe this run only: a longer run can turn a tadpole into a horseshoe or remove a horseshoe.

The conservation totals and their drift describe the massive bodies only. They say how well the massive bodies were integrated, not how accurately any test particle was followed.

What this program is, and what it does not model. Multiple is a teaching model of two or more Newtonian point masses in three dimensions. It has no finite stellar radii, tides, collision physics, relativity, gas, or dissipative processes. Exact coincidence of two point masses is singular and the calculation ends with an error rather than a silently wrong answer (Beat 8). Optional test particles (Beat 9) are massless: they feel the massive bodies but pull on nothing. The motion is computed step by step with a predictor and a corrector, so it carries a numerical error that grows at close approach, and three or more bodies generally move chaotically, so two numerically different calculations of the "same" physical problem can separate onto different trajectories after enough close encounters. Use it to see how many-body gravity behaves, and to learn how to judge a numerical result under those conditions.

Beat 0 · Many bodies, one force law

Three equal-mass stars, each about the mass of the Sun, start at the corners of a right triangle — \((4.6\times10^{10},0,0)\), \((-4.6\times10^{10},0,0)\) and \((0,4.6\times10^{10},0)\) m — with two of them already moving at 30 km/s and the third at rest. Ask the program what happens.

python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --n_bodies 3 --masses_solar 1,1,1 \
  --positions_init "46000000000,0,0;-46000000000,0,0;0,46000000000,0" \
  --velocities_init "0,0,0;0,-30000,0;-30000,0,0" --dt 2000 --eps1 0.005 --eps2 1e-07 --display_frame com

8000 accepted steps span 18.094 days. Follow the red, green and blue curves into the close encounter. The initial momentum is (−30000, −30000, 0) m/s, while the energy and angular-momentum totals remain approximately conserved.

Rerun at --max_steps 2000. Describe the geometry before the close encounter. Compare the two simulated durations: accepted-step count is not elapsed time when the timestep adapts.

Note: each body feels every other body. The pairwise gravitational acceleration is proportional to the other body's mass and inversely proportional to separation squared; there is no fixed central mass.

Note: acceleration of body i is \(\mathbf a_i=G M_\odot\sum_{j\ne i}m_j(\mathbf r_j-\mathbf r_i)/|\mathbf r_j-\mathbf r_i|^3\), with m in solar masses. The body's own mass cancels from its acceleration but affects the others.

Beat 3 · Read conserved totals

python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --show_energy_diagnostic

Eleven console samples cover fractions 0.0 through 1.0 of the elapsed time. E includes kinetic and gravitational potential energy; K is kinetic energy alone. E_internal subtracts the bulk centre-of-mass kinetic energy. P and L are the total linear and angular momentum in user coordinates.

Locate the largest sampled kinetic energy, then compare the maximum energy drift line and graph with just the start and end totals. The maximum can occur between printed rows; agreement of rounded endpoints does not bound it.

Note: pairwise forces conserve momentum and angular momentum analytically; an isolated Newtonian system conserves total energy. The displayed drift diagnoses numerical error, rather than a new physical force.

Beat 4 · A vanishing reference total

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,1 --positions_init "5e10,0,0;-5e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,25760,0;0,-25760,0" --max_steps 3000 --output_type trajectories

Equal and opposite initial momenta cancel. A fractional error divided by the initial momentum norm would divide by zero. The code instead normalizes against the sum of individual initial momentum magnitudes when the total nearly cancels; the initial-scale value is 51520 m/s for this example.

Change only the second body's y velocity to −25759 m/s. Compare the reported momentum drift with the exact-cancellation run. The scale-aware fallback also applies near cancellation, not only at exact zero.

Change --projection yz on the original two-body command. Why does a face-on circle collapse to a nearly straight trace? Return to xy for the rest of the evening.

Beat 5 · Predict, correct, repeat

Keep the same initial state and accepted-step ceiling while changing the corrector threshold:

For comparison, the default settings are --eps2 1e-07.

python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --eps2 1e-10 --dt 2000
python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --eps2 0.004 --dt 2000

The corrector revises the predictor's new velocity using accelerations evaluated at trial positions. eps2 sets a velocity-increment stopping criterion; eps1 controls predictor acceleration change. These are algorithm thresholds, not promised fractional errors in the final path.

Compare elapsed days and maximum conservation drifts with Beat 0. Eight thousand accepted steps at different settings need not cover the same physical interval. For a convergence claim, compare at a common simulated time and check positions as well as drift.

Beat 9 · Jupiter clears its neighbourhood

A test particle feels every massive body but pulls on none, so it cannot change the massive bodies' motion. Each one costs one force term per massive body: 100 test particles around two stars need 200 terms per force evaluation, where 102 bodies that all pull on one another would need 5151 pairs.

The International Astronomical Union's 2006 definition of a planet requires, among other things, that the body has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. A run of the program cannot test that definition, but it can show the mechanism behind it. The Sun and Jupiter (\(9.548\times10^{-4}\) solar masses) start 5.2026 AU apart on a circular orbit with their centre of mass at rest. Forty-one test particles are launched from 3.00 to 5.00 AU, in steps of 0.05 AU (1 AU \(=1.495978707\times10^{11}\) m), each with the circular speed it would have about a point mass at the centre of mass:

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,9.548e-4 --positions_init "-7.4241e8,0,0;7.77555e11,0,0" \
  --velocities_init "0,-12.462,0;0,13051.96,0" --dt 1.25e6 --eps1 0.05 --max_steps 36000 \
  --output_type survival --test_ring "4.48794e11,7.47989e11,41" --removal_radii "6.96e8,5.31e10" \
  --escape_radius 3e12

--test_ring spreads the particles around the centre of mass, each one 137.5° (the golden angle) further round than the one before, all moving counterclockwise like Jupiter; --test_phases random scatters them at random angles instead. --removal_radii removes a particle that comes within the Sun's radius, or within 0.355 AU of Jupiter: Jupiter's Hill radius \(r_H=a\,(m/3M)^{1/3}\), inside which Jupiter's pull outweighs the Sun's tidal pull. --escape_radius removes a particle that gets farther than 20 AU from the centre of mass. --output_type survival keeps no paths. It records when and why each particle is removed, then plots survival time against starting distance. When you close that window, a second one shows where some of the survivors are at the end of the run. The step ceiling --dt 1.25e6 is 1/300 of Jupiter's period and gives the innermost particle about 131 steps per orbit. --eps1 0.05 lets that ceiling set the step; with the default 0.005, the acceleration-change test alone would demand about 1250 steps per orbit.

The 36000 steps cover 1426 years, about 120 Jupiter orbits; on a typical computer the run takes under a minute. Jupiter removes 12 of the 41 particles, every one from 4.35 AU to 4.90 AU, each by a close approach inside its Hill radius. The first goes after 1.6 years; most go within a few dozen Jupiter orbits; the last, at 4.85 AU, lasts 552 years. Every particle from 3.00 AU to 4.30 AU survives, and so do the two at 4.95 and 5.00 AU. Those two are not ordinary asteroids inside Jupiter's orbit. The table calls 4.95 AU a horseshoe and 5.00 AU a tadpole L4: both now share Jupiter's orbit, and only the tadpole is a Trojan. The second command below looks at that difference on purpose.

The edge near 4.3 AU matches a standard estimate. Close to a planet, its mean-motion resonances crowd together until they overlap, and nearly circular orbits there become chaotic. The chaotic zone reaches about \(\Delta a\approx1.3\,\mu^{2/7}a\) inward from the planet's orbit, where \(\mu\) is the planet-to-Sun mass ratio (Wisdom, 1980). For Jupiter that is 0.93 AU, so the zone begins near 4.27 AU. The estimate is an approximate boundary for particles in the planet's plane, not a wall, and it does not promise that every orbit inside it is removed in a given run.

This run shows Jupiter emptying its neighbourhood of nearly circular orbits within about a hundred orbits, a tiny fraction of the age of the Solar System. That is the dynamical reason a planet ends up dominating its orbital zone, which is what the clearing criterion is about. It is an illustration, not a test of the definition: the Trojans, which share Jupiter's orbit and are not cleared, are allowed for because Jupiter controls them. Pluto, by contrast, shares its zone with many bodies it does not control.

Rerun with --max_steps 3000, about 119 years. Which of the 12 removed particles are still present at the end? What does that tell you about choosing the length of a survival run?

Now look along Jupiter's own orbit. Put 24 particles at random angles on each of nine circles from 5.00 to 5.40 AU:

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,9.548e-4 --positions_init "-7.4241e8,0,0;7.77555e11,0,0" \
  --velocities_init "0,-12.462,0;0,13051.96,0" --dt 1.5e6 --eps1 0.05 --max_steps 20000 \
  --output_type survival --test_ring "7.47989e11,8.07828e11,9" --test_ring_per_radius 24 \
  --test_phases random --test_seed 1 --removal_radii "6.96e8,5.31e10" --escape_radius 3e12

--test_seed 1 makes the random angles the same every time; change it to draw a different set. Of the 216 particles, 4 start inside Jupiter's Hill radius and are removed at once. After 951 years, 189 survive. Their angle paths (see Reading the output) are what matter: 72 are tadpoles at L4 and 72 at L5, 44 are horseshoes and 1 circulates. A tadpole's angle swings back and forth on one side of Jupiter, around the point 60° ahead (L4) or behind (L5), without ever reaching Jupiter or the point opposite it. These are Trojan orbits. With this seed, every tadpole started at least 26° from Jupiter. In the right-hand panel of the plot they fill most of each circle; the horseshoes cluster at the ends of the radius range and near the far side of the orbit.

Close that window and the second one opens. It shows the tadpoles (blue) and the circulating particle (green) at the end of the run, and leaves out the removed particles and every horseshoe: a single run cannot say which horseshoes would be removed later, so they are not drawn. The circulating particle is not a Trojan and need not last either. Angles are still measured from Jupiter, so in the right-hand view from above, Jupiter stays fixed on the right, with its L4 and L5 points marked. Every L4 tadpole ends between 27° and 162° ahead of Jupiter and every L5 tadpole between 29° and 156° behind it, all between 4.96 and 5.46 AU from the centre of mass. Seen from above, they form two long arcs along Jupiter's orbit, one on each side of the planet, through L4 and L5.

Triple --max_steps to 60000, about 2850 years. Which kind of survivor loses members, and which keeps every one?

Note: each test particle has its own step control. Within one step of the massive bodies, a particle's step is halved, as often as needed, while its acceleration changes by more than eps1, its corrector does not settle to eps2, or the straight line between its start and end points relative to a body passes inside that body's removal radius; the massive bodies' positions within the step come from the same smooth interpolation the animation uses. Where the separate pulls on a particle nearly cancel, as at the centre of two equal stars, the change in acceleration is judged against a tenth of the sum of those pulls rather than against the tiny net pull. Removal is checked at the end of every such small step. At the smallest step allowed (1/65536 of the massive bodies' step), a state that is not finite, a corrector that has not settled, or an acceleration change beyond eps1 gives the fate numerical, and the particle's last good state is kept. Only a step that passes all of those checks while its straight line crosses a removal radius is recorded instead as an encounter at the crossing point. A curved path that dips into a removal radius between two of those points while the straight line between them does not can still be missed. All this greatly reduces close-approach errors, but it does not bound the error a particle builds up over a long run, and each halving costs time, so keep the printed fewest steps per starting orbit above about 100 and expect runs with many close approaches to be slower. The conservation drift does not include the test particles, so it says nothing about their accuracy. The practical check is to rerun with half the --dt and twice the --max_steps, confirm from the printed simulated time that both runs cover the same time (they do here, because the step stays at its ceiling), and compare which particles survive.

Code files

main.py parses command-line options and prints the summary; physics_multiple.py evaluates Newtonian forces on massive bodies and test particles, and conservation totals; driver_multiple.py integrates and samples the run; plot_multiple.py draws static paths, drift graphs, animation and test-particle survival. tests/test_physics_multiple.py exercises the code and this Help file together.

Experiment 10 — Advanced
Galaxy Collision — Optional Toy Model, and Past the Trust Limit
python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1e11,1e11 --positions_init "-7.7e20,0,0;7.7e20,0,0" --velocities_init "50000,0,0;-50000,0,0" --dt 1e12 --max_steps 10000 --output_type trajectories

Two \(10^{11}\,M_\odot\) cores 50 kpc apart approaching at 100 km/s would take order \(5\times10^8\) yr to cross that separation at constant speed, but their mutual gravity accelerates the infall, so this exactly radial pair reaches closest approach sooner, near \(2.4\times10^8\) yr. Place a few companion particles about 10 kpc from each core; at that radius a \(10^{11}\,M_\odot\) point mass has a circular speed of about 207 km/s and an escape speed of about 293 km/s. First give the companions negligible mass and follow the approach only while the calculation remains resolved; then raise them toward \(10^{10}\,M_\odot\). This is a handful of point masses, not a collisionless galaxy or a merger with gas, friction, or radiation.

Then push the same head-on pair, with no companions and no softening, past where the program can still integrate it:

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1e11,1e11 --positions_init "-7.7e20,0,0;7.7e20,0,0" --velocities_init "50000,0,0;-50000,0,0" --dt 1e12 --max_steps 17800 --output_type trajectories

Experiment 11 — Advanced
Planets Around a Binary Star
python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,1 --positions_init "7.48e10,0,0;-7.48e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,21061,0;0,-21061,0" --dt 1.1e5 --eps1 0.05 --max_steps 60000 --output_type survival --test_ring "2.244e11,5.236e11,41" --removal_radii "7.5e9,7.5e9" --escape_radius 3e12

Two one-solar-mass stars 1 AU apart orbit their centre of mass counterclockwise every 0.707 years. Forty-one test particles are launched around both stars, from 1.50 to 3.50 AU from the centre of mass, moving counterclockwise with the circular speed for a point mass of two solar masses at the centre. A particle is removed if it comes within 0.05 AU of either star (a close approach, not the star's surface) or gets farther than 20 AU away. Which of these starting conditions survive this run?

Check: 60000 steps reach 209.14 years, about 296 binary orbits. Fourteen particles are removed (12 escaped, 2 encounters): every one from 1.50 to 2.05 AU, and those at 2.20 and 2.25 AU. Every particle from 2.30 AU outward survives, and so do the two at 2.10 and 2.15 AU. Rerun with --dt 5.5e4 --max_steps 120000, which covers the same 209 years with half the step: exactly the same particles survive, though some removed ones go at different times or by a different route.

A resolved encounter or a pass beyond the escape radius fails this experiment's survival criterion; a survival in a 296-orbit run does not yet show stability. Holman and Wiegert (1999; full reference after Experiment 12) followed particles at eight evenly spaced starting angles per distance for 10,000 binary periods and found, for a circular equal-mass pair, an empirical inner boundary near 2.4 AU: the innermost distance at which all eight of their sampled angles survived. That is not a promise that every orbit farther out survives; they also found unstable islands beyond it, and their removal rules differ from these. Each distance here has a single starting angle, and in the chaotic zone a particle can live or die according to that angle, so a different ring will not reproduce these removals particle by particle. Try eight random angles per distance near the edge:

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,1 --positions_init "7.48e10,0,0;-7.48e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,21061,0;0,-21061,0" --dt 1.1e5 --eps1 0.05 --max_steps 60000 --output_type survival --test_ring "2.8424e11,4.0392e11,9" --test_ring_per_radius 8 --test_phases random --test_seed 1 --removal_radii "7.5e9,7.5e9" --escape_radius 3e12

Record how many of the eight survive at each distance from 1.9 to 2.7 AU. With this seed: none at 1.9 AU, 4 at 2.0, 4 at 2.1, 5 at 2.2, and all 8 from 2.3 AU outward. Then double --max_steps with the same seed and see whether the mixed band moves. The angle path column is not the result here: the stars have equal masses, so the angles are measured from body 1 and every survivor simply circulates. The second window marks the stars' two equilateral points, 90° from body 1 and 0.87 AU from the centre of mass; for equal masses they are not stable, and the legend says so.

Extends Beat 9.

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,1 --positions_init "7.48e10,0,0;-7.48e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,21061,0;0,-21061,0" --dt 9e3 --eps1 0.05 --max_steps 45000 --output_type survival --test_ring "1.496e10,6.732e10,36" --test_center 1 --removal_radii "7.5e9,7.5e9" --escape_radius 3e12

Same binary, but now 36 test particles circle star 1 alone, from 0.10 to 0.45 AU. --test_center 1 centres the ring on body 1 and gives each particle body 1's velocity plus the circular speed for body 1's mass alone.

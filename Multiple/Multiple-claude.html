Overview

Multiple simulates the Newtonian gravitational motion of two or more point masses in three dimensions. Every body is integrated dynamically and exerts gravity on every other body; unlike MercPert, no primary orbit is prescribed and no body is dynamically privileged.

The default example is a planar three-body encounter in the spirit of Schutz's example: three equal-mass stars approach one another, exchange energy and angular momentum, and can form a temporary or long-lived binary while the third body moves onto a much wider trajectory. Longer integrations reveal repeated encounters and binary hardening.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: one pairwise force law, summed over every pair, gives every body's acceleration; energy, momentum, and angular momentum are exactly conserved by the motion that law implies, and the printed conservation diagnostics are how you tell how far to trust a step-by-step calculation of several bodies at once, especially once their mutual gravity makes the motion chaotic. Beats 0 to 3 build up the force law and the conserved quantities, Beat 4 explains a diagnostic refinement this release adds, Beats 5 to 7 open up the numerical method, and Beat 8 uses everything before it to ask how far a given run can be trusted.

What this program is, and what it does not model. Multiple is a teaching model of two or more Newtonian point masses in three dimensions. It has no finite stellar radii, tides, collision physics, relativity, gas, or dissipative processes. Exact coincidence of two point masses is singular and the calculation ends with an error rather than a silently wrong answer (Beat 8). The motion is computed step by step with a predictor and a corrector, so it carries a numerical error that grows at close approach, and three or more bodies generally move chaotically, so two numerically different calculations of the "same" physical problem can separate onto different trajectories after enough close encounters. Use it to see how many-body gravity behaves, and to learn how to judge a numerical result under those conditions.

How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose printed summary illustrates it, and then the equation the summary follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is those equations at work, not independent evidence that they are right. The one outside check this page uses is hand arithmetic on the printed totals themselves — for example, that the printed momentum after a Galilean boost equals the old momentum plus the added velocity times the total mass (Beat 2), or that the sum of each body's mass times its instantaneous acceleration is zero (Beat 1). Both follow from the equations directly and do not depend on the integrator.

Every beat uses the same program. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Beat 0 runs the default three-body encounter, Beat 1 gives the three bodies different masses, Beat 2 boosts the whole system and compares the two display frames, Beat 3 reads the full conservation table, Beat 4 looks at a two-body configuration whose momentum cancels exactly, Beats 5 and 6 open up the predictor–corrector and the adaptive step, Beat 7 contrasts the two output modes, and Beat 8 pushes a calculation to where it stops being trustworthy and then past where it runs at all. Run every command from the program's folder. Lengths are in metres, speeds in metres per second, masses in solar masses, and energies, momenta and angular momenta are printed already scaled by one solar mass, as the console labels them. Simulated times are printed in days.

What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag. A red LAW tag marks a law of nature the model adopts and does not derive: Eq. 1 is the only one. A green ODE tag marks a differential equation the program integrates. A grey DEFINITION tag marks a quantity defined by a formula. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows from equations already stated. A violet ALGORITHM tag marks a rule of the numerical method — a choice the program makes, not a physical law. The tags are there so a law, a definition, and a numerical rule are never mistaken for one another.

Beat 0 · Many bodies, one force law

Three equal-mass stars, each about the mass of the Sun, start at the corners of a right triangle — \((4.6\times10^{10},0,0)\), \((-4.6\times10^{10},0,0)\) and \((0,4.6\times10^{10},0)\) m — with two of them already moving at 30 km/s and the third at rest. Ask the program what happens.

python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories

Look at the six lines from Multiple 1.4.0 ... through Maximum conservation drift, and at fraction 0.0 of the conservation table.

Three tasks, in order. First, read accepted steps: 8000; simulated time: 1.8094e+01 days: 8000 accepted steps cover 18.094 days. Then read the initial totals: \(E=-4.6226\times10^9\), \(P=(-3.0\times10^4,-3.0\times10^4,0)\) m/s, \(L=(0,0,2.76\times10^{15})\) m\(^2\)/s. Then read the final totals, printed to the same four digits: identical. The Maximum conservation drift line does not mean the run stayed that quiet throughout: energy drift \(3.2235\times10^{-4}\), momentum drift \(6.8454\times10^{-14}\), angular-momentum drift \(3.7074\times10^{-6}\). The energy figure alone is an absolute swing of about \(1.4901\times10^{6}\) m\(^2\)/s\(^2\) — roughly fifteen times the last printed digit of \(E_0=-4.6226\times10^9\), not a fraction of it. That swing happens partway through the run, around day 17.59 (a close pass this page returns to in Beat 8), well after the initial state and well before the final one, so neither printed total, nor any of the eleven rows of the conservation table (Beat 3), samples it directly. The initial and final totals agree because the run happens to start and end away from that excursion, not because the excursion is too small to show up. Before reading on, count how many distinct pairs of bodies there are among the three, and guess how that count would grow if a fourth body joined.

Then the law that moves all three. Each body pulls on each other body with a force that grows with both masses and falls as the square of their separation, directed along the line between them:

Eq. 1 — Newton's law of gravitation, one pair LAW
\[ \mathbf a_{A\leftarrow B} \;=\; \frac{G\,m_B}{r_{AB}^{3}}\,(\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A), \qquad r_{AB}=|\mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B|. \]

This is exactly Binary's Eq. 1, restated as an acceleration rather than a force so it can be summed. With only two bodies this is already the whole calculation; with three or more, a body's total acceleration is the vector sum of every such pull acting on it:

Eq. 2 — Equations of motion, body A ODE
\[ \ddot{\mathbf r}_A \;=\; \sum_{B\neq A} \frac{G\,m_B}{r_{AB}^{3}}\,(\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A). \]

In the code the masses are stored in solar-mass units and the constant \(GM_\odot=1.3271244 \times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\) is factored out, so compute_accelerations() in physics_multiple.py evaluates Eq. 2 with \(G m_B\) replaced by \(GM_\odot\,\tilde m_B\), where \(\tilde m_B\) is body B's mass in solar units. Every unordered pair is evaluated once, and Newton's third law lets the same evaluation update both bodies" running accelerations at once:

Eq. 3 — Pair-loop acceleration ALGORITHM
\[ \mathbf a_A \mathrel{+}= \frac{GM_\odot\,\tilde m_B}{r_{AB}^{3}}\,\boldsymbol\Delta_{AB}, \qquad \mathbf a_B \mathrel{-}= \frac{GM_\odot\,\tilde m_A}{r_{AB}^{3}}\,\boldsymbol\Delta_{AB}, \qquad \boldsymbol\Delta_{AB}=\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A. \]

Because the two updates use the same computed \(\boldsymbol\Delta_{AB}\) and \(r_{AB}\) with opposite signs, each pair costs one distance calculation, not two. Three bodies make \(3\times2/2=3\) such pairs; ten bodies would make 45, and the cost of one force evaluation grows as \(N(N-1)/2\) (Governing Equations). Every printed and plotted quantity in this program is a consequence of Eq. 2, evaluated once per body per force evaluation and integrated forward in time (Beat 5).

Eq. 2 says nothing about which body has which mass beyond the \(m_B\) of the body doing the pulling. Beat 1 asks what that means for how far each body actually moves.

Beat 1 · Every body answers to every other

Equation 2 sums the pulls of every other body, but says nothing about the mass of the body being pulled. Keep the default positions and velocities from Beat 0 but make the three masses unequal, and see what each body's initial acceleration depends on.

python main.py --masses_solar 2,1,0.5 --max_steps 2000 --output_type trajectories

Look at the same six lines as Beat 0. Compare the initial totals with Beat 0's: energy is now \(-5.2701\times10^9\), momentum \((-1.5\times10^4,-3.0\times10^4,0)\) m/s, and angular momentum \((0,0,2.07\times10^{15})\) m\(^2\)/s.

Three tasks, in order. First, notice that momentum and angular momentum no longer match Beat 0's: body 0's velocity is still zero, but its mass is now 2 solar masses instead of 1, so it no longer contributes zero to every sum trivially — only its velocity being zero keeps it out of \(\mathbf P\), while \(L\) drops because the two moving bodies now carry different masses. The energy drift is \(8.0878\times10^{-6}\), even smaller than Beat 0's, because this shorter run (2000 steps instead of 8000) has not yet reached the encounter that eventually stresses Beat 0's integration (Beat 6). Then evaluate the three initial accelerations directly from Eq. 2 — not from the program's plot, but from the same formula it uses:

python3 -c " import physics_multiple as phys, numpy as np pos = np.array([[4.6e10,0.,0.],[-4.6e10,0.,0.],[0.,4.6e10,0.]]) masses = np.array([2.,1.,0.5]) print(phys.compute_accelerations(pos, masses)) "

which prints accelerations of magnitude 0.028972, 0.043871 and 0.070121 m/s\(^2\) for the 2, 1 and 0.5 solar-mass bodies respectively. The heaviest body has the smallest acceleration and the lightest has the largest — the opposite of what "more force on a bigger mass" might suggest at first, and Eq. 2 explains why: body A's own mass, \(m_A\), does not appear anywhere on the right-hand side. A body's acceleration depends only on the other bodies" masses and positions, never on its own; the same cancellation Binary's Beat 1 finds for two bodies holds pair by pair here too. Then check that the three bodies still push back on one another exactly as hard as they are pushed. Multiply each acceleration by its own body's mass and add the three vectors: \(2\times(-0.026767,0.011087,0)+1\times(0.042446,0.011087,0)+0.5\times(0.022174,-0.066523,0)\) comes to \((0,\,0,\,0)\) to six decimal places. That is Newton's third law summed over every pair at once: mass-weighted, the three accelerations cancel exactly, even though the accelerations themselves do not.

Then why the cancellation is not a coincidence. Multiply Eq. 2 by \(m_A\) for every body and add all three equations. Every pairwise term \(m_A\,\dfrac{Gm_B}{r_{AB}^3}(\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A)\) appears twice, once from body A's equation and once, with the opposite sign and \(m_B\) swapped for \(m_A\), from body B's, and the pair of terms cancels (this is Eq. 3's pair-loop form). Summed over all pairs, the right-hand side vanishes identically:

Eq. 4 — Total momentum has no net force DERIVED
\[ \sum_A m_A\,\ddot{\mathbf r}_A \;=\; \mathbf 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot{\mathbf P} \;=\; \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbf P \equiv \sum_A m_A\mathbf v_A. \]

That is exactly the hand check above, generalized to any number of bodies and any instant, not just \(t=0\): the mass-weighted sum of the accelerations is always zero, so the total momentum \(\mathbf P\) never changes under Eq. 2. Beat 2 makes \(\mathbf P\) itself, and the closely related center of mass, the main subject.

Beat 2 · Momentum, boosts, and two display frames

Beat 1 showed that the total momentum \(\mathbf P\) of an isolated system never changes. Test what happens to \(\mathbf P\), and to the plot, when every body is given the same extra velocity — a Galilean boost — and compare the program's two ways of displaying the result.

python main.py --velocities_init "100000,0,0;100000,-30000,0;70000,0,0" --max_steps 2000 --output_type trajectories --display_frame user
python main.py --velocities_init "100000,0,0;100000,-30000,0;70000,0,0" --max_steps 2000 --output_type trajectories --display_frame com

The velocities above are Beat 0's default \((0,0,0)\), \((0,-30000,0)\), \((-30000,0,0)\) with \(\mathbf u=(1.0\times10^5,0,0)\) m/s added to every body. Look at the printed totals and the Display frame line in both runs, and at the orbit plot.

Three tasks, in order. First, the momentum. Beat 0's initial \(\mathbf P_{\rm old}\) was \((-3.0\times10^4,-3.0\times10^4,0)\) m/s; here it is \((2.7\times10^5,-3.0\times10^4,0)\) m/s. The \(y\) and \(z\) components are unchanged and the \(x\) component has grown by exactly \(3.0\times10^5\), which is the total mass, 3 solar masses, times the added \(u_x=1.0\times10^5\) m/s: \(\mathbf P_{\rm new}=\mathbf P_{\rm old}+M\mathbf u\), not \(M\mathbf u\) alone, because Beat 0's system already had a nonzero momentum before the boost. Then the energy. It jumps from \(-4.6226\times10^9\) to \(7.3774\times10^9\), a change of \(1.2000\times10^{10}\); this is \(\tfrac12 M u^2+\mathbf P_{\rm old}\!\cdot\!\mathbf u=\tfrac12(3)(10^5)^2+(-3\times10^4)(10^5)= 1.5\times10^{10}-3\times10^9=1.2\times10^{10}\), confirming the general Galilean energy shift below. Meanwhile \(E_{\rm internal}\) is \(-4.9226\times10^9\) in both the boosted runs and in Beat 0, unchanged by the boost to eleven figures at \(t=0\). Then the two display-frame runs: their printed totals and drift line are identical to each other, run for run, because --display_frame only changes what the plot subtracts before drawing; the user run's plot drifts across its axes as the whole system moves at speed \(u\), and the com run's plot stays centered on the moving center of mass.

Then the two ideas behind those numbers. The center of mass and its velocity are:

Eq. 5 — Center of mass and its velocity DEFINITION
\[ \mathbf R_{\rm CM}=\frac{\sum_A m_A\mathbf r_A}{\sum_A m_A}, \qquad \mathbf V_{\rm CM}=\frac{\sum_A m_A\mathbf v_A}{\sum_A m_A}=\frac{\mathbf P}{M}. \]

Adding a common velocity \(\mathbf u\) to every body's velocity adds \(\mathbf u\) to \(\mathbf V_{\rm CM}\) and, from Eq. 5, adds \(M\mathbf u\) to \(\mathbf P=\sum_A m_A\mathbf v_A\) — the relation checked by hand above. Positions, and therefore the relative geometry that Eq. 2 depends on, are untouched, so the boosted system evolves exactly like the unboosted one, with every position simply gaining \(\mathbf u t\) relative to the unboosted run (the unboosted center of mass was already moving at its own \(\mathbf V_{\rm CM}\), so this describes the difference between the two runs, not the boosted run's total drift). A common velocity boost changes both conserved totals: \(\mathbf P\) by \(M\mathbf u\), and \(E\) by \(\mathbf P_{\rm old}\!\cdot\!\mathbf u+\tfrac12 M|\mathbf u|^2\) — exactly the \(1.2\times10^{10}\) computed above. Neither ordinary kinetic energy nor total momentum is invariant under this kind of boost; a boost is a change of velocity, not of coordinate origin. (A spatial translation of the origin, with no change to any velocity, leaves both \(K\) and \(\mathbf P\) unchanged — a different symmetry from the one exercised here.) What Eq. 6 below shows is narrower and more useful: a specific combination of \(E\) and \(\mathbf P\), the internal energy \(E_{\rm internal}\), is unchanged by a boost even though \(E\) and \(\mathbf P\) separately are not. Splitting each body's velocity into \(\mathbf V_{\rm CM}\) plus its velocity relative to the center of mass splits the total kinetic energy into a center-of-mass piece and an internal piece, and \(E_{\rm internal}\) used throughout this program's console output subtracts exactly the center-of-mass piece:

Eq. 6 — Boost-independent internal energy DERIVED
\[ E_{\rm internal} \;=\; E - \frac{|\mathbf P|^2}{2M}. \]

Because \(\mathbf P\) itself transforms as \(\mathbf P\to\mathbf P+M\mathbf u\), it is not obvious by inspection that \(E_{\rm internal}\) comes out the same before and after a boost, but Beat 3 derives \(E\) and \(P\) in full and the numbers above confirm it holds at \(t=0\) to the precision printed. The --display_frame option controls only the plotted origin: with 'com', the default, every plotted position has the instantaneous \(\mathbf R_{\rm CM}\) of Eq. 5 subtracted before drawing, so a uniformly drifting system stays visually centered; with 'user', the raw integration coordinates are drawn as-is. The stored integration coordinates, and everything the console prints, are unaffected either way — they always use the frame you supplied in positions_init and velocities_init. Pressing F during an animation toggles between the two without restarting the run.

Beat 3 · Energy, internal energy, and angular momentum

Beat 0's printed totals agreed to four digits at the start and the end of that run, even though its Maximum conservation drift line reported an excursion far larger than that precision, hidden between the two printed totals. Read the full conservation table for that same run to see more of what happens in between, not just at the two ends.

python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories

Look at all eleven rows of Conservation over ten equal time intervals, in particular the K column and the E and E_internal columns.

Three tasks, in order. First, read \(K\) down the table: 9.0000e+08, 8.5502e+08, 9.2612e+08, 1.1262e+09, 1.4963e+09, 2.1326e+09, 3.2828e+09, 5.8220e+09, 1.6250e+10, 1.5392e+10, 1.6084e+10. \(K\) is not monotonic and swings by more than a factor of 18 over the run, largest near the end. Then read \(E\) and \(E_{\rm internal}\) down the same eleven rows: both stay at \(-4.6226\times 10^9\) and \(-4.9226\times10^9\) to the four digits printed, in every row, even while \(K\) is swinging. \(E=K+U\) (Eq. 9 below) is exactly constant for the true motion, as the next paragraph shows, so if \(K\) is not, \(U\) must swing oppositely by very nearly the same amount: kinetic and potential energy are trading back and forth as the bodies move closer together and farther apart. That trade is exact for the true solution; the program's computed \(E\) carries the same small numerical drift Beat 0 already found (up to \(3.2235\times10^{-4}\) at its worst), so the computed \(K\) and \(U\) trade off only as exactly as that drift allows — closely, at every printed row, but not perfectly at every accepted step. Then read \(L_z\), the only nonzero component here, down the same rows: 2.7600e+15 in every row printed, though the Maximum conservation drift line for this run already reported a nonzero angular momentum drift of \(3.7074\times10^{-6}\) — a real, if tiny, departure that eleven rows at four significant figures cannot show.

Then the three quantities and why two of them, not three, are exactly conserved by the exact equations. Every body carries kinetic energy, and every pair carries a mutual potential energy:

Eq. 7 — Kinetic energy DEFINITION
\[ K \;=\; \tfrac12\sum_A m_A v_A^2 . \]
Eq. 8 — Gravitational potential energy DERIVED
\[ U \;=\; -\sum_{A<B} \frac{G\,m_A m_B}{r_{AB}} . \]
Eq. 9 — Total mechanical energy DEFINITION
\[ E \;=\; K+U . \]

Equation 8 follows from Eq. 1 the same way it does for two bodies (Binary's Eq. 6): \(-U\) is the work needed to separate every pair to infinity, summed over pairs. For Beat 0's initial state, \(K_0=9.0000\times10^8\) and \(E_0=-4.6226\times10^9\), so \(U_0=E_0-K_0=-5.5226\times10^9\), which matches \(-GM_\odot\sum_{A<B}\tilde m_A\tilde m_B/r_{AB}\) evaluated directly from the three starting positions. The program omits one common solar-mass factor from \(K\), \(U\) and \(E\) as printed, the same convention as the accelerations (Beat 0); this does not affect any conservation test, since it is a fixed overall scale. Exact motion under Eqs. 1 and 2 conserves \(E\), just as it conserves the total momentum of Beat 1's Eq. 4, so any drift the program reports in either one is purely numerical (Beat 8). Total angular momentum about the coordinate origin,

Eq. 10 — Total angular momentum DEFINITION
\[ \mathbf L \;=\; \sum_A m_A\,\mathbf r_A\times\mathbf v_A , \]

is exactly conserved too, because the pairwise force in Eq. 1 acts along the line joining each pair and so exerts no torque about any fixed point. \(E\) and \(\mathbf L\) are conserved in every inertial frame, including the boosted one of Beat 2, but a large center-of-mass kinetic energy \(K_{\rm CM}=|\mathbf P|^2/(2M)\) can make a fractional energy drift look artificially small, and the same bulk motion adds \(M(\mathbf R_{\rm CM}\times\mathbf V_{\rm CM})\) to \(\mathbf L\) about the origin. Setting --display_frame com does not change \(E\), \(\mathbf P\), \(\mathbf L\), or the drift line; it only recenters the plot (Beat 2). The printed \(E_{\rm internal}=E-|\mathbf P|^2/(2M)\), Eq. 6, already removes the \(K_{\rm CM}\) piece without rewriting the initial data by hand.

Beat 4 · When a conserved total is (almost) exactly zero

Every drift line so far has been a fraction of the initial total: energy drift divided by \(|E_0|\), momentum drift divided by \(|\mathbf P_0|\). That works as long as the initial total is not itself close to zero. Build a system whose total momentum cancels exactly and see what the program does instead.

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,1 --positions_init "5e10,0,0;-5e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,25760,0;0,-25760,0" --max_steps 3000 --output_type trajectories

Two equal one-solar-mass bodies, \(1.0\times10^{11}\) m apart, with opposite tangential speeds of \(2.576\times10^4\) m/s: a near-circular two-body orbit. Look at the printed momentum and at the result dictionary's momentum_drift_normalization, which the console summary does not print directly.

python3 -c "
from driver_multiple import SimulationParams, run_simulation
p = SimulationParams(n_bodies=2, masses_solar=[1,1],
    positions_init=[[5e10,0,0],[-5e10,0,0]],
    velocities_init=[[0,25760,0],[0,-25760,0]],
    dt=2000., max_steps=3000, eps1=.005, eps2=1e-7,
    output_type='trajectories', animation_mode='trails',
    frame_time=2e5, frame_interval_ms=50, trail_time=6e5,
    projection='xy', axis_mode='fixed', display_frame='com')
r = run_simulation(p)
print(r['momentum_drift_normalization'], r['momentum_drift_scale'])
print(r['angular_momentum_drift_normalization'], r['angular_momentum_drift_scale'])
"

Three tasks, in order. First, the printed momentum: \(\mathbf P=(0,0,0)\) exactly, both at the start and at the end, because the two opposite momenta, each of magnitude \(2.576\times10^4\) m/s, cancel exactly by construction. Then the query above, which prints characteristic_scale 51520.0 for momentum and initial_norm 2576000000000000.0 for angular momentum. Momentum and angular momentum are treated differently here even though both started from the same run: \(\mathbf L=(0,0,2.576\times10^{15})\) m\(^2\)/s is manifestly nonzero, so its own norm is a perfectly good denominator, but \(|\mathbf P_0|=0\) cannot be divided by. Then compare 51520.0 with \(2\times(1)(25760)\): the characteristic momentum scale is exactly the sum of each body's own momentum magnitude, \(m_A|\mathbf v_A|\), even though those two contributions point in opposite directions and cancel in the vector sum.

Then why, and what changed in this release. A fraction with a zero or near-zero denominator is not a useful diagnostic, so _energy_drift_scale() in driver_multiple.py has long used a fallback for energy: if \(|E_0|\) is smaller than a tight tolerance times the characteristic scale \(K_0+|U_0|\) (which can never be zero for a bound, finite system), the program divides by that characteristic scale instead and labels the normalization 'characteristic_energy'. Model version 1.4.0 extends the same idea to momentum and angular momentum, which can cancel exactly — as they do here for momentum, by symmetric construction, not merely by coincidence:

Eq. 11 — Characteristic scale for a vector total ALGORITHM
\[ \sigma_{\mathbf P} = \sum_A m_A|\mathbf v_A| \;\ge\; |\mathbf P_0|, \qquad \sigma_{\mathbf L} = \sum_A m_A|\mathbf r_A\times\mathbf v_A| \;\ge\; |\mathbf L_0|, \] \[ \text{use } \sigma \text{ as the drift denominator when } |\mathbf P_0|\text{ or }|\mathbf L_0| \le \texttt{ENERGY\_CANCELLATION\_TOLERANCE}\times\sigma . \]

The triangle inequality guarantees \(\sigma_{\mathbf P}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathbf L}\) are always at least as large as the vector norm they bound, and strictly positive whenever any body is actually moving, so they make a stable, dimensionally correct denominator exactly when the ordinary norm cannot serve as one. scaled_characteristic_momentum() and scaled_characteristic_angular_momentum() in physics_multiple.py compute \(\sigma_{\mathbf P}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathbf L}\) once, from the initial state, and _vector_drift_metadata() in driver_multiple.py chooses between them and the plain initial norm at the start of the run, exactly the way the existing energy fallback already worked. The three possible values of momentum_drift_normalization and angular_momentum_drift_normalization are 'initial_norm' (used whenever the initial vector itself is not near-cancelling, as for angular momentum here and for momentum in every other beat on this page), 'characteristic_scale' (the new near-cancellation case, as for momentum here), and 'absolute_scaled', reserved for the rare case where every body is exactly still and there is no individual-body quantity to divide by at all. Before this release, this exact two-body configuration silently printed an unscaled absolute momentum drift with no fractional meaning; it now reports a proper fraction of a stable, nonzero scale.

Beat 5 · The predictor–corrector, body by body

Equation 2 has no closed-form solution for three or more bodies, so Multiple advances the system in small steps, the same predictor-then-corrector scheme Binary uses for two bodies, but with every body's acceleration and every body's convergence tested on its own. Run Beat 0's default calculation with the corrector tightened and loosened, and count how many times the force law, Eq. 2, actually gets evaluated.

python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --eps2 1e-10
python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --eps2 0.004

Multiple's console does not print a force-evaluation count, so this beat instruments compute_accelerations() with a call counter around run_simulation() for the same three eps2 values, the default \(10^{-7}\) plus these two, all with max_steps=8000.

Three tasks, in order. First, the counts: 25046 calls at the default \(\varepsilon_2=10^{-7}\) (3.13 per accepted step), 33046 at the stricter \(10^{-10}\) (4.13 per step), and 17046 at the loosest allowed value, \(0.004\) (2.13 per step, just under the required \(\varepsilon_2< \varepsilon_1=0.005\)). Tightening \(\varepsilon_2\) by three orders of magnitude raises the cost by about 30%; loosening it to the edge of what eps1 allows saves about a third. Then the result: all three runs report the identical \(3.2235\times10^{-4}\)/\(3.2235\times10^{-4}\)/ \(3.2339\times10^{-4}\) maximum energy drift and the identical 8000 accepted steps at the identical final time. Loosening the corrector by seven orders of magnitude changed the drift in the fourth significant figure. Then the minimum possible cost, 2.13 calls per step: one call for the acceleration at the start of the step, one for the predicted acceleration, and, when the very first corrector pass already satisfies eps2, no further call is needed, because the convergence test compares the corrector's own output to the predictor's, not to a fresh force evaluation.

Then the two stages that cost those calls. The predictor estimates the end of the step from the start-of-step acceleration alone, holding that acceleration constant over the step: a second-order (constant-acceleration) Taylor prediction for position, paired with a first-order (explicit Euler) prediction for velocity:

Eq. 12 — Constant-acceleration predictor ALGORITHM
\[ \tilde{\mathbf r}_A=\mathbf r_A^n+\mathbf v_A^n\Delta t+\tfrac12\mathbf a_A^n\Delta t^2, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf v}_A=\mathbf v_A^n+\mathbf a_A^n\Delta t . \]

The program evaluates Eq. 2 once more at the predicted positions of every body (the second call per step) and, if that passes the acceleration-change gate of Beat 6, refines the estimate with an iterated trapezoidal corrector that replaces the start-of-step acceleration by the average of the values at the two ends:

Eq. 13 — Iterated trapezoidal corrector ALGORITHM
\[ \mathbf v_A^{n+1}=\mathbf v_A^n+\tfrac12(\mathbf a_A^n+\mathbf a_A^{n+1})\Delta t, \qquad \mathbf r_A^{n+1}=\mathbf r_A^n+\tfrac12(\mathbf v_A^n+\mathbf v_A^{n+1})\Delta t . \]

The end acceleration \(\mathbf a_A^{n+1}\) depends on the end position, which Eq. 13 is computing, so the program compares the newly corrected velocity increment against the previous estimate's, for every body independently, and repeats until the worst body agrees to eps2 or ten passes are used up:

Eq. 14 — Corrector convergence test ALGORITHM
\[ \delta_A^{(k)} = \frac{\lVert\Delta\mathbf v_A^{(k)}-\Delta\mathbf v_A^{(k-1)}\rVert} {\max\!\bigl(\lVert\Delta\mathbf v_A^{(k)}\rVert,\lVert\Delta\mathbf v_A^{(k-1)}\rVert\bigr)}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf v_A=\mathbf v_A-\mathbf v_A^{n}, \qquad \text{accept when } \max_A \delta_A^{(k)} \lt \varepsilon_2 . \]

Comparing velocity increments, not raw coordinate velocities, is what makes this test unaffected by a uniform Galilean boost of the whole system (Beat 2): a boost changes every body's raw velocity but not the size of the correction any one pass makes to it. Testing every body's own \(\delta_A\) and requiring the worst of them to converge, rather than some system-wide average, means one violent pair cannot be masked by several quiet bodies elsewhere in the system — the same principle Beat 6 uses for the step-size gate. If ten passes are not enough, the working timestep is halved and the whole step, predictor included, is retried from the start-of-step state.

Beat 6 · The adaptive timestep, worst body first

Beat 0's default run never once mentioned a timestep problem, yet three equal-mass bodies starting fairly far apart cannot stay gentle forever. Look inside the same 8000-step default run at the actual step size used, accepted step by accepted step, not just its final report.

python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories

The console prints only the final Maximum conservation drift line; this beat reads the run's dt_used array directly, the per-step timestep that run_simulation() records for every accepted state when output_type='trajectories'.

Three tasks, in order. First, the first 425 accepted steps all use the full \(\Delta t=2000\) s ceiling: nothing shortens the step while the three bodies are still comparatively far apart. At step 426 the working step drops to 1000 s for the first time, then grows back by 10% a step — 1100, 1210, 1331, 1464.1, 1610.51, 1771.561 — until the next close approach cuts it again. Then the extreme: by step 5247 the working step has collapsed to 0.2285 s, nearly ten thousand times shorter than the 2000 s ceiling, as two of the three bodies pass unusually close to one another. Then the aftermath: the step never fully recovers to 2000 s again before the run ends at step 8000. From about step 3200 onward it oscillates in a narrow band of roughly 20 to 110 s, repeatedly cut and regrown, consistent with a tight binary having formed between two of the bodies (the classic Help's "Binary hardening" scenario is this same encounter) whose own short orbital period keeps demanding short steps for as long as the run continues. Despite all of this the run's maximum fractional energy drift was still only \(3.2235\times10^{-4}\) (Beat 0): the controller is doing exactly what it is meant to, buying the accuracy that the encounter needs without being asked.

Then the rule that produces that behavior. Before the corrector runs at all, the program compares one trial body's Euler-predicted acceleration with its acceleration at the start of the step:

Eq. 15 — Per-body acceleration-change gate and step recovery ALGORITHM
\[ \delta_{a,A} = \frac{\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}_A-\mathbf a_A\rVert} {\max(\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}_A\rVert,\lVert\mathbf a_A\rVert)}, \qquad \text{if } \max_A\delta_{a,A} \gt \varepsilon_1:\ \Delta t\leftarrow\tfrac12\Delta t, \qquad \text{else, after acceptance: } \Delta t\leftarrow\min(1.1\,\Delta t,\ \texttt{dt}). \]

Every one of the three bodies is tested, and the single worst \(\delta_{a,A}\) decides whether the step is rejected, exactly as Eq. 14's corrector test uses the worst body's \(\delta_A\). A body sitting far from any encounter contributes a small, harmless \(\delta_{a,A}\); it is the body (or pair) actually undergoing the close approach that drives the maximum over \(\Delta t\) toward the floor. A rejected step is retried at half the timestep with no other bookkeeping; an accepted one lets the working step grow by exactly 10% toward the user ceiling dt, so a single violent encounter forces short steps only where, and only as long as, it is actually needed, while the rest of the run (or the rest of a many-body system in which some bodies stay quiet) is free to run at the full ceiling. Beat 1's unequal-mass run reaches a minimum step of only 7.4 s over its shorter 2000-step span, far short of Beat 0's 0.2285 s, because it ends before the deep encounter Beat 0 eventually reaches (Beat 8 returns to this run for exactly that reason).

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,1 --positions_init "5e10,0,0;-5e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,25760,0;0,-25760,0" --max_steps 3000 --output_type animation --frame_time 2e5

Look at the Animation frames line the console prints just before the plot window opens.

Three tasks, in order. First, with --max_steps 600 in place of 3000 (so the run finishes quickly for this check), the console prints Animation frames: 7; frame spacing: 2.0000e+05 simulated s; approximate playback time: 3.5000e-01 real s: seven frames at \(t=0,\,2\times10^5,\,4\times10^5,\dots\) s, each 50 ms apart in real playback time by default, for 0.35 s of total real playback. (The full 3000-step run above has ten times the accepted steps and gives 31 frames, 1.55 s of playback; the count scales with how much simulated time the run covers, not with anything about interpolation.) Then compare a stored accepted step's time with a requested frame time directly: in the 3000-step run, the accepted step at \(t=198000\) s is immediately followed by one at \(t=200000\) s, a full \(\Delta t=2000\) s apart, and the requested frame at \(t=2\times10^5\) s falls exactly on the later of those two, so no interpolation is actually needed there. Then pick a frame time that does not land on an accepted state, \(t=199000\) s, exactly halfway between the same two bracketing steps, and compare the program's cubic-Hermite estimate of body 0's position with a naive straight-line (linear) interpolation between the same two bracketing states: the two estimates are \((4.973745\times10^{10},\,5.117264\times10^9,0)\) and \((4.973745\times10^{10},\,5.117263\times10^9,0)\) m, differing by about 6636 m out of a \(10^{11}\)-m orbit — small over one 2000-s step at this gentle a speed, but systematic. This discrepancy is not a property of frame_time itself; it depends only on how far apart the two bracketing accepted states are in time and how much the true path curves between them (a longer accepted step, or a tighter orbit, both make it larger). Choosing a different frame_time only moves which requested sample times fall where between the same bracketing states — it can even land exactly on an accepted state, as the \(t=2\times10^5\) s frame above does, making the discrepancy zero for that frame — it never changes the accepted steps the integrator actually took (Beat 6).

Then why a curve, not a straight line, is used to bridge two accepted states. The program stores both the position and the velocity at every accepted step, so it can match not just the endpoints but the slope at each endpoint too, using the standard cubic-Hermite basis:

Eq. 16 — Cubic-Hermite frame interpolation ALGORITHM
\[ s=\frac{t-t_0}{t_1-t_0},\qquad \mathbf r(t) = h_{00}(s)\,\mathbf r_0 + h_{10}(s)\,(t_1{-}t_0)\,\mathbf v_0 + h_{01}(s)\,\mathbf r_1 + h_{11}(s)\,(t_1{-}t_0)\,\mathbf v_1, \] \[ h_{00}=2s^3{-}3s^2{+}1,\quad h_{10}=s^3{-}2s^2{+}s,\quad h_{01}=-2s^3{+}3s^2,\quad h_{11}=s^3{-}s^2, \]

with the velocity at the requested time given by the corresponding derivative combination, so that \(\mathbf r(t_0)=\mathbf r_0\), \(\mathbf r(t_1)=\mathbf r_1\), and the interpolated velocity matches \(\mathbf v_0\) and \(\mathbf v_1\) at the two ends exactly. This is display sampling only; it never feeds back into the integration, which always advances by the adaptive dt_work of Beats 5 and 6 regardless of what output_type or frame_time were requested. _hermite_state() in driver_multiple.py implements Eq. 16, and run_simulation() calls it once for every requested frame time that a newly accepted step's bracket newly covers — one accepted step can cross several requested frame times at once if the step is long relative to frame_time. Because 'animation' stores only these uniformly spaced frames plus the accepted-step conservation samples of Beat 3, not every accepted state, it can use far less memory than 'trajectories' for a long run; a guard rejects any request whose maximum possible duration would need more than 1,000,000 stored frames, asking for a larger frame_time instead.

Beat 8 · How far to trust it

Every number in every beat so far came from an approximation. With two bodies on a fixed Keplerian orbit, Binary can check the integration against an exact formula. Multiple has no such formula once three or more bodies interact, so the only way to judge a number is to change the numerical controls and see whether the number changes. Do that with the classic Help's own "Numerical convergence" scenario, then push a genuinely singular configuration until it breaks.

python main.py --dt 1000 --max_steps 4000 --output_type trajectories --show_energy_diagnostic
python main.py --dt 500 --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --show_energy_diagnostic

Look at Maximum conservation drift's energy figure in both runs.

Three tasks, in order. First, the smaller-dt run: \(\Delta t=1000\) s, \(\varepsilon_1\) and \(\varepsilon_2\) at their defaults, reaches 4000 accepted steps and day 17.559. The even smaller step, \(\Delta t=500\) s, 8000 steps, reaches day 17.608. Their reported Maximum conservation drift energy figures are \(1.7950\times10^{-5}\) and \(3.2449\times10^{-4}\) — more than eighteen times larger for the finer step, which looks like the opposite of ordinary convergence. Before concluding anything from that, notice what it actually compares: the two runs do not reach the same simulated time, so the two maxima are each the largest drift somewhere in a different stretch of the trajectory, not the same stretch measured twice.

Then compare them properly, over a stretch both runs actually share. Restricted to the first 17.5 days — before either run's reported maximum — the two integrations agree closely: \(7.0649\times10^{-6}\) for \(\Delta t=1000\) against \(7.1182\times10^{-6}\) for \(\Delta t=500\), both at essentially the same moment, day 14.851. That is ordinary, well-behaved convergence, and the whole-run comparison above hides it. The two runs land at different final days (17.559 against 17.608) despite spending the same nominal step budget (\(4000\times1000\,\mathrm{s}=8000\times500\,\mathrm{s}=4\times10^{6}\) s) because, once the adaptive controller starts shrinking the step for a close encounter (Beat 6), it is largely the number of accepted steps still available, not the ceiling dt, that decides how far a run can push into that encounter; the run with twice as many steps in its budget simply goes further before running out.

Then look at what each run's own maximum actually is. The \(\Delta t=500\) run's \(3.2449\times10^{-4}\) occurs at day 17.59335, during a sharp, brief close pass where the two nearest bodies come within \(1.6394\times10^{8}\) m of each other — far closer than anything in the common stretch above. The \(\Delta t=1000\) run never reaches that pass; it simply runs out of steps at day 17.559, shortly before it. Extended by 2000 more accepted steps (--max_steps 6000, no other change), the \(\Delta t=1000\) run reaches day 17.595 and reports a maximum drift of \(3.2472\times10^{-4}\) at day 17.59336 — matching the \(\Delta t=500\) run's own \(3.2449\times10^{-4}\) at day 17.59335 to within two parts in a thousand. The two step sizes agree well at the same point in the trajectory; the eighteen-fold gap in the original whole-run comparison was entirely a consequence of comparing two different stretches of the run, not evidence that halving dt made the integration worse.

The lesson is not that finer settings are useless — away from a close encounter, Beat 5's \(\varepsilon_2\) comparison and Binary's Beat 7 both show the expected, orderly improvement — but that a single Maximum conservation drift figure from two runs is only comparable when both runs cover the same stretch of simulated time. A run that happens to reach further into, or through, a close encounter will usually report a larger maximum than one that stops just short of it, whatever the step size; that says where the encounter is, not which run is more accurate. To judge convergence near a close encounter, compare over a specified common time (as above), extend the shorter run to the same endpoint, or check with a tighter third calculation, as this beat just did — not the printed summary line alone. Three or more bodies can still separate onto genuinely different trajectories after enough close encounters (the warning at the top of this page), but establishing that takes exactly this kind of matched comparison, never a mismatched one. At the far end of this scale sits an exact singularity. Two \(10^{11}\,M_\odot\) point masses, 50 kpc apart, launched directly at one another with zero impact parameter (the classic Help's "Galaxy collision" scenario) head for \(r=0\), where Eq. 1 is infinite:

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1e11,1e11 --positions_init "-7.7e20,0,0;7.7e20,0,0" --velocities_init "50000,0,0;-50000,0,0" --dt 1e12 --max_steps 17500 --output_type trajectories
python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1e11,1e11 --positions_init "-7.7e20,0,0;7.7e20,0,0" --velocities_init "50000,0,0;-50000,0,0" --dt 1e12 --max_steps 17800 --output_type trajectories

The first command, max_steps=17500, completes normally. The second, max_steps=17800, only 300 steps further into the same head-on approach, stops with Multiple error: The adaptive timestep became too small to advance simulation time in floating-point arithmetic. Beat 6's halving rule (Eq. 15) keeps trying smaller and smaller steps as the bodies approach exact coincidence, and eventually the working timestep underflows to a value that, added to the current simulated time in floating-point arithmetic, changes it not at all. Rather than loop forever or silently report a frozen or corrupted state, the program raises a clear error and stops. This is the model's own limit, not a bug: two point masses with no softening length genuinely do experience unbounded force at exact coincidence, and no finite step size can integrate through that. A physically softer model, or a nonzero impact parameter, would let the calculation continue past the encounter instead (Experiment 10 asks for exactly that). Knowing where a calculation stops being trustworthy, and where it stops running at all, are two forms of the same discipline that runs through every beat on this page.

Algorithm

This section collects in one place the rules of the numerical method that Beats 5 to 7 introduced one at a time.

One accepted step

  1. Evaluate every body's acceleration at the start of the step from Eq. 2 (equivalently, the pair-loop of Eq. 3).
  2. Take a trial step of the working length \(\Delta t\) with the constant-acceleration predictor, Eq. 12, and evaluate the accelerations at the predicted positions.
  3. Form \(\delta_{a,A}\) from Eq. 15 for every body. If the worst exceeds eps1, halve \(\Delta t\) and return to step 2.
  4. Apply the trapezoidal corrector, Eq. 13, and repeat it, re-evaluating the end-of-step acceleration each time, until every body's \(\delta_A\) of Eq. 14 is below eps2. If ten passes do not suffice, halve \(\Delta t\) and return to step 2.
  5. Accept the step. Record the new state; in 'trajectories' mode append it directly, and in 'animation' mode interpolate any newly reachable frame times with Eq. 16. Update the running maximum drift for energy, momentum and angular momentum using the scale chosen once at the start of the run (Beat 4). Let the working step grow by 10%, capped at the user-specified dt, and begin the next step.

Predictor–Corrector

The end-of-step acceleration used inside Eq. 13 is recomputed from the refined positions and the corrector is iterated, up to ten passes, with convergence judged from the dimensionless relative change of each body's own velocity increment (Eq. 14), never from raw coordinate components. If ten iterations are insufficient, the timestep is halved and the same step retried, never silently accepted unconverged.

Adaptive Timestep

Before the corrector runs at all, the program compares every body's acceleration at the start of the step with its Euler-predicted end-of-step acceleration (Eq. 15). Testing every body independently, and rejecting the step whenever the single worst body's fractional change exceeds eps1, means one body's close encounter cannot be diluted by many quiet bodies elsewhere in the system (Beat 6). After an accepted step the working timestep may grow by 10% toward the ceiling dt; a rejected step is retried at half its previous length. If repeated halving drives the added simulated time below what floating-point arithmetic can represent as a change, the program raises a clear error rather than looping or silently corrupting the state (Beat 8).

Output Modes

'trajectories' stores every accepted position, velocity, and per-step conservation value; nothing is discarded. 'animation' instead stores frames at uniformly spaced physical times, obtained from Eq. 16 applied to the bracketing accepted states, plus the eleven console conservation samples of Beat 3, independent of how many accepted steps the adaptive controller actually used. A guard limits any single animation request to 1,000,000 stored frames.

  • Initial and Final user-frame totals give \(E\), the boost-independent \(E_{\rm internal}\) of Eq. 6, \(\mathbf P\), and \(\mathbf L\), each already scaled by one solar mass (Beat 3).
  • Maximum conservation drift reports the largest fractional (or, when Beat 4's near-cancellation criterion applies, characteristic-scaled) departure of energy, momentum, and angular momentum from their initial values over every accepted step, not just the two endpoints.
  • The eleven-row table linearly indexes ten equal time intervals from the run's start to its end (not ten equal step counts) and locally quadratically interpolates \(E\), \(E_{\rm internal}\), \(K\), \(\mathbf P\), and \(\mathbf L\) from the three nearest accepted states (Beat 3); a one-step run uses two-point interpolation and the two endpoints are always taken exactly.

The result dictionary that run_simulation() returns carries additional fields the console does not print directly, including energy_drift_normalization, momentum_drift_normalization, angular_momentum_drift_normalization (Beat 4: one of 'initial_norm', 'characteristic_scale', or 'absolute_scaled') and their matching *_drift_scale values. Older keys beginning max_fractional_ remain as aliases for max_momentum_drift and max_angular_momentum_drift but should not be used to infer the normalization.

Suggested Experiments

Run every command from the Multiple program directory. Quoted semicolon-separated triples are single shell arguments. Each card names the beat it extends and a difficulty rating.

Experiment 1 — Introductory
Two-Body Sanity Check
python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1,1 --positions_init "5e10,0,0;-5e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,25760,0;0,-25760,0" --max_steps 3000 --output_type trajectories

Before exploring chaotic three-body motion, test a familiar binary. Two equal one-solar-mass bodies \(1.0\times10^{11}\) m apart, with opposite tangential speeds of about \(2.576\times10^4\) m/s, follow nearly circular paths about their shared center of mass. Their two opposite momenta cancel exactly, so this is Beat 4's near-cancellation example.

Check: the printed momentum is exactly \((0,0,0)\) at both ends of the run, and querying the result dictionary gives momentum_drift_normalization = 'characteristic_scale' with a characteristic scale of 51520.0 m/s, rather than the pre-1.4.0 unscaled absolute drift.

Extends Beat 4.
Experiment 2 — Introductory
Default Three-Body Encounter
python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories

Run the planar shipped example. Record which two bodies make the closest sustained pair, whether the third makes a wide excursion, and the reported conservation drift. Extend max_steps if you want to watch later encounters, but do not expect a short run to reveal a unique final fate.

Check: 8000 accepted steps cover 18.094 days; the maximum reported drift is energy \(3.2235\times10^{-4}\), momentum \(6.8454\times10^{-14}\), and angular momentum \(3.7074\times10^{-6}\) (Beats 0 and 3).

Extends Beats 0 and 3.
Experiment 3 — Introductory
Change the Masses
python main.py --masses_solar 2,1,0.5 --max_steps 2000 --output_type trajectories

Keep the default positions and velocities but reassign the three masses. Does that run produce a bound pair, and which body is left on the widest orbit? Compare that single outcome with one other mass assignment rather than treating one deterministic trajectory as a frequency.

Check: the initial totals are \(E=-5.2701\times10^9\), \(\mathbf P=(-1.5\times10^4,-3.0\times10^4,0)\) m/s, matching Beat 1's own run and its hand-checked initial accelerations.

Experiment 4 — Intermediate
Out-of-Plane Encounter
python main.py --velocities_init "0,0,0;0,-30000,0;-30000,0,5000" --projection xz --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories

Add a modest \(z\)-velocity to one body and compare the 'xy' and 'xz' projections; the calculation itself is always fully three-dimensional (Beat 0), and choosing a projection only changes what is plotted, the same idea as Beat 2's display frame acting only on the plot.

Check: the run reaches 8000 accepted steps at 18.876 days; the initial angular momentum \(\mathbf L=(2.3\times10^{14},0,2.76\times10^{15})\) m\(^2\)/s now has a nonzero \(x\) component that the default in-plane run (Beat 0) does not, and the maximum reported drift is energy \(1.7662\times10^{-4}\), angular momentum \(3.6488\times10^{-6}\).

Experiment 5 — Intermediate
Numerical Convergence
python main.py --dt 1000 --max_steps 4000 --output_type trajectories --show_energy_diagnostic
python main.py --dt 500 --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --show_energy_diagnostic

Repeat the same physical initial conditions with dt, dt/2, and optionally dt/4, then repeat with tighter eps1 and eps2. Compare the reported conservation drift only over a stretch of simulated time that every run actually reaches; near a close encounter (Beat 6) a finer step's run often stops at a different final time than a coarser one, so their whole-run maxima can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with which run is more accurate.

Check: \(\Delta t=1000\) s (4000 steps) reaches day 17.559 with a maximum energy drift of \(1.7950\times10^{-5}\); \(\Delta t=500\) s (8000 steps) reaches day 17.608 with \(3.2449\times10^{-4}\). Restricted to the first 17.5 days both runs share, the two agree closely (\(7.0649\times10^{-6}\) against \(7.1182\times10^{-6}\)); the larger whole-run figure for \(\Delta t=500\) comes from a sharp close pass near day 17.593 that the shorter \(\Delta t=1000\) run has not yet reached (Beat 8).

Extends Beats 5 and 8.
Experiment 6 — Intermediate
Center-of-Mass Frame
python main.py --velocities_init "100000,0,0;100000,-30000,0;70000,0,0" --max_steps 2000 --output_type trajectories --display_frame user
python main.py --velocities_init "100000,0,0;100000,-30000,0;70000,0,0" --max_steps 2000 --output_type trajectories --display_frame com

Add the same extra velocity \(\mathbf u=(1.0\times10^5,0,0)\) m/s to every body. Set --display_frame user and watch the encounter drift across the plot; switch to --display_frame com (or press F during an animation) and confirm the relative motion is unchanged while the system stays centered.

Extends Beat 2.
Experiment 7 — Intermediate
Binary Hardening
python main.py --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories --display_frame com

In this same default run, track all three pair separations to identify which two bodies form a persistent close pair, rather than relying only on the plot. Compare the third body's distance from that pair's center of mass before and after later encounters, and watch the adaptive step size respond.

Check: the working timestep first drops below its 2000-s ceiling at accepted step 426, reaches a minimum of 0.2285 s at step 5247, and never fully recovers to the ceiling again before the run ends at step 8000 (Beat 6) — a numerical signature of a tightening, short-period pair.

Experiment 8 — Advanced
Small Star Cluster
python main.py --n_bodies 4 --masses_solar 1,1,1,1 --positions_init "0,0,0;1.5e14,0,0;0,1.5e14,0;-1.5e14,0,0" --velocities_init "0,0,0;0,5000,0;-5000,0,0;0,-5000,0" --dt 1e7 --max_steps 8000 --output_type trajectories

A 1000-AU box (\(1.5\times10^{14}\) m \(\approx1003\) AU) of four equal solar-mass stars moving at up to 5 km/s has a crossing time of order \(3\times10^{10}\) s (roughly 950 years). Leave --display_frame com. Compare the printed \(E\) and \(E_{\rm internal}\), the expansion of the cluster, and any ejections; then try adding one more massive body. These are point-mass experiments, not collisional star-cluster models.

Check: the run reaches 8000 accepted steps and 925,930 days (about 2535 years, several crossing times), with maximum reported drift energy \(1.4993\times10^{-9}\), momentum \(1.4082\times10^{-14}\), angular momentum \(1.5323\times10^{-9}\) — far smaller than Beat 0's default encounter, because this configuration never brings two bodies as close together.

Extends Beats 0 and 3.
Experiment 9 — Advanced
Compare with MercPert
python main.py --n_bodies 3 --masses_solar 1,0.1,1e-8 --positions_init "0,0,0;1e11,0,0;4e10,0,0" --velocities_init "0,-3450,0;0,34500,0;0,57500,0" --max_steps 4000 --output_type trajectories

Experiment 10 — Advanced
Galaxy Collision — Optional Toy Model, and Past the Trust Limit
python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1e11,1e11 --positions_init "-7.7e20,0,0;7.7e20,0,0" --velocities_init "50000,0,0;-50000,0,0" --dt 1e12 --max_steps 10000 --output_type trajectories

Two \(10^{11}\,M_\odot\) cores 50 kpc apart approaching at 100 km/s would take order \(5\times10^8\) yr to cross that separation at constant speed, but their mutual gravity accelerates the infall, so this exactly radial pair reaches closest approach sooner, near \(2.4\times10^8\) yr. Place a few companion particles about 10 kpc from each core; at that radius a \(10^{11}\,M_\odot\) point mass has a circular speed of about 207 km/s and an escape speed of about 293 km/s. First give the companions negligible mass so the cores pass through; then raise them toward \(10^{10}\,M_\odot\). This is a handful of point masses, not a collisionless galaxy or a merger with gas, friction, or radiation.

Then push the same head-on pair, with no companions and no softening, past where the program can still integrate it:

python main.py --n_bodies 2 --masses_solar 1e11,1e11 --positions_init "-7.7e20,0,0;7.7e20,0,0" --velocities_init "50000,0,0;-50000,0,0" --dt 1e12 --max_steps 17800 --output_type trajectories

Check: --max_steps 10000 and 17500 both complete normally; --max_steps 17800, only 300 steps further into the same approach, stops with Multiple error: The adaptive timestep became too small to advance simulation time in floating-point arithmetic., the exact-coincidence singularity of Beat 8.

Extends Beat 8.

Restoring Default Parameter Values

A new run with python main.py uses all default settings; options in a previous command do not carry over. If you change a program file for an exercise, restore it using any of the following methods:

  • Same session: press Ctrl+Z (Windows) or Cmd+Z (macOS) repeatedly in your editor to undo your edits.
  • If you are using PyCharm: right-click the edited file in the Project panel → Local History → Show History → select a version from before your changes → click Revert.
  • Full reset: re-download the ZIP from github.com/tomyee3-org/GFTGU-Programs and replace this program's folder with the fresh copy.

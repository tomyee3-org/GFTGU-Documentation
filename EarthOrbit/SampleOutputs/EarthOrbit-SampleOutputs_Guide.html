EarthOrbit

Representative Sample Outputs

Version 1.4.0  |  Build 08cdcf30c04d  |  Updated 23 September 2026

Purpose. One figure for each Beat of the EarthOrbit Help, in Beat order, for an instructor reading alongside it. Each section gives the exact command, the plot it produces, and the values the program prints. Beat 9 has no figure: its commands stop with a one-line error before any plot is drawn. No external image files are required.

In every figure, the dashed blue curve is the reference Earth surface and the red curve is the projectile trajectory. Coordinates are geocentric metres and both axes use the same scale. The launch point is at the top of Earth; uInit is initially horizontal and vInit is initially radial.

Figure 1 — Beat 0 · Newton's cannon, launched

python main.py

The default 7.9 km/s launch from 300 m under the simplified constant-g law travels about 14° around Earth before its path meets the surface.

What it shows

  • A short red arc leaves the top of the dashed Earth circle and rejoins it; at this scale the 300 m launch height is not visible.
  • The defaults are the simplified force law, h0 = 300 m, uInit = 7900 m/s and dt = 0.4 s.
  • The printed time and distance are interpolated to the surface crossing rather than read from the last stored sample.

Headline values

  • EarthOrbit 1.4.0 (build 08cdcf30c04d) — 503 trajectory samples
  • Outcome: Surface impact before one revolution
  • Fraction of a revolution: 0.039512; Total angular travel: 14.224 deg
  • Total flight time: 200.44 s
  • Total distance traveled (interpolated to impact): 1.5835e+06 m

Figure 2 — Beat 1 · Two ways to point at the centre

python main.py --force_law inverse_square

The same launch under the inverse-square law flies a little farther before impact than the simplified-law run of Figure 1.

What it shows

  • Near the surface the two force laws almost agree, so the arc looks nearly the same as Figure 1; the difference shows up in the printed numbers.
  • Inverse-square runs add osculating elements, computed from the launch state as an ideal two-body orbit.
  • The negative osculating perigee altitude means the ideal orbit passes below the surface, consistent with the impact.

Headline values

  • Fraction of a revolution: 0.043687 and Total flight time: 221.62 s (Figure 1: 0.039512 and 200.44 s)
  • Total angular travel: 15.727 deg
  • Osculating eccentricity: 0.0013002
  • Osculating perigee altitude: -16265 m; Osculating apogee altitude: 300.00 m

Figure 3 — Beat 2 · The speed that never lands

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 7000

A launch at 7725.7 m/s from 300 km, very close to the local circular speed, completes a full orbit.

What it shows

  • A red loop encircles Earth just outside the surface; the 7000-sample run goes past one revolution, so the start of the loop is overlapped.
  • The ideal orbit is almost exactly circular, with an eccentricity of a few millionths.
  • The computed maximum altitude is well above the 300 km ideal apogee: at dt = 1 s the fixed-step integrator lets the orbit drift outward.

Headline values

  • Completed revolutions: 1; Total angular travel: 460.86 deg
  • Revolution 1: time 5460.9 s; minimum altitude 3.0000e+05 m; maximum altitude 3.4847e+05 m
  • Osculating eccentricity: 6.1431e-06
  • Osculating perigee altitude: 2.9992e+05 m; Osculating apogee altitude: 3.0000e+05 m

Figure 5 — Beat 4 · Leaving for good

An 11.5 km/s launch from 300 km is above the inverse-square escape speed and follows an open path away from Earth.

Figure 6 — Beat 5 · Why the simplified law can't close an ellipse

Under the simplified law, a launch about 2.9% below that law's circular speed at 300 km does not retrace its path: each revolution traces a different loop.

  • Each revolution dips far below 300 km and climbs back; the second low point is higher than the first.
  • Beat 5's other two commands launch at 7500 m/s under each law; both impact before one revolution, the simplified run sooner.

Figure 8 — Beat 7 · How far to trust a printed number

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 4 --maxSteps 1450

At dt = 4 s an ideally circular 300 km orbit spirals visibly outward within one revolution.

What it shows

  • The red path does not rejoin its start: at the top of the figure the end of the loop sits well outside the beginning.
  • The launch state is an exactly circular ideal orbit, so all of the outward drift is numerical.
  • Beat 7's other four commands halve dt each time; the maximum altitude and revolution time settle toward the ideal values.

  • Osculating eccentricity: 0.0000
  • dt = 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25 s: Revolution 1 maximum altitude 3.9682e+05, 3.4847e+05, 3.2425e+05, 3.1213e+05 m
  • dt = 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25 s: Revolution 1 time 5490.8, 5460.9, 5446.1, 5438.7 s

Figure 9 — Beat 8 · Kepler's third law, and its absence

python main.py --h0 1500000 --uInit 7113.0422 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 7009

A near-circular inverse-square orbit at 1500 km altitude, one of the four circular runs Beat 8 compares.

What it shows

  • One red near-circular loop well outside Earth; its period is longer than that of the 300 km orbit.
  • Beat 8's other three commands give circular periods at 300 km under the inverse-square law and at both altitudes under the simplified law.
  • The simplified-law period grows much less between the two altitudes than the inverse-square period does.

Headline values

  • Revolution 1: time 6988.7 s; minimum altitude 1.5000e+06 m; maximum altitude 1.5446e+06 m
  • Osculating eccentricity: 1.4901e-08
  • 300 km, inverse_square (--uInit 7725.7237): Revolution 1 time 5460.9 s
  • simplified at 300 km and 1500 km: Revolution 1 time 5189.8 s and 5636.6 s

Notes for evaluation

  • Two models: simplified keeps the acceleration magnitude at 9.8 m/s²; inverse_square uses MU_EARTH = 3.98600435507×10¹⁴ m³/s². Only inverse_square runs print osculating elements.
  • Osculating elements describe the ideal two-body orbit implied by the launch state. The per-revolution altitudes describe the trajectory actually computed, which drifts outward at coarse dt (Figures 3, 4 and 8).
  • Integrator: fixed-step, first-order, not symplectic. Halving dt roughly halves the drift.
  • Precision: every printed number has five significant digits, which does not mean the model or method is that accurate.
  • Stopping with an error: Beat 9's four commands print one-line EarthOrbit: ... messages and draw no plot. A step whose straight-line chord passes through Earth also stops the run with an error, for example at --h0 300000 --uInit 7629.44 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 6001.

Image and source provenance

All nine figures were regenerated for this guide from EarthOrbit version 1.4.0, build 08cdcf30c04d, on 23 September 2026; no earlier images were reused. Each was produced by running the command shown with matplotlib's non-interactive Agg backend and saving the figure that plot_earth_orbit would otherwise display (800 × 800 pixels at 100 dpi), then converting it to a 256-colour indexed PNG. The headline values are the console output of the same runs. The nine embedded PNG files total 192.5 KiB.

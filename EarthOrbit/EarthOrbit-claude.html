Description

EarthOrbit is a numerical realisation of Newton's cannon, the thought experiment Newton used in the Principia (1687) to argue that falling and orbiting are the same motion. A projectile is launched horizontally from a tower above Earth's surface. Launched with too large a shortfall from the local circular speed, it falls back to the ground on a curve; launched fast enough, it falls at exactly the rate the ground curves away beneath it, tracing a path that never lands. Exactly how large a shortfall counts as "too large" depends on how high above the surface the tower stands — Experiment 6 works this out precisely, including how the answer differs from the program's own numerically integrated outcome. The program integrates the projectile's two-dimensional trajectory step by step and reports which of these outcomes a given launch produces: impact, a closed orbit, or escape.

Two gravity models are available. The default, simplified, is the near-surface teaching approximation used for this exercise: the acceleration has the fixed magnitude \(g=9.8\) m s−2 and always points toward Earth's centre. The optional inverse_square model instead uses the real \(1/r^2\) law, with the modern JPL DE440 value of Earth's gravitational parameter. Both force laws admit an exact circular orbit at the matching launch speed, but inverse_square is needed for the Keplerian ellipse and escape behaviour that makes Newton's cannon interesting beyond that one case. Under inverse_square every ideal bound orbit that misses the ground closes on itself and repeats; under simplified a non-circular orbit generally drifts into a precessing rosette instead, with exact circular orbits and a few special resonant shapes as the exceptions (see the spine sentence below and Beat 5). Neither statement is a promise about the numerical path, which drifts slightly even for an ideal closed orbit (Beat 7). All six run settings can be supplied at the command line; use python main.py --help to see their descriptions and defaults.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: launch speed relative to the local circular speed decides whether the launch point becomes the near side or the far side of an orbit, and the inverse-square law is the one that makes every such bound orbit close on itself and repeat. Below circular, the launch point is the orbit's apogee and the projectile dips toward a lower perigee it has not yet reached; above circular, the launch point is the perigee and the projectile rises toward a higher apogee. Whether a sub-circular launch actually strikes the ground depends on how far that lower perigee sits below Earth's surface — close to the surface, where this program's default launch sits, even a tiny deficit sends the perigee underground almost at once; far from the surface, a sizeable deficit can still leave the perigee comfortably above ground, so the projectile completes an orbit instead of impacting (Experiment 6 maps exactly where that boundary sits). Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument, a command whose printed summary illustrates it, and then the equation the summary follows from.

Textbook reference: Bernard Schutz, Gravity from the Ground Up, Cambridge University Press (2003), Chapter 4 — Investigations 4.1 and 4.2.
What this program is, and what it does not model. EarthOrbit is a teaching model of a point projectile over an airless, non-rotating, spherical Earth. It has no atmosphere or drag, no wind, no oblateness or third-body forces, and no station-keeping; its one fixed-step integrator is not symplectic, so it does not conserve energy exactly even for an orbit that should be perfectly circular. The simplified force law is a genuine physical approximation, not the real law of gravity, and it does not share the inverse-square law's special property that every bound orbit closes into a repeating ellipse. Use the program to see how launch speed decides an outcome and to learn where a fixed-step calculation can and cannot be trusted; Beats 5 and 7 say exactly where it cannot.

How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equations at work, not independent evidence that they are right. The outside check this page uses is the exact two-body formulas of Beats 2 through 4 and 8, which the inverse_square runs can be compared against by hand.

Every beat uses the same program. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Beat 0 runs the default launch, Beat 1 compares the two force laws side by side, Beats 2 through 4 walk the circular/elliptical/escape sequence under inverse_square, Beat 5 shows what the simplified law does instead, Beat 6 looks at how the printed numbers are estimated between stored samples, Beat 7 asks how far a printed number can be trusted, Beat 8 checks Kepler's third law, and Beat 9 collects the limits of the model and of a run.

Run every command from the program's folder. Each run prints a summary and then opens a plot window; close the window to end the program. Each beat names the printed lines to look at. In several beats the printed numbers are what to study and the plot can be left until later. Altitudes and radii are in metres, speeds in metres per second, angles in degrees, and printed real numbers have five significant figures.

What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag saying what kind of statement it is. A green ODE tag marks a differential equation that the program integrates; Eq. 1 is the equation of motion itself, and Eq. 3 is the physical force law that is the honest answer to "what really makes the projectile accelerate." A grey PRESCRIPTION tag marks a rule the program adopts by choice rather than derives; Eq. 2, the constant-magnitude force, is the clearest example, since it is close to gravity only near the surface and is not Newton's actual inverse-square law. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows algebraically from equations already stated. The numerical method itself — the averaged-velocity step of Eq. 7 and the interpolation formulas of Eqs. 8 and 9, all under Algorithm — is an algorithm rather than physics and is not tagged. Ten equations are numbered; a few unnumbered ones are derived inside the beats where they are needed.

Beat 0 · Newton's cannon, launched

Launch a projectile 300 m over Newton's mountain at 7900 m/s, sideways, with the default near-surface gravity model. Ask the program what happens.

python main.py

Look at the four printed lines below the sample count, and at the plot: a blue dashed circle for Earth's surface and a red trajectory line.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the numbers: 503 trajectory samples, and an outcome of Surface impact before one revolution, after only 0.039512 of a full turn — 14.224° of arc — in a flight of 200.44 s, covering 1.5835e+06 m. The projectile does not get far. Then compare that launch speed with the speed that would hold a circular path at this altitude under this same force law, \(v_c=\sqrt{gr}\) with \(r=R_\oplus+300\) m (Beat 1 derives this). It comes to 7906.3 m/s. The default launch, 7900 m/s, is 99.92% of that value — a deficit of about 0.08%, one part in roughly 1250. Write down what you expect a 0.08% speed deficit to do to a nearly circular path before reading on: does 14° of arc before impact strike you as a lot, or a little, for such a small shortfall? Then look at the plot: rather than a broad, shallow near-miss, the trajectory curves sharply back to the ground well within the first quarter-turn. Beat 5 explains why a small speed deficit does not buy nearly as much margin here as it would under real gravity.

Then the equations behind the run. Let \((x,y)\) be the projectile's position with the origin at Earth's centre, and \((u,v)\) its velocity. Newton's second law gives four coupled first-order ODEs:

Eq. 1 — Equations of motion ODE
\[ \dot{x}=u,\qquad \dot{y}=v,\qquad \dot{u}=a_x,\qquad \dot{v}=a_y,\qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. \]

The launch point is \((x_0,y_0)=(0,\,R_\oplus+h_0)\) with velocity \((u_0,v_0)=(\texttt{uInit}, \texttt{vInit})\); by default \(v_0=0\), a purely horizontal (tangential) launch. What \((a_x,a_y)\) are depends on the chosen force_law, and that choice is Beat 1's subject. The program keeps \((x,y,u,v)\) as its state, and compute_acceleration(x, y, force_law) in physics_earthorbit.py returns \((a_x,a_y)\) at every step.

Beat 1 · Two ways to point at the centre

Both force laws point straight at Earth's centre; they disagree only about how the magnitude changes with distance. Run the same launch under both.

python main.py
python main.py --force_law inverse_square

Compare the printed outcome lines of the two runs.

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the sample counts: 503 for the default simplified run against 556 for inverse_square, and the outcome lines shift to match — 0.039512 of a revolution in 200.44 s against 0.043687 of a revolution in 221.62 s. Even at only 300 m altitude and 200-odd seconds of flight, the two force laws are already measurably different, not identical (Experiment 3 tracks how that difference grows with altitude). Then read the inverse_square run's two extra lines: an osculating eccentricity of 0.0013002 and a perigee altitude of −16,265 m, meaningfully below the surface, consistent with a run that ends in impact. Then read \(g\) and \(\mu_\oplus\): \(g=9.8\) m s−2 is the conventional, rounded textbook surface value, and \(\mu_\oplus=3.986\,004\,355\,07\times10^{14}\) m3 s−2 is the modern JPL DE440 Earth gravitational parameter. Neither one is defined from the other; they merely happen to approximately agree at Earth's reference radius, because \(g\) was chosen, long before JPL DE440, to match the measured surface acceleration. Dividing \(\mu_\oplus\) by \(R_\oplus^2\) gives 9.798092 m s−2, about 1.95 parts in 10,000 below the rounded \(g=9.8\); going the other way, \(gR_\oplus^2\approx3.98678\times10^{14}\), about 1.95 parts in 10,000 above \(\mu_\oplus\) (K_APPROX in physics_earthorbit.py is exactly this product, kept only for this comparison). Then ask which law a real satellite obeys: only inverse_square is real physics; the default is an approximation that happens to agree with it near the surface, to about 0.02% (the 1.95-parts-in-10,000 figure above), not by construction.

Then the two laws themselves. Both point from \((x,y)\) toward the origin, along the unit vector \((-x/r,-y/r)\); they differ only in the scalar multiplying it:

Eq. 2 — Simplified force law PRESCRIPTION
\[ a_x=-\frac{g\,x}{r},\qquad a_y=-\frac{g\,y}{r},\qquad |\mathbf a|=g \text{ at every } r. \]
Eq. 3 — Inverse-square force law ODE
\[ a_x=-\frac{\mu_\oplus\,x}{r^3},\qquad a_y=-\frac{\mu_\oplus\,y}{r^3},\qquad |\mathbf a|=\frac{\mu_\oplus}{r^2}. \]

Eq. 2 is tagged as a prescription rather than a physical law because it is a teaching choice: holding \(|\mathbf a|\) fixed at every radius is not how gravity behaves anywhere except, by construction, at \(r=R_\oplus\). Eq. 3 is Newton's actual law of universal gravitation, and it is what the rest of this page's orbital-mechanics content (Beats 2 through 4 and 8) is built on. compute_acceleration() validates \(x\) and \(y\) as finite, rejects the Earth-centre singularity \(r=0\), and translates an unrepresentable inverse-square magnitude into a clear ValueError instead of a silent overflow.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 2 and Experiment 3 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 2 · The speed that never lands

Newton's cannon predicts one exact speed, at any given altitude, for which the projectile's fall matches the curvature of the ground beneath it: a circular orbit. Launch at that speed, 300 km up, under the real inverse-square law.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 7000

Look at the outcome line, the per-revolution line, and the three osculating-element lines.

Three tasks, in order. First, the outcome: Orbit completed; termination: maxSteps reached, with Completed revolutions: 1. The projectile never touches the ground in the 7000 s simulated. Then the per-revolution line: Revolution 1: time 5460.9 s; minimum altitude 3.0000e+05 m; maximum altitude 3.4847e+05 m. The minimum altitude is exactly the launch altitude, as it should be for a circular orbit, but the maximum has crept up by 48.5 km over one turn — Beat 7 explains that drift; it is numerical, not physical. Then the three osculating-element lines: Osculating eccentricity: 6.1431e-06, Osculating perigee altitude: 2.9992e+05 m, Osculating apogee altitude: 3.0000e+05 m — very nearly, but not quite, a circle, computed from the launch state alone (Eq. 5 explains how). The command above rounds the circular speed to --uInit 7725.7, and that 0.0237 m/s rounding is exactly what the tiny but nonzero eccentricity is picking up: an osculating ellipse whose perigee sits about 82 m below the launch altitude and whose apogee sits exactly on it. Compare the revolution time, 5460.9 s, with the exact orbital period below: 5431.3 s, a difference of about 0.5%, consistent with the drift already noticed.

Then where 7725.7 m/s comes from. At orbital radius \(r=R_\oplus+h_0\), the circular condition balances centripetal acceleration against gravity, \(v_c^2/r=\mu_\oplus/r^2\); the escape speed, defined by zero total energy, is \(\sqrt2\) times that:

Eq. 4 — Circular and escape speed DERIVED
\[ v_c=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_\oplus}{r}},\qquad v_e=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu_\oplus}{r}}=\sqrt2\,v_c. \]

At \(h_0=300\) km, \(r=6\,678\,200\) m, Eq. 4 gives \(v_c=7725.72\) m/s and \(v_e=10\,925.8\) m/s — the command above rounds \(v_c\) to one decimal place, which is why the computed orbit is very slightly non-circular in principle, though not at the five-figure precision the console prints. The orbital period follows from the circumference divided by the speed, \(T=2\pi r/v_c=2\pi\sqrt{r^3/\mu_\oplus}\), which is 5431.3 s — Beat 8 returns to this formula. Beats 3 and 4 use the same two speeds as landmarks: below \(v_c\), the projectile returns to \(r=r_0\) as an apogee rather than staying there; between \(v_c\) and \(v_e\), the orbit is a bound ellipse; at or above \(v_e\), it never returns.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 1 and Experiment 4 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 3 · Between circular and escape

A launch speed between \(v_c\) and \(v_e\) should trace a closed ellipse with the launch point as one of its two apsides. Launch faster than circular at the same 300 km altitude.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 9000 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 10585

Look at the per-revolution line and the three osculating-element lines.

Three tasks, in order. First, the per-revolution line: Revolution 1: time 10580 s; minimum altitude 3.0000e+05 m; maximum altitude 7.7644e+06 m. The minimum altitude is again the launch altitude — this launch is tangential, so \(r_0\) is an apsis — but now the maximum is far higher, about 7.76 million metres, roughly the radius of Earth itself above the launch point. Then the osculating elements: Osculating eccentricity: 0.35708, Osculating perigee altitude: 3.0000e+05 m, Osculating apogee altitude: 7.7183e+06 m. The perigee altitude matches the launch altitude to five figures, confirming the launch point is the perigee, and the apogee is close to but not exactly the per-revolution maximum, because the two are computed differently: the osculating apogee is an exact two-body formula evaluated once from the launch state (Eq. 5), while the per-revolution maximum is a three-point parabola fit to the numerically integrated samples (Beat 6). Then ask why 9000 m/s, only 16.5% above the 7725.7 m/s circular speed, produces an apogee altitude more than 25 times the perigee altitude: orbital energy and eccentricity both grow quickly with speed near circular, which Eq. 5 makes precise.

Then the two-body formulas the console evaluates once, from \((x_0,y_0,u_0,v_0)\) alone, to get the osculating elements — the exact ellipse the projectile would follow from this instant onward under ideal two-body gravity:

Eq. 5 — Specific orbital energy and eccentricity DERIVED
\[ \mathcal E=\tfrac12(u_0^2+v_0^2)-\frac{\mu_\oplus}{r_0},\qquad h=x_0v_0-y_0u_0,\qquad e=\sqrt{1+\frac{2\mathcal Eh^2}{\mu_\oplus^2}}. \]

Here \(h\) is the specific angular momentum (not to be confused with altitude, also written \(h\) elsewhere on this page and in the program's h0). For a bound orbit, \(\mathcal E<0\), and the semimajor axis is \(a=-\mu_\oplus/(2\mathcal E)\), giving perigee and apogee radii \(a(1-e)\) and \(a(1+e)\); when the launch is tangential and at radius \(r_0\), one of the two apsides is exactly \(r_0\) itself, which is the perigee here because 9000 m/s exceeds the circular speed. These are the formulas _inverse_square_elements() in driver_earthorbit.py evaluates; the per-revolution minimum and maximum altitude, by contrast, come from the stored numerical samples and are described under Algorithm.

Beat 4 · Leaving for good

At or above the escape speed \(v_e\), Eq. 4 predicts the projectile never returns. Launch above it.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 11500 --vInit 0 --dt 2 --maxSteps 3001 --force_law inverse_square

Look at the outcome line and the osculating-element lines.

Three tasks, in order. First, the outcome: Escape trajectory; maxSteps reached, with 0.33682 of a revolution completed in 6000 s of flight — 121.26° of arc — reaching an interpolated maximum altitude of 3.3678e+07 m, about 33,700 km, more than five Earth radii above the surface, and still climbing when the run ends. Then the eccentricity: Osculating eccentricity: 1.2157 — greater than 1, the signature of an unbound (hyperbolic) orbit, for which Eq. 5's apogee formula does not apply; the perigee altitude still prints, 3.0000e+05 m, because a hyperbola has exactly one perigee and no apogee. Then the last line: Final/maximum altitude at maxSteps: 3.3678e+07 m. This combines what would otherwise be two separate lines — a final altitude and a maximum altitude — into one, because for this tangential, outward launch the projectile is still climbing when the run ends, so its final altitude and its maximum altitude to that point happen to be the same number; printing both would say the same thing twice. This is a property of this particular launch, not of every escape: an unbound trajectory aimed inward or with a nonzero vInit can descend first, swing past a perigee, and only then climb away, in which case its final and maximum altitudes differ and the summary prints them as two separate lines instead (see the Printed Summary section, which states this conditionally rather than as a rule).

Then why 11,500 m/s escapes. Eq. 4 gives \(v_e=10\,925.8\) m/s at 300 km altitude, so 11,500 m/s is 5.3% above it, and Eq. 5's energy \(\mathcal E=\tfrac12v_0^2-\mu_\oplus/r_0\) is positive whenever \(v_0>v_e\), which is exactly the condition \(e>1\) in the same equation. A positive-energy orbit is not a closed curve at all, so "orbit" is the wrong word for it; the program instead tracks how far it has climbed and reports that. The elements["unbound"] flag records \(\mathcal E\ge0\) directly, and escaped additionally requires that the run did not end in surface impact, so a launch that is technically unbound but happens to be aimed back at the ground (Experiment 5 explores non-tangential launches) is still reported as an impact, not an escape.

Beat 5 · Why the simplified law can't close an ellipse

Beat 0's puzzle was that a launch just 0.08% below the local circular speed impacted after only 14° of arc under the simplified force law. Try the same absolute launch speed, 7500 m/s, under both laws at 300 km altitude, where the two circular speeds are far enough apart to see clearly: 7725.7 m/s for inverse_square, 8089.9 m/s for simplified (Eq. 6 explains why these differ).

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7500 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 15000
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7500 --force_law simplified --dt 1 --maxSteps 15000

Compare the two outcome lines and the angular travel before impact.

Three tasks, in order. First, inverse_square: impact after 0.21440 of a revolution, 77.183° of arc, in 1160.5 s, with an osculating eccentricity of only 0.057581 — a gently eccentric ellipse whose perigee happens to lie 427 km below the surface. Then simplified: impact after just 0.13321 of a revolution, 47.954° of arc, in only 720.81 s — noticeably less arc and less time. Then check whether that is a fair comparison before reading anything into it: 7500 m/s is only 2.92% below the inverse_square circular speed but 7.29% below the simplified one — the same absolute speed is not the same fractional shortfall, so simplified starting from more than twice the relative deficit is at least part of why it impacts sooner. This run, by itself, has not yet isolated the effect Eq. 6 describes.

A controlled comparison needs the same fractional deficit under both laws. 7500 m/s is 2.9217% below inverse_square's circular speed; the equivalent 2.9217%-below-circular launch under simplified is --uInit 7853.53:

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7853.53 --force_law simplified --dt 1 --maxSteps 15000

This run does not impact at all within 15,000 steps: Completed revolutions: 2, with a first-revolution minimum altitude of just 46,686 m — deep, but still above ground — and a second revolution that dips only to 65,796 m, again drifting shallower rather than deeper (the same energy-gain drift Beat 7 measures and Experiment 3 revisits). So at matched fractional deficits the two laws' behaviour is not simply "simplified impacts sooner": here it is inverse_square that impacts and simplified that survives two full revolutions. The 7500 m/s comparison above and this matched-fraction comparison disagree on which law looks more forgiving, which is itself the lesson: Eq. 6 is a property of each law near exactly circular, not a promise about which law's projectile falls back to Earth first at some particular finite deficit, and a demonstration that "looks different" is not automatically evidence for a specific claimed reason unless the setup actually isolates that reason.

Then the reason a "circular-ish" launch behaves so differently under the two laws. Perturb a circular orbit of radius \(r\) very slightly and ask how fast the radius oscillates compared with how fast the angle advances. For a central force of magnitude \(f(r)\) (directed inward), the ratio of the two frequencies — the epicyclic frequency \(\kappa\) to the orbital angular frequency \(\omega\) — is a standard result of orbital mechanics:

Eq. 6 — Epicyclic-to-orbital frequency ratio DERIVED
\[ \frac{\kappa^2}{\omega^2}=3+\frac{r}{f}\frac{df}{dr}. \]

For the inverse-square law, \(f=\mu_\oplus/r^2\), so \(r f'/f=-2\) and \(\kappa^2/\omega^2=1\): the radial wobble and the angular revolution keep exact step, which is precisely why every bound inverse-square orbit closes into a repeating ellipse (this is Bertrand's theorem; the isotropic harmonic oscillator is the only other force law with the same property). For the simplified law, \(f=g\) is constant, so \(f'=0\) and \(\kappa^2/\omega^2=3\): the radial oscillation runs \(\sqrt3\approx1.732\) times faster than the orbital revolution, so a perturbed "circular" orbit does not retrace a fixed ellipse — it precesses into a rosette, and the radial excursion reaches its low point well before a full turn is complete, consistent with impact arriving at a smaller fraction of a revolution than the inverse-square case above. This is why the warning under Description says the simplified law does not share the inverse-square law's special closure property, and why a small speed deficit near "circular" is riskier under it than the inverse-square intuition suggests.

A circular orbit itself is exact under either law. Eq. 6's mismatch only affects perturbed orbits. A launch at exactly the local circular speed stays exactly circular under any central force, including the simplified one, because the centripetal balance \(v^2/r=f(r)\) holds at every point of a circle regardless of how \(f\) varies elsewhere. The near-circular simplified runs in Beat 7 slowly gain altitude for a different, purely numerical reason — the fixed-step integrator's own energy drift — not because of Eq. 6.

Beat 6 · Between the samples

The stored trajectory is a list of points \(\Delta t\) apart. Surface impact, revolution boundaries, and altitude extrema almost never land exactly on a stored sample, so the printed summary estimates them between samples rather than reading them off directly. See what the default run has to work with.

python main.py
python -c "from driver_earthorbit import run_earth_orbit as run; r = run(dt=0.4, maxSteps=15000, return_diagnostics=True); import math; print([math.hypot(x,y) for x,y in zip(r[0][-2:], r[1][-2:])])"

The first command prints the summary. The second, run from the program's folder, prints the radii of the last two stored points. Look at the printed range and the printed flight time, and at those two radii.

Three tasks, in order. First, the raw endpoint. The last two stored radii are 6,378,200.17 m and 6,378,199.01 m against \(R_\oplus=6{,}378{,}200\) m — the second-to-last sample is still 0.172 m above the surface, and the last is 0.991 m below it. The loop stops at the first point below the surface, so the raw final sample already lies inside the ground, but only by about a metre here because \(\Delta t=0.4\) s is fine relative to the impact speed. Then interpolate by hand: a fraction \(0.172/(0.172+0.991)=0.1479\) of the way from the second-to-last sample to the last would land exactly on the surface — but the program's printed impact fraction is 0.09183, not 0.1479, because the interpolation is not along radius but along the straight chord between the two \((x,y)\) points and where that chord crosses the circle \(r=R_\oplus\) (Eq. 8), which is close to but not identical to a linear interpolation of radius alone. Then the printed flight time, 200.44 s (200.4367 s unrounded): the second-to-last sample is at \(t=200.4\) s, one step short of \(t=200.8\) s, and \(0.4\times0.09183\approx0.0367\) s, which is exactly the extra 0.0367 s the printed flight time adds to 200.4 s — the same 0.09183 interpolation fraction used for position, applied to time (Eq. 8).

Then the three estimation rules, described in full under Algorithm: a straight-chord/circle intersection for surface impact (Eq. 8), linear interpolation in accumulated angle for revolution boundaries, and a local three-point parabola for altitude extrema (Eq. 9). All three use the raw stored samples only; none of them alters the arrays the plot draws, so the red trajectory line always shows the actual integrated points, including the one sample that lies fractionally inside Earth after an impact run.

Beat 7 · How far to trust a printed number

Beat 2's circular orbit drifted outward by 48.5 km over one revolution at \(\Delta t=1\) s. Is that a lot? Repeat the same circular launch at several timesteps, sized generously enough that each run completes its first revolution, and watch the drift in the Revolution 1 line's maximum altitude. The numerically integrated period always comes out a little longer than the exact \(T=5431.3\) s (Eq. 4) — that is itself part of the same drift this beat measures — so each --maxSteps below allows some room beyond the exact-period estimate.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 4 --maxSteps 1450
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 2 --maxSteps 2890
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 5481
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 0.5 --maxSteps 10950
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 0.25 --maxSteps 21890

Three tasks, in order. First, read the drift column: each time \(\Delta t\) is halved, the drift in maximum altitude very nearly halves too — a clean signature of a first-order numerical method. Then read the revolution-time column the same way: it too converges toward the exact 5431.3 s as \(\Delta t\) shrinks — 5550.8, 5490.8, 5460.9, 5446.1, 5438.7 s — the same drift showing up as a period error instead of an altitude error. Then ask what "first order" costs here: to cut either error in half you double the number of stored points, and each additional decimal figure of accuracy costs roughly another factor of ten in step count. Beat 2's 300 km circular orbit was run at \(\Delta t=1\) s specifically to make the drift visible for teaching purposes; a student measuring an orbit quantitatively should use a finer step, such as \(\Delta t=0.1\) s or smaller, and confirm the answer stops changing by repeating at an even finer step.

Then the source of the drift: the integration step the driver applies at every iteration, described in full under Algorithm:

Eq. 7 — Averaged-velocity integration step (numerical method, untagged)
\[ u_{n+1}=u_n+a_x(x_n,y_n)\,\Delta t,\qquad v_{n+1}=v_n+a_y(x_n,y_n)\,\Delta t, \] \[ x_{n+1}=x_n+\tfrac12(u_n+u_{n+1})\,\Delta t,\qquad y_{n+1}=y_n+\tfrac12(v_n+v_{n+1})\,\Delta t. \]

Acceleration is evaluated once per step, at the position the step started from, not at its midpoint or end; only the position update uses the average of the two velocities. This is not velocity Verlet or leapfrog, and it is not symplectic, so it does not conserve the orbit's energy exactly even when the exact orbit is a circle. A coarse enough step makes this dramatic: try --dt 10 --maxSteps 15000 at the same launch as the default (300 m altitude, 7900 m/s, simplified). It completes 25 full revolutions rather than impacting, and its per-revolution altitude climbs from a minimum of about 192 m to a maximum of 4.78 million metres by the 25th — a runaway numerical energy gain, not a physical one, for a launch that impacted almost immediately at the default \(\Delta t=0.4\) s. This is the sharpest illustration on this page of why Beat 9 and the warning under Description say the algorithm is not symplectic: two runs that differ only in timestep can disagree not just in a few digits but in the entire qualitative outcome.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 9 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 8 · Kepler's third law, and its absence

Kepler's third law, \(T^2\propto r^3\), is a signature of the inverse-square law specifically — Eq. 6 already showed the simplified law does not share the inverse-square law's closed-orbit property, so it should not share this one either. Compare circular periods at two different altitudes under each force law.

python main.py --h0 300000  --uInit 7725.7237 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 5481
python main.py --h0 1500000 --uInit 7113.0422 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 7009
python main.py --h0 300000  --uInit 8089.8925 --force_law simplified     --dt 1 --maxSteps 5216
python main.py --h0 1500000 --uInit 8786.7150 --force_law simplified     --dt 1 --maxSteps 5683

Look at each run's per-revolution-time line.

Three tasks, in order. First, the two inverse_square periods: Revolution 1: time 5460.9 s at \(r=6{,}678{,}200\) m and Revolution 1: time 6988.7 s at \(r=7{,}878{,}200\) m. Divide \(T^2\) by \(r^3\) for each, using these printed, numerically integrated periods: \(1.0013\times10^{-13}\) and \(9.9888\times10^{-14}\) — close to each other (within about 1.2%) but not identical, because these periods carry the same first-order drift Beat 7 measured; the run was only stepped to \(\Delta t=1\) s, not the finer step a precise test would need. Then the two simplified periods: Revolution 1: time 5189.8 s and Revolution 1: time 5636.6 s at the same two radii. Divide \(T^2\) by \(r\) instead of \(r^3\): \(4.0331\) and \(4.0328\) — much closer to each other than the inverse_square pair, simply because the simplified circular periods here are shorter and so accumulate less drift per orbit at the same \(\Delta t\), not because the underlying law is being tested more precisely. Then compare these measured constants with the exact values Eq. 10 predicts from \(r\) and \(\mu_\oplus\) or \(g\) alone, with no integration involved: \(4\pi^2/\mu_\oplus=9.9043\times 10^{-14}\) s2 m−3, about 0.85–1.10% below the two measured inverse-square ratios; \(4\pi^2/g=4.0284\) s2 m−1, about 0.11–0.12% below the two measured simplified ratios. Both force laws show the same pattern — the printed, integrated period always comes out a little long, which is why its \(T^2/r^n\) always comes out a little high — and the inverse-square case shows it about ten times more strongly than the simplified case at this \(\Delta t\), consistent with Beat 7's absolute drift table. Neither run used the same launch speed at both altitudes — each used its own circular speed from Eq. 4 or its simplified-law analogue — which is exactly what a fair test of "does period depend only on radius, in this fixed way" requires.

Then the two period formulas, both following from circumference divided by circular speed and the corresponding entry in Eq. 4 or its analogue:

Eq. 10 — Circular period, inverse-square versus simplified DERIVED
\[ T_{\text{inv-sq}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu_\oplus}}\ \Rightarrow\ T^2\propto r^3, \qquad T_{\text{simplified}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r}{g}}\ \Rightarrow\ T^2\propto r. \]

The inverse-square case is Kepler's third law exactly, with the constant of proportionality \(4\pi^2/\mu_\oplus\) the same for every satellite of Earth regardless of its orbit's size, because it depends only on \(\mu_\oplus\) — not on which planet the satellites orbit. A satellite of a different body uses that body's own \(\mu\) and gets a different constant; using \(\mu\) rather than mass directly is what makes the constant depend on the central body alone and not on the satellite's own mass. The simplified case is not a law of nature at all — it is what follows from assuming, incorrectly, that gravity keeps the surface value \(g\) at every altitude — and the very different \(r\)-dependence is one more way (besides Eq. 6) of seeing that the two force laws are not the same physics at different levels of approximation, but genuinely different force laws that happen to agree at one radius.

Beat 9 · Where the model and the run stop

Everything so far assumed the settings were physically sensible and the run had room to finish. Both assumptions have limits. Try a few commands that reach them.

python main.py --h0 -1
python main.py --dt 0
python main.py --maxSteps 1
python main.py --uInit 1e308 --dt 10

Look at what each command prints.

Three tasks, in order. First, the input checks: --h0 -1 prints EarthOrbit: h0 must be non-negative for this Earth-surface launch model; --dt 0 prints EarthOrbit: dt must be a positive finite timestep in seconds; --maxSteps 1 prints EarthOrbit: maxSteps must be an integer >= 2. Each stops the program with a one-line, prefixed message rather than a Python traceback, and none of them opens a plot. Then the overflow guard: an extreme but individually finite uInit and dt can make the integrated state overflow; the driver detects this and prints EarthOrbit: integration produced a non-finite state; reduce the timestep or use less extreme initial values rather than silently returning infinities. A related, more subtle guard covers a different failure of a large dt: a single step large enough that its straight-line displacement passes all the way through Earth, entering on one side and exiting the other, with neither endpoint landing inside the reference sphere. Left undetected, that step would report whatever the far side of Earth happens to look like — an ordinary orbit or an escape — instead of the impact that actually occurred partway through it; the driver instead raises EarthOrbit: this step's straight-line displacement passed through Earth's reference surface without either endpoint landing inside it; dt is too coarse to resolve the surface crossing -- reduce dt. This check tests the stored straight-line chord between two consecutive samples -- the same chord the averaged-velocity update in Eq. 7 actually advances along, so it is consistent with what the integrator itself does, not with the true curved path between samples. That means it can only see a chord that clips the sphere; a genuinely curved trajectory that stays outside Earth the whole time while its straight-line chord dips inside would still be rejected as if it had crossed, which is the conservative direction for a safety check to err in. Every command on this page uses a dt far below the size that would trigger this, but a very large --dt paired with a fast inward vInit can reach it. Then notice what EarthOrbit does not do: unlike some other GFTGU programs, it never raises an error merely because maxSteps is reached before impact or before a full revolution — reaching the step budget is treated as a normal, if incomplete, outcome, and the summary says so explicitly ("No complete revolution before maxSteps" or "Escape trajectory; maxSteps reached"), which is why every escape example on this page supplied a generous --maxSteps rather than needing the program to detect and reject a too-small one.

Then the list of what the model leaves out, which no command-line setting can restore because the program has no parameter for it. The simulation is two-dimensional: it cannot represent inclined or out-of-plane orbits. It has no atmosphere or drag, so no re-entry heating or deceleration; no wind; no Earth rotation, so no Coriolis effect and no distinction between a launch to the east and a launch to the west; no oblateness, so no J2 precession; no third-body forces from the Moon or Sun; and no station-keeping. The fixed-step, non-adaptive, non-symplectic integrator of Eq. 7 accumulates first-order error and does not exactly conserve energy (Beat 7); the osculating elements of Eq. 5 describe the ideal two-body orbit implied by the launch state, not necessarily the numerically integrated trajectory's actual long-term behaviour, especially at large \(\Delta t\) or after many revolutions. Impact and revolution-boundary interpolation (Eqs. 8 and 9) converge to the true crossing as \(\Delta t\to0\) but are not exact at finite \(\Delta t\). Five-significant-figure display supports comparisons between runs; it is not a claim that the underlying physical model or numerical method is accurate to five figures.

Algorithm

This section collects the program's numerical rules in one place. Eq. 7 is introduced in Beat 7; Eqs. 8 and 9 in Beat 6.

One run, in order

  1. Validate the settings: h0 must be non-negative and finite, uInit, vInit, and dt must be finite with dt>0, maxSteps must be an integer of at least 2, and force_law must be "simplified" or "inverse_square".
  2. Store the launch point \((0,\,R_\oplus+h_0)\) with velocity \((\texttt{uInit},\texttt{vInit})\).
  3. While the last stored radius is at least \(R_\oplus\) and fewer than maxSteps points are stored, advance the state one timestep with Eq. 7, using the acceleration law selected by force_law, and store the new \(x\) and \(y\) (and, if diagnostics are requested, \(t\), \(u\), and \(v\)). Each step also checks whether its straight-line displacement passed through Earth's reference sphere without landing inside it — a coarse-\(\Delta t\) failure mode distinct from ending inside the sphere — and stops with a clear error if so (Beat 9).
  4. Build a 401-point closed reference circle for Earth's surface: 400 evenly spaced angular samples, with the first point repeated as coordinate 401 to close the curve.
  5. Analyze the stored trajectory (below), print the summary, and plot the trajectory against the reference circle.

Unlike some other GFTGU programs, this loop never raises an error for reaching maxSteps before impact or before a full revolution; that is a normal, reported outcome (Beat 9). It does validate that maxSteps is large enough to hold at least the launch point and one advance.

Surface-impact interpolation

When the trajectory ends in impact, the console analysis does not use the raw final sample, which already lies below the surface; it finds where the straight chord between the last two stored points crosses the reference circle \(r=R_\oplus\):

Eq. 8 — Surface-impact interpolation (numerical method, untagged)
\[ \text{solve } |\,(x_{n-1},y_{n-1})+s\,[(x_n,y_n)-(x_{n-1},y_{n-1})]\,|=R_\oplus \text{ for } s\in[0,1], \] \[ \text{then } t^*=t_{n-1}+s(t_n-t_{n-1}). \]

This is a quadratic in \(s\); the program keeps the smaller root that lies in \([0,1]\) (with a small numerical tolerance), which is the first crossing along the segment. The interpolated position, time, and distance travelled all use this same \(s\); the plotted trajectory is unaffected and still shows the raw stored point below the surface.

Revolution boundaries and altitude extrema

Accumulated angular progress is tracked from the launch direction, unwrapped and made monotonic. A completed revolution's start and end times and positions are found by linear interpolation in progress against the target angles \(2\pi(n-1)\) and \(2\pi n\); each revolution's minimum and maximum altitude then use a local three-point parabola through the extreme stored sample and its two neighbours, falling back to the stored sample itself when the parabola's vertex would fall outside the surrounding interval:

Eq. 9 — Three-point parabola for a local altitude extremum (numerical method, untagged)
\[ \delta=h_{k-1}-2h_k+h_{k+1},\qquad H^*=h_k-\frac{(h_{k+1}-h_{k-1})^2}{8\delta}\quad\text{when } \delta\ne0 \text{ and the vertex lies within the samples.} \]

The same three-point parabola locates the overall maximum altitude reported for a run that does not complete a revolution. All of Eqs. 8 and 9 are implemented in analyze_earth_orbit() and its helpers in driver_earthorbit.py, and none of them alters the arrays returned to the plotter.

Version and Build ID. python main.py --version prints the model version and the Build ID without running a simulation. The Build ID is a short hash of the four program modules listed under Code Modules and of nothing else — this Help file, the Release Notes, the sample outputs, and the test suite are not part of it. It appears at the top of this page and on the first line of every run's printed summary.

Output

The matplotlib plot displays the title EarthOrbit — Attempting to Achieve Orbit and shows:

  • a blue dashed circle for Earth's surface, from the 401-point reference curve,
  • a red trajectory line through every stored sample, including the first one below the surface after an impact run,
  • equal horizontal and vertical physical scales, so circles and ellipses are not visually distorted,
  • grid lines and a legend.

run_earth_orbit() returns NumPy arrays xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth by default; with return_diagnostics=True it additionally returns ts, us, and vs, aligned with the position arrays, for period, energy, and convergence experiments. plot_earth_orbit(xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth) displays the figure and returns its matplotlib Figure and Axes.

Interpolation does not alter the plotted arrays. The console summary's surface-impact, revolution-boundary, and altitude-extremum estimates (Eqs. 8 and 9) are computed alongside the raw stored trajectory, never in place of it; the final plotted point may lie slightly below the surface after an impact run, exactly as stored, even though the printed range and flight time report the interpolated crossing.

Printed Summary

Every run prints a summary and then opens the plot. The first line gives the version, the Build ID, and the number of trajectory samples. This is the full summary of the default run, python main.py:

EarthOrbit 1.4.0 (build 08cdcf30c04d) — 503 trajectory samples
Outcome: Surface impact before one revolution
Fraction of a revolution: 0.039512
Total flight time: 200.44 s
Total angular travel: 14.224 deg

Total distance traveled (interpolated to impact): 1.5835e+06 m

The summary's shape depends on the outcome (Beats 0 through 4 show each in turn):

Impact or Incomplete Trajectory

If the projectile does not complete a revolution, the summary reports the fraction of a revolution traversed, total flight time, total angular travel, interpolated maximum altitude, and total path distance. For impact, the time and final path segment are interpolated to the Earth circle (Eq. 8) rather than using the raw first below-surface sample. If maxSteps ends the run first, the distance is reported through the final stored sample instead.

One or More Completed Revolutions

Once accumulated angular travel reaches \(2\pi\), the summary reports the number of completed revolutions, total flight time, and total angular travel, and for every completed revolution its interpolated duration and its minimum and maximum altitude (Eq. 9). A partial final revolution contributes to the totals but gets no separate per-revolution line.

Inverse-Square Elements and Escape

With --force_law inverse_square, the launch state defines osculating Keplerian elements (Eq. 5): eccentricity and perigee altitude always print, and a bound ellipse (\(e<1\)) also prints an apogee altitude. An unbound trajectory (\(e\ge1\)) has no apogee and instead prints its final altitude at maxSteps; when that altitude is also the largest reached, the two identical concepts are combined into one line rather than printed twice.

All real numbers are printed to five significant figures. Use python main.py --version to print only the version and Build ID, with no simulation.

Suggested Experiments

Suggested order: the experiments below run roughly from introductory parameter exploration to more demanding numerical-analysis and code-modification exercises. The standard printout already supplies most quantities discussed on this page; a few experiments additionally ask you to inspect raw diagnostic arrays or vary a setting the console summary does not sweep for you.
Experiment 1 · Introductory
Find the circular speed by bisection

Goes with Beat 0 and Beat 2.

Core task: Without using Eq. 4, find the inverse-square circular speed at \(h_0=300\) km by bisection: run python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit <guess> --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 8000 at a few guessed speeds. The osculating eccentricity alone cannot tell you which way to move your guess — it is zero only exactly at circular and grows the same way on both sides of it — so read the perigee and apogee altitude lines instead: for a tangential launch, whichever one equals your launch altitude \(h_0\) tells you where the launch point sits on the orbit. If the printed apogee altitude matches \(h_0\), your guess is below circular (the launch point is the far side of the orbit) and you should raise it; if the perigee altitude matches \(h_0\), your guess is above circular and you should lower it; narrow the bracket until the eccentricity itself is small. Extension: Automate this bisection in Python using run_earth_orbit() and analyze_earth_orbit() directly — the sign test above is exactly elements["apogee_altitude"] versus elements["perigee_altitude"] compared with h0 — and confirm your answer against Eq. 4 to five figures.

Check: Eq. 4 gives 7725.72 m/s at 300 km altitude. At 7700 m/s, below circular, the printed apogee altitude (3.0000e+05 m) matches \(h_0\) while the perigee altitude (2.1179e+05 m) does not — the sign the bisection needs. A guess within a few m/s of 7725.72 should print an eccentricity below 0.001, with both perigee and apogee close to \(h_0\).

Push maxSteps and dt to their edges

Experiment 3 · Introductory–Intermediate
Where the two force laws start to disagree

Goes with Beat 1 and Beat 5.

Beat 1 already found the two laws measurably different at 300 m altitude over 200 s — different sample counts, different flight times — even though both still end in impact. This experiment asks a different question: not when the two laws first differ, but when that difference starts to change the outcome itself. Raise --h0 (try 30,000, then 300,000) at the default uInit and compare the two force laws' outcome lines at each altitude. At what altitude does the choice of force law start to change which of impact, orbit, or escape the program reports?

Check: at \(h_0=300{,}000\) m, uInit=7900 completes revolutions under both force laws within maxSteps=15000 at \(\Delta t=1\) s, but very differently: inverse_square gives a mildly eccentric ellipse (eccentricity 0.045625, apogee altitude 9.3851e+05 m) while simplified dips to a first-revolution minimum altitude of only 97,103 m — more than 200 km below the 300 km launch altitude — and then, in its second revolution, dips to a shallower 116,306 m rather than a deeper one: the fixed-step drift Beat 7 measures is an energy gain, so successive close approaches under simplified trend higher, not lower, even as the orbit keeps precessing.

Experiment 6 · Intermediate
Two impact boundaries, not one: the analytic threshold versus the numerical threshold

Goes with Beat 2, Beat 3, and Beat 4.

At \(h_0=300\) km under inverse_square, run a sequence of launch speeds spanning well below \(v_c=7725.7\) m/s to well above \(v_e=10{,}925.8\) m/s — for example every 500 m/s from 6000 to 12000 — and record each run's outcome and, when printed, its osculating eccentricity. Plot eccentricity against launch speed and identify the orbit-versus-escape boundary at \(v_e\).

Now narrow in on the impact boundary. This is not one question but two, and they have different answers. The analytic threshold comes from the launch state alone: below \(v_c\) the launch point is an apogee (Beat 3's note under Eq. 5), so bisect on launch speed watching only the printed Osculating perigee altitude line, and find where it crosses zero. That line is computed from Eq. 5 using \(h_0\), \(u_0\), \(v_0\) alone — no dt, no maxSteps, no integration — and the crossing is at 7636.45 m/s. The numerical threshold is a different bisection at a fixed \(\Delta t\): watch the printed Outcome line instead, and find where that flips. This one uses the actual stepped trajectory — the same first-order, non-symplectic stepper Beat 7 and Beat 9 already show drifting away from the exact orbit.

For the numerical bisection, maxSteps matters as much as \(\Delta t\). Give every run enough steps to cover about 6000 s of flight — longer than one orbital period at this altitude — which means --maxSteps 6001 at \(\Delta t=1\) s, 12001 at 0.5 s, and 60001 at 0.1 s:

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7635 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1   --maxSteps 6001
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7635 --force_law inverse_square --dt 0.5 --maxSteps 12001
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7635 --force_law inverse_square --dt 0.1 --maxSteps 60001

Count a speed as surviving only when the Outcome line says Orbit completed. The default maxSteps=15000 is not enough at \(\Delta t=0.1\) s: it stops after 1499.9 s with No complete revolution before maxSteps, which says nothing about whether an impact would have come later. At 7635.72 m/s, for example, that short run stops there, while the 60001-step run reports Surface impact before one revolution after 2599.2 s.

Near the ground the two thresholds do not agree. At \(u_0=7635\) m/s the analytic osculating perigee altitude is \(-4{,}733.5\) m, so the ideal two-body orbit passes under the ground. Yet the first command above (\(\Delta t=1\) s) reports a completed orbit with no impact at all (minimum altitude \(+18{,}102\) m). The second (\(\Delta t=0.5\) s) also completes an orbit, with its minimum down to \(+6{,}872.2\) m, and only the third (\(\Delta t=0.1\) s) actually strikes the surface. The stepper can carry the projectile past a perigee the ideal orbit would have put underground, because it samples position too coarsely to register the dip.

Scanning launch speed in steps of 0.01 m/s with those three commands gives the flip points below. At \(\Delta t=1\) s there is also a third outcome between the two Outcome dialects: at 7629.44 m/s the program stops with Beat 9's Earth-crossing error (EarthOrbit: this step's straight-line displacement passed through Earth's reference surface ...) instead of printing an Outcome line, because one coarse step's chord passes through the reference sphere. Treat that as "reduce \(\Delta t\)", not as either impact or orbit.

The gap is measured from the lowest surviving speed. It shrinks roughly in proportion to \(\Delta t\): halving the step from 1 s to 0.5 s roughly halves it, and cutting the step by five from 0.5 s to 0.1 s cuts it by about five. That is the signature of first-order global convergence, the same behaviour Beat 7's drift table shows. The analytic 7636.45 m/s answers "where is the ideal perigee at the ground", a question that needs no \(\Delta t\) at all. The numbers in the table answer "where does this program, at this step size, report an impact", which cannot be asked without choosing a \(\Delta t\), and they approach the analytic value from below as \(\Delta t\) shrinks.

Check: 9000 m/s gives eccentricity 0.35708 (bound ellipse); 11,500 m/s gives eccentricity 1.2157 (unbound); Eq. 5 places the bound/unbound transition exactly at \(e=1\), i.e. \(v_0=v_e\), and Beat 4's own 11,500 m/s run is the concrete case that crosses it. Contrast the 300 km case above with Beat 0's near-surface default launch, where the analytic threshold sits only about 0.09 m/s below the local circular speed instead of 89 m/s — the "how far below circular is safe" margin depends entirely on altitude, which is exactly the point this experiment is meant to make concrete, and the analytic/numerical split above is the reminder that "safe" also depends on \(\Delta t\) near the ground.

Experiment 7 · Intermediate–Advanced
Plot the rosette

Goes with Beat 5.

Call run_earth_orbit() directly with force_law="simplified" at a speed noticeably below the local simplified-law circular speed (Beat 5 uses 7500 m/s against 8089.9 m/s at 300 km) but small enough not to impact within a few thousand stored points, and plot the trajectory with plot_earthorbit.plot_earth_orbit(). Identify, by eye, that the point of closest approach to Earth does not recur at the same angle on successive inward swings — the rosette pattern Eq. 6 predicts. Repeat with force_law="inverse_square" at the corresponding fraction of its own circular speed and confirm the perigee angle stays fixed instead.

Check: a launch speed below either circular speed makes the launch altitude an apogee, not a perigee, so look for the closest-approach point elsewhere in the orbit, not at the launch point itself.

Experiment 8 · Intermediate–Advanced
Interpolate the impact point by hand

Goes with Beat 6.

Write a short script that calls run_earth_orbit(return_diagnostics=True) for the default launch, takes the last two stored \((x,y)\) points, and solves Eq. 8's quadratic in \(s\) yourself with numpy or the quadratic formula. Compare your \(s\) and the resulting impact point with analyze_earth_orbit()'s impact_fraction and with the printed range and flight time.

Check: for the default run, the last two stored radii are 6,378,200.17 m and 6,378,199.01 m, and the correct impact fraction is 0.09183, not the naive radius-only interpolation 0.1479 (Beat 6 explains the difference).

Experiment 9 · Advanced
Reproduce the drift table

Goes with Beat 7.

Reproduce Beat 7's five-row table yourself: run the 300 km circular launch at \(\Delta t=4,\,2,\,1,\,0.5,\,0.25\) s (or add a finer \(\Delta t=0.1\) s row of your own), record each run's interpolated maximum altitude, and confirm that the drift above 300 km very nearly halves each time \(\Delta t\) halves. Then reproduce the dt=10 catastrophic case from the same beat under the default near-surface launch and describe, in your own words, why a numerical "orbit" at that timestep should not be trusted.

Check: Beat 7's table gives drifts of 193.24, 96.82, 48.47, 24.25 and 12.13 km above 300 km at \(\Delta t=4,\,2,\,1,\,0.5,\,0.25\) s; each halving of \(\Delta t\) should roughly halve the drift.

Experiment 10 · Advanced
Verify Kepler's third law numerically

Goes with Beat 8.

Run circular orbits under inverse_square at three or more altitudes of your choosing, each at its own circular speed from Eq. 4, using a small enough \(\Delta t\) that Beat 7's drift does not confuse the comparison. Record each run's per-revolution time and compute \(T^2/r^3\) for each. Then repeat the same three altitudes under simplified and compute \(T^2/r\) instead. Plot \(\log T\) against \(\log r\) for both force laws on the same axes and read the slope: 3/2 for inverse-square, 1/2 for simplified.

Check: the exact target values, from \(4\pi^2/\mu_\oplus\) and \(4\pi^2/g\) with no integration involved, are \(T^2/r^3=9.9043\times10^{-14}\) s2 m−3 for inverse_square and \(T^2/r=4.0284\) s2 m−1 for simplified. Beat 8's own two altitudes, run at only \(\Delta t=1\) s, land close but not exact — \(1.0013\times10^{-13}\) and \(9.9888\times10^{-14}\) for inverse_square, about 0.85–1.10% high; \(4.0331\) and \(4.0328\) for simplified, about 0.11–0.12% high — so a small \(\Delta t\) here matters more for a clean Kepler's-third-law check than it might first appear; that is the whole point of choosing "a small enough \(\Delta t\)" in the core task above.

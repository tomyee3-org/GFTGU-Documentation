Description

EarthOrbit is a numerical realisation of Newton's cannon, the thought experiment Newton used in the Principia (1687) to argue that falling and orbiting are the same motion. A projectile is launched horizontally from a tower above Earth's surface. Launched with too large a shortfall from the local circular speed, it falls back to the ground on a curve; launched fast enough, it falls at exactly the rate the ground curves away beneath it, tracing a path that never lands. Exactly how large a shortfall counts as "too large" depends on how high above the surface the tower stands — Experiment 6 works this out precisely, including how the answer differs from the program's own numerically integrated outcome. The program integrates the projectile's two-dimensional trajectory step by step and reports which of these outcomes a given launch produces: impact, a closed orbit, or escape.

The dashed blue circle is Earth's reference surface; red is the computed path. The plot uses geocentric metres on equal axes. At launch, uInit points sideways and positive vInit points away from Earth's centre. Each printed number has five significant digits; that is formatting, not a claim of five-digit physical accuracy.

Reading the summary

Every run prints a summary at the terminal and then opens a plot.

In the summary, the first line gives version, build and the number of stored trajectory samples. “Surface impact” uses an interpolated crossing; “Orbit completed” means at least one numerically tracked revolution; “Escape trajectory” requires an unbound inverse-square launch and no impact during the run. A maximum-step stop says only where integration ended.

Completed revolutions, angular travel, flight time, distance and altitude extremes refer to the computed path. The extra Osculating lines in inverse-square runs are ideal two-body elements calculated from the launch state. The two kinds of number can differ because the finite-step integrator drifts. For an unbound launch there is no osculating apogee. A final/maximum altitude line is combined only if both format to the same value.

Beat 0 · A near miss that hits

Launch 300 m up at 7900 m/s with the default simplified gravity law.

python main.py
Look for

503 samples and Surface impact before one revolution: 0.039512 of a turn, 14.224°, in 200.44 s. On the plot, the red arc joins the blue surface again. The launch is only about 0.08% slower than this law's circular speed, 7906.3 m/s.

At each timestep the program updates position \((x,y)\) and velocity \((u,v)\) under acceleration toward Earth's centre. Its default law keeps that acceleration's magnitude fixed at 9.8 m/s². Ask: would you have expected a tiny speed shortfall to yield such a short flight?

Beat 1 · Two gravity laws

Change just the force law and compare the outcome lines.

python main.py
python main.py --force_law inverse_square
Compare

The first run impacts in 200.44 s; inverse square takes 221.62 s and travels 0.043687 of a revolution. The inverse-square summary adds osculating elements from the launch state; a negative perigee altitude (−16,265 m) signals a path that intersects Earth.

Both acceleration vectors point inward. The first has constant magnitude \(g=9.8\) m/s²; the second has magnitude \(\mu_\oplus/r^2\), where \(\mu_\oplus=3.98600435507\times10^{14}\) m³/s² and \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). The two nearly agree at the reference surface, but differ as altitude grows.

Beat 2 · The circular launch

Move the cannon to 300 km and choose the inverse-square circular speed rounded to 7725.7 m/s.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 7000
Read

One completed revolution; its time is 5460.9 s. The integrated maximum altitude reaches 348.47 km, although the launch is at 300 km. The osculating eccentricity from the launch state is only 6.1431e-06. These lines refer to different things: the numerically computed path drifts outward, while the ideal orbit is nearly circular.

The ideal inverse-square speeds at \(r=R_\oplus+h_0\) are \(v_c=\sqrt{\mu_\oplus/r}\) and \(v_e=\sqrt{2\mu_\oplus/r}\). At 300 km they are about 7725.72 and 10925.8 m/s; the ideal circular period is 5431.3 s. Compare that with the printed period before moving on.

Beat 3 · An ellipse

Keep the altitude but launch faster than circular and below escape.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 9000 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 10585
Read

Revolution 1 takes 10580 s and reaches a computed maximum altitude of 7.7644e+06 m. The launch-state osculating apogee is 7.7183e+06 m. The gap measures numerical drift, not a change to the launch-state formula.

Earth is at a focus, not the centre of the ellipse. Since 9000 m/s exceeds the local circular speed, the tangential launch starts at perigee. The ideal eccentricity follows from specific energy \(\mathcal E=v^2/2-\mu_\oplus/r\) and specific angular momentum \(h=xv-yu\): \(e=\sqrt{1+2\mathcal E h^2/\mu_\oplus^2}\). Here it is 0.35708.

Beat 4 · Escape

Go above the 300 km escape speed.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 11500 --vInit 0 --dt 2 --maxSteps 3001 --force_law inverse_square
Read

Escape trajectory; maxSteps reached, eccentricity 1.2157, and final/maximum altitude 3.3678e+07 m. The run lasts 6000 s and has not reached infinity: “escape” labels the unbound launch state and a path that did not hit Earth during this run. There is a perigee but no apogee.

For inverse-square gravity the ideal specific energy is nonnegative at or above \(v_e\). A fast launch aimed inward can still hit Earth, so the console reports impact in that case.

Beat 5 · Make the comparison fair

Try the same speed under both laws at 300 km. Which impacts sooner?

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7500 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 15000
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7500 --force_law simplified --dt 1 --maxSteps 15000
Compare

Inverse square impacts after 0.21440 of a revolution; simplified after 0.13321. But 7500 m/s lies 2.92% below one law's circular speed (7725.7 m/s) and 7.29% below the other's (8089.9 m/s). Equal launch speeds do not mean equal deficits.

Now match the fractional deficit: 7853.53 m/s is 2.92% below the simplified circular speed here.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7853.53 --force_law simplified --dt 1 --maxSteps 15000

This time simplified completes two revolutions, with minima of 46686 m and 65796 m. Its noncircular ideal path generally precesses rather than retracing a Kepler ellipse. A comparison at one absolute speed alone cannot establish which law is more forgiving at an equal fractional deficit.

Beat 6 · Between stored samples

The plot joins samples; events usually occur between them. Start with a short run and inspect the last two radii.

python main.py
python -c "from driver_earthorbit import run_earth_orbit as run; r = run(dt=0.4, maxSteps=15000, return_diagnostics=True); import math; print([math.hypot(x,y) for x,y in zip(r[0][-2:], r[1][-2:])])"
Notice

The last raw radius is slightly inside \(R_\oplus=6378200\) m, yet the printed flight time is interpolated to the crossing, 200.44 s. Surface impact uses the straight chord's intersection with the Earth circle; revolution boundaries use interpolation in angle; altitude peaks use a local three-point parabola.

The red plot retains the first below-surface stored point. The summary stops at its estimated surface crossing. These distinctions matter when reading a close miss.

Beat 7 · Check the timestep

Launch at the full-precision 300 km circular speed and halve dt, allowing enough points for a revolution.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 4 --maxSteps 1450
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 2 --maxSteps 2890
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 5481
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 0.5 --maxSteps 10950
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237298314685 --force_law inverse_square --dt 0.25 --maxSteps 21890
Record

Revolution 1 maxima: 493240, 396820, 348470, 324250, 312130 m. Subtract 300000 m: the excess nearly halves whenever the timestep halves. The period also approaches the ideal 5431.3 s (printed: 5550.8, 5490.8, 5460.9, 5446.1, 5438.7 s).

The method advances velocity using acceleration at the start of the step, then position using the old and new velocities' average. This first-order method is not symplectic; a five-significant-digit printout cannot certify its accuracy.

Beat 8 · Two period laws

Measure one nearly circular revolution at two altitudes under each law.

python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 7725.7237 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 5481
python main.py --h0 1500000 --uInit 7113.0422 --force_law inverse_square --dt 1 --maxSteps 7009
python main.py --h0 300000 --uInit 8089.8925 --force_law simplified --dt 1 --maxSteps 5216
python main.py --h0 1500000 --uInit 8786.7150 --force_law simplified --dt 1 --maxSteps 5683
Compare

Printed periods are 5460.9 and 6988.7 s for inverse square, then 5189.8 and 5636.6 s for simplified. Inverse square predicts \(T^2/r^3=4\pi^2/\mu_\oplus\); simplified predicts \(T^2/r=4\pi^2/g\). Calculate both ratios from the printed times and radii. Their small departures from the exact constants are the numerical drift from Beat 7.

The circular speed must be recomputed at each altitude. Do not compare periods using one fixed launch speed at both heights.

Beat 9 · Know when the run stops

Each command below should stop with a single EarthOrbit: error and no plot.

python main.py --h0 -1
python main.py --dt 0
python main.py --maxSteps 1
python main.py --uInit 1e308 --dt 10

A very coarse step can also send a straight segment all the way through Earth's reference sphere even if both sampled endpoints lie outside. The program rejects that step and asks for a smaller dt. maxSteps itself is a cap on stored points, including the launch point; reaching it is ordinarily a reported outcome, not an error. The model is planar and ignores atmosphere, Earth's rotation, oblateness and third bodies.

For a negative number in scientific notation, use the equals form such as --vInit=-1e5.

Parameters

Use python main.py --help for the command-line contract. All option changes apply to one run; python main.py restores defaults. Metres, seconds and metres per second are the input units.

OptionDefaultMeaning
--h0300.0Launch altitude above the reference surface, m; nonnegative.
--uInit7900.0Initial tangential velocity, m/s.
--vInit0.0Initial radial velocity, m/s; positive outward.
--dt0.4Positive fixed timestep, s.
--maxSteps15000Stored-point cap, including launch; integer at least 2.
--force_lawsimplifiedsimplified: fixed 9.8 m/s² inward; inverse_square: \(\mu_\oplus/r^2\) inward.

--version reports the common version and source build. The four Python modules require Python, NumPy and Matplotlib; the program does not need an internet connection. Equations on this page use MathJax loaded from a public online service and need a connection to be typeset.

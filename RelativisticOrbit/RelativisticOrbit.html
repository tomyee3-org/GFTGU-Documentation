Description

RelativisticOrbit follows a small particle falling freely around a non-rotating black hole of one solar mass, and draws its orbit. Close to the hole the orbit is not an ellipse. Its closest approach, the periapsis, moves forward from one pass to the next, so that the path traces a rosette; much closer in, the particle can whirl round several times before it swings out again, or it can fall through the horizon and never come back. A second model, Newtonian gravity started from the same point with the same velocity, runs beside it for comparison.

The program integrates the orbit step by step in the particle's own time, its proper time \(\tau\), and prints a summary before it opens the plot: why the run stopped, how far the particle turned, where it found the periapsides and how far each one advanced, and how well two quantities that should stay constant did stay constant. Beneath that it prints what the constants of motion say the orbit must do, worked out exactly without integrating anything: whether it is bound, where it turns, and by how much its periapsis advances.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: a freely falling particle outside a black hole obeys a Newtonian-looking equation with one extra inward pull, \(3h^2/(c^2r^2)\) times the Newtonian one, and that pull makes each periapsis advance by an angle that the particle's energy and angular momentum fix exactly: a fraction of a degree far away, more than a whole turn close in, and, below a critical angular momentum, no return at all; the exact prediction printed beside each run and the printed drift of the conserved quantities are how you tell how far to trust the step-by-step orbit.

The same exterior Schwarzschild spacetime describes motion outside any spherical, non-rotating mass, not only a black hole. In principle, therefore, the geodesics shown here also apply outside an idealized non-rotating star. A real star, however, has a material surface that would terminate the motion if the particle struck it, whereas the present module only includes black-hole stopping conditions such as horizon crossing. As it stands, the program does not provide a way to specify a stellar radius or model impacts on a material surface.

How to judge a run. Compare the measured periapsis advance with the predicted from the constants of motion block, then inspect max |ΔE/E0| and max |Δh/h0|. The prediction solves the same orbit equation by a different route: it is an outside check on the integration, not an independent test of the physical model. Periapsides are sampled at accepted steps, so the measured advance can jump about even as the conserved-quantity drift falls.

Reading the output

termination says why integration stopped, while motion in the predicted block classifies the ideal orbit from the constants. accepted steps, proper time, and azimuthal turns describe the integrated path. periapsides found counts discrete minima; a circular path has no sequence of such events. The advance is printed only when at least two periapsides are found. The exact predicted advance and the weak-field approximation are separately labeled.

python main.py --version
python main.py --help

The first command prints the shared version/build and the second lists the parser's options; neither starts an orbit. Python 3.10 or later is required. The program runs offline; MathJax typesets this page's equations when online.

Beat 0 · The rosette

Begin with the default Schwarzschild orbit: A particle starts 15000 m from a black hole of one solar mass, moving sideways. Watch what it does.

python main.py --model schwarzschild --x_init 15000 --u_init 120000000 --dt 2e-06 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001 ^
  --max_steps 6000 --max_orbits 10

The run stops at 6000 accepted steps after 9.285146 turns. It finds six periapsides at about 15000 m and measures 2.44005 rad of advance per radial period. The independent calculation from the constants of motion predicts 2.44037 rad and an apoapsis of 23954.4 m. The horizon is about 2953.3 m and the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) about 8859.8 m, both inside this orbit.

Point to two successive closest approaches in the plot. Why can their distances agree while their directions differ? The extra Schwarzschild inward term in the orbit equation is \(3h^2/(c^2r^2)\) times the Newtonian attraction, where \(h=x_0u_0\) is conserved angular momentum per unit mass. The orbital diagram is not a flat-space map of the geometry.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 1 · Relativity switched off

Start the particle from the same place with the same velocity, but let it obey Newton's law of gravity instead.

python main.py --model newtonian --x_init 15000 --u_init 120000000

The Newtonian prediction is an ellipse between 15000 m and 65554 m with zero advance. The numerical run measures only 0.00101314 rad, a residual numerical error. Compare its 2.572195 turns in 6000 steps with Beat 0's 9.285146 turns and its tighter relativistic apoapsis.

Which output gives the prediction, and which records the integrated path? At the common start, \(3u_0^2/c^2\approx0.481\): the additional inward term is about 48% of the Newtonian one. The two models do not describe the same physical orbit merely because their input numbers match.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 3 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 2 · The advance, predicted exactly

The program does not have to integrate the orbit to know where it turns and how far its periapsis moves; the constants of motion decide both. Read the prediction for the default run of Beat 0, and then test it on an orbit that starts at its farthest point instead of its closest.

For comparison, the default settings are --u_init 120000000, --dt 2e-06, --max_steps 6000.

python main.py --u_init 1.1e8 --dt 5e-7 --max_steps 24000

The starting radius of 15000 m is now the apoapsis. The prediction gives a periapsis of 13171.1 m and an advance of 4.07222 rad; the run finds 13170.9 m and 4.07277 rad. Beat 0's predicted advance was 2.44037 rad.

Explain why going closer to the hole makes the excess angle larger. The predicted turning points and advance follow from energy and angular momentum, without taking integration steps. Compare the measured and predicted values as a numerical check; agreement does not independently prove the Schwarzschild model.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 4 and Experiment 12 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 3 · The weak field

Move the particle out to a million metres, 339 horizon radii, and give it nearly the speed for a circle there. Run it with relativity and then without.

python main.py --x_init 1e6 --u_init 1.153e7 --dt 1e-4 --max_steps 20000 --model schwarzschild
python main.py --model newtonian --x_init 1e6 --u_init 1.153e7 --dt 1e-4 --max_steps 20000

The Schwarzschild run measures 0.0280654 rad per radial period; the exact prediction is 0.0280972 rad and the weak-field approximation is 0.0279099 rad. Newtonian gravity predicts zero, while its numerical run measures 0.000315267 rad.

Compare each measured angle with its own prediction. Why is the Newtonian nonzero result an estimate of numerical contamination? The weak-field formula \(6\pi GM/(c^2p)\), with \(p\) from the predicted turning points, is an approximation; the exact advance is a different output line.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 5 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 4 · Circular orbits and the ISCO

Put the particle on a circle at \(8GM/c^2\), with exactly the circular speed there. Then start it at \(5GM/c^2\), inside the innermost stable circular orbit, with a rate 0.1% faster than the circular value.

For comparison, the default settings are --x_init 15000, --u_init 120000000, --dt 2e-06, --max_orbits 10.

python main.py --x_init 11813.0003044 --u_init 134071263.04595919 --max_orbits 4 --max_steps 8000 --dt 2e-06
python main.py --x_init 7383.12519025 --u_init 212197265.2804 --dt 1e-7 --max_orbits 12 --max_steps 40000

The first predicts circular (stable) at 11813 m and 6.28319 rad as the advance of a small radial disturbance; a perfectly circular path itself has no periapsis sequence to measure. The second begins inside the ISCO but about 0.1% above its circular proper-time rate: it travels out to 15002.2 m, predicts 24.4301 rad of advance and measures 24.4374 rad.

Look at the two plots. Why is a small disturbance mild outside the ISCO but dramatic on the unstable inner circle? Do not start a circular run by copying the six-significant-figure circular dy/dtau line; use the full input value shown here or compute it with circular_proper_time_speed().

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 6 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 5 · Inside the ISCO, and back out

Start the particle at 20000 m, moving sideways well below the circular speed, so that it falls inwards. Run it at the default settings and then with a smaller step and tighter tolerances.

python main.py --x_init 20000 --u_init 8e7 --max_steps 8000 --dt 2e-06 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
python main.py --x_init 20000 --u_init 8e7 --dt 2e-7 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 20000

The predicted orbit is bound, with periapsis 7564.95 m, inside the ISCO at 8859.8 m. The coarse run finds periapsides near 7476 m and a measured advance of 10.0873 rad against the predicted 9.40306 rad. The tighter run finds 7564.13 m and 9.40766 rad, with energy drift falling from 4.988e-03 to 5.746e-05.

Which picture deserves more trust, and what two numbers justify it? The ISCO is a limit for stable circular paths, not a wall for eccentric orbits. The closer passage is where errors in a coarse integration become most conspicuous.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 7 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 6 · The plunge

Go back to the default start at 15000 m and make it slower. At 1.044e8 m/s and at 1.045e8 m/s the particle's fate differs; run both with a small step and tight tolerances, and the second also at the default settings.

python main.py --u_init 1.044e8 --dt 1e-7 --eps1 0.005 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 12000
python main.py --u_init 1.045e8 --dt 1e-7 --eps1 0.005 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 40000
python main.py --u_init 1.045e8 --dt 2e-06 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001 --max_steps 6000

The first prediction is plunge and the run crosses the horizon after 9266 steps and 2.844796 turns. The second is predicted bound, with periapsis 7710.01 m and advance 13.5506 rad; the tight run measures 13.5564 rad. The default-step run from that same second start crosses the horizon instead, with energy drift 2.686e-02.

Why can the coarse calculation make a bound input appear to plunge? Near the unstable potential peak, a small change in conserved energy changes the fate. Read the termination, drift and prediction together; the horizon end point is interpolated between the last two accepted states and omitted from drift maxima.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 8 and Experiment 9 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 7 · How far to trust the orbit

Run the default orbit of Beat 0 again with a step half as large, a quarter as large, and a tenth as large with tighter tolerances. Each run needs more steps to cover the same proper time.

For comparison, the default settings are --dt 2e-06, --max_steps 6000.

python main.py --dt 1e-6 --max_steps 12000 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
python main.py --dt 5e-7 --max_steps 24000 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
python main.py --dt 2e-7 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 60000

The energy drifts for default, half, quarter and tenth steps are 1.573e-04, 3.928e-05, 9.875e-06 and 1.587e-06. Measured advances in the same order are 2.44005, 2.44109, 2.44014 and 2.44033 rad; the prediction is 2.44037 rad.

The drift shrinks almost fourfold on each halving, yet the measured angle first moves farther from the prediction. Why? Periapsides are identified only at accepted points; an angular sampling error of order \(\omega_p\Delta\tau/(n-1)\) can change sign. Look for stable agreement under refinement as well as a smaller conserved-quantity drift.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 10 and Experiment 12 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 8 · Escape, Mercury and rejected inputs

Four runs the program handles least well: a particle fast enough to escape, the orbit of Mercury with and without relativity, and a start inside the hole, which is rejected.

python main.py --u_init 1.8e8 --max_steps 12000 --x_init 15000 --dt 2e-06 --max_orbits 10 ^
  --model schwarzschild
python main.py --x_init 4.6e10 --u_init 58980 --dt 1e4 --max_steps 4000 --max_orbits 5 --model schwarzschild
python main.py --model newtonian --x_init 4.6e10 --u_init 58980 --dt 1e4 --max_steps 4000 --max_orbits 5
python main.py --x_init 2000 --u_init 120000000 --max_steps 6000 --dt 2e-06 --max_orbits 10 ^
  --model schwarzschild

The first prediction is escape, although the run ends at the 12000-step limit because the program has no escape detector. The Mercury input predicts 5.01831e-07 rad of relativistic advance, while the Schwarzschild and Newtonian integrations report 0.000121605 and 0.000119678 rad. The final command is expected to fail: 2000 m lies inside the horizon.

Why is neither measured Mercury angle evidence of 43 arcseconds per century? The control that should have zero advance produces nearly the same spurious angle. Use the predicted angle for this small effect; the coarse integration and accepted-step periapsis sampling cannot resolve it. The invalid input stops before a plot opens.

Try next

Check a plot marker. Run python main.py --show_periapsides and inspect the marked accepted-step minima. Compare a marker's radius with the printed periapsis range. The cursor coordinate display can help you locate it.

Run a clockwise path. Try python main.py --u_init -1.2e8. Compare its turn count, predicted fate and advance with Beat 0; what changes sign, and what should remain a magnitude?

Measure the other boundary. Use python main.py --u_init 1.0444e8 --max_steps 10 and python main.py --u_init 1.04445e8 --max_steps 10 to bracket the plunge/bound threshold from the prediction alone. Ten steps do not trace the eventual fate.

Suggested Experiments

The experiments are ranked from introductory to advanced, and each is cited by the beat it extends. Each command is run from the program's folder. When choosing a step for a new orbit, a useful first estimate for \(N\) steps per turn at radius \(r\) is \(\Delta\tau\sim2\pi r/(|u_0|N)\); the acceleration-change and corrector tests shorten trial steps when needed.

1 · Introductory — Mark the Periapsides

Run the default with the periapsides marked. Check that successive marks are equally far from the hole and equally spaced in angle, and estimate the angle between neighbouring marks from the plot. Compare the angle accumulated between successive periapsides with the printed advance plus a whole turn; the plotted directions themselves show that angle modulo a whole turn.

python main.py --show_periapsides

2 · Introductory — Count the Turns

Stop the default orbit after three turns. How many periapsides does it find, and why not three? Read azimuthal turns: the run stops at the first step that completes three turns, so the count slightly exceeds 3.

python main.py --max_orbits 3 --show_periapsides

3 · Introductory — Same Start, Two Laws

Run both models for three turns from the same start and compare the plots and the apoapsis radii. Which orbit closes, and which one is pulled in more tightly?

python main.py --max_orbits 3 --max_steps 7000
python main.py --model newtonian --max_orbits 3 --max_steps 7000

4 · Intermediate — Which Turning Point?

Start just below and just above the circular value at 15000 m, 1.12051e+08 m/s. In each run, read from the motion line whether the start is the periapsis or the apoapsis, and compare the measured and predicted advances. Why do both runs have nearly the same advance, although their starts are on opposite sides of the circular value?

python main.py --u_init 1.12e8
python main.py --u_init 1.125e8

5 · Intermediate — How Weak Is Weak?

Only the prediction is needed here, so ten steps are enough. For each radius, divide apsidal advance by weak-field 6piGM/(c^2 p) and compare the ratio minus 1 with \((18+e^2)/4\) times \(GM/(c^2p)\), Beat 3. How far out must an orbit be for the weak-field formula in Beat 3 to agree with the exact prediction to 1%?

python main.py --x_init 1e5 --u_init 3.8e7 --max_steps 10
python main.py --x_init 1e7 --u_init 3.7e6 --max_steps 10
python main.py --x_init 1e9 --u_init 3.7e5 --max_steps 10

6 · Intermediate Programming — Circular Speeds and Stability

Run this script from the program's folder. It prints each radius and the full circular proper-time rate \(u_{\rm circ}=\sqrt{GM/(r-3GM/c^2)}\) (Beat 4), which can be copied into --x_init and --u_init, and the predicted kind of orbit at radii from 4.5 to 10 times \(GM/c^2\), started exactly at the circular value. Where does the kind change from unstable to stable? Then start one of the stable circles 0.1% faster than circular, with a small step, and compare its measured advance with the printed prediction.

import physics_relativistic_orbit as physics

m = physics.GM_SUN / physics.C2
for factor in (4.5, 5, 6.5, 8, 10):
    r = factor * m
    u = physics.circular_proper_time_speed(r)
    prediction = physics.predict_orbit(r, u)
    print(factor, repr(r), repr(u), prediction.kind, prediction.apsidal_advance)

7 · Intermediate to Advanced — The Deepest Return

From 20000 m, lower --u_init below 8e7 in small steps, keeping the tight settings of Beat 5. The first command still returns and the second plunges. Narrow the interval between them and record the smallest periapsis that still returns. Compare it with the ISCO and with the unstable potential maximum \(r_- = h^2[1-\sqrt{1-12(GM/(ch))^2}]/(2GM)\), where \(h=x_0u_0\) must be recalculated for each launch. This maximum exists for \(|h|>\sqrt{12}\,GM/c\); its limiting value is the ISCO at equality.

python main.py --x_init 20000 --u_init 7.99e7 --dt 2e-7 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 20000
python main.py --x_init 20000 --u_init 7.95e7 --dt 2e-7 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 20000

8 · Advanced — Toward the Photon Orbit

At 5000 m, just outside the photon orbit, the circular value is 4.82471e+08 m/s, well above the speed of light, because it is a proper-time rate. Start well below it and then just above it. Why does the faster start escape rather than stay bound, Compare both predictions with the energy-based escape discussion in Beat 8. A rate dy/dτ above c is not a locally measured speed above c.

python main.py --x_init 5000 --u_init 2e8 --dt 1e-7 --max_steps 10000
python main.py --x_init 5000 --u_init 4.83e8 --dt 1e-8 --max_steps 20000

9 · Advanced — Hunt the Zoom-Whirl Boundary

Between 1.044e8 and 1.045e8 m/s at 15000 m the orbit changes from a plunge to a bound zoom-whirl. Use the motion line, which needs only ten steps, to narrow the boundary; then run a start just above it with the tight settings of Beat 6 and many steps, and count the whirls.

python main.py --u_init 1.0444e8 --max_steps 10
python main.py --u_init 1.04445e8 --max_steps 10

10 · Advanced — Convergence Inside the ISCO

Repeat the orbit of Beat 5 with smaller steps and tighter tolerances. Check that the periapsis radius and the advance approach the predicted 7564.95 m and 9.40306 rad and that the energy drift falls, and compare the remaining error in the advance with the angular sampling scale \(\omega_p\Delta\tau/(n-1)\) in Beat 7, where \(\omega_p=|h|/r_p^2\) and \(n\) is the number of detected periapsides.

python main.py --x_init 20000 --u_init 8e7 --dt 1e-7 --eps1 0.005 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 40000

11 · Advanced — Mercury's 43 Arcseconds

Read the predicted advance for Mercury's orbit from ten steps. Convert it to arcseconds per century, using 415 orbits per century and 206265 arcseconds per radian, and compare it with the 43 arcseconds per century that Einstein explained in 1915. Why can the prediction do what the integration of Beat 8 cannot?

python main.py --x_init 4.6e10 --u_init 58980 --max_steps 10

12 · Advanced Programming — Read the Result and the Prediction

Every number in the summary is available from the driver's result and from predict_orbit(). Run this script from the program's folder and compare its numbers with Beat 0; then extend it, for example to print the angle of each periapsis or the radius at every step.

import physics_relativistic_orbit as physics
from driver_relativistic_orbit import RelativisticOrbitParams, integrate_relativistic_orbit

params = RelativisticOrbitParams(x_init=1.5e4, u_init=1.2e8, dt=2e-6,
                                 max_steps=6000, max_orbits=10, eps1=0.05, eps2=1e-4)
result = integrate_relativistic_orbit(params)
prediction = physics.predict_orbit(params.x_init, params.u_init)
print(len(result.periapsis_radius), result.mean_periapsis_advance, prediction.apsidal_advance)

Command-line parameters

Options override defaults for one run. --show_isco and --show_periapsides only change the picture; --no-show_isco and --no-show_periapsides undo those switches. Negative values for --u_init are allowed.

ParameterDefaultDescription
--x_init15000Starting areal radius \(x_0\) in m, on the \(+x\) axis. It must be positive, and outside the horizon, 2953.3 m, in Schwarzschild mode.
--u_init1.2e8Starting \(dy/d\tau\), \(u_0\), in m/s; any finite value, including zero or a negative value. It is a proper-time rate, not a measured speed.
--dt2e-6Largest proper-time step \(\Delta\tau_{\max}\) in s.
--max_steps6000Largest number of accepted steps; a run can stop here before it reaches --max_orbits.
--max_orbits10Number of turns, after which the run stops; the last step can overshoot it slightly.
--eps10.05Largest fractional change of the acceleration in one step, \(\varepsilon_1\); strictly between 0 and 1.
--eps21e-4Convergence threshold \(\varepsilon_2\) of the corrector; strictly between 0 and --eps1.
--modelschwarzschildschwarzschild uses relativistic gravity; newtonian uses Newtonian gravity from the same start.
--show_iscoonDraw the ISCO circle at \(6GM/c^2\) in Schwarzschild mode; --no-show_isco hides it.
--show_periapsidesoffMark the periapsides found with an x; --no-show_periapsides hides them.

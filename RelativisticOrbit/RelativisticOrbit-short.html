Start here

RelativisticOrbit draws a test particle's orbit around a fixed, non-rotating, one-solar-mass black hole. The second model, newtonian, starts from the same diagram position and sideways rate so you can compare the trajectories. Every run prints its numerical result and a prediction from the constants of motion before opening the plot.

Run commands from the folder containing main.py; close each plot before starting the next. All radii are metres and times are seconds. The plot uses areal radius and angle as flat diagram coordinates; --u_init is dy/dtau, a proper-time coordinate rate, not a speed measured by a nearby observer. The central mass is held fixed.

How to judge a run. Compare the measured periapsis advance with the predicted from the constants of motion block, then inspect max |ΔE/E0| and max |Δh/h0|. The prediction solves the same orbit equation by a different route: it is an outside check on the integration, not an independent test of the physical model. Periapsides are sampled at accepted steps, so the measured advance can jump about even as the conserved-quantity drift falls.

Beat 0 · Watch a rosette

Run. Begin with the default Schwarzschild orbit.

python main.py --model schwarzschild --x_init 15000 --u_init 120000000 --dt 2e-06 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001 \
  --max_steps 6000 --max_orbits 10

Observe. The run stops at 6000 accepted steps after 9.285146 turns. It finds six periapsides at about 15000 m and measures 2.44005 rad of advance per radial period. The independent calculation from the constants of motion predicts 2.44037 rad and an apoapsis of 23954.4 m. The horizon is about 2953.3 m and the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) about 8859.8 m, both inside this orbit.

Your turn. Point to two successive closest approaches in the plot. Why can their distances agree while their directions differ? The extra Schwarzschild inward term in the orbit equation is \(3h^2/(c^2r^2)\) times the Newtonian attraction, where \(h=x_0u_0\) is conserved angular momentum per unit mass. The orbital diagram is not a flat-space map of the geometry.

Beat 1 · Switch off the extra pull

Run. Keep the initial data and change only the force law.

python main.py --model newtonian --x_init 15000 --u_init 120000000

Observe. The Newtonian prediction is an ellipse between 15000 m and 65554 m with zero advance. The numerical run measures only 0.00101314 rad, a residual numerical error. Compare its 2.572195 turns in 6000 steps with Beat 0's 9.285146 turns and its tighter relativistic apoapsis.

Your turn. Which output gives the prediction, and which records the integrated path? At the common start, \(3u_0^2/c^2\approx0.481\): the additional inward term is about 48% of the Newtonian one. The two models do not describe the same physical orbit merely because their input numbers match.

Beat 2 · Read the prediction first

Run. Lower the starting sideways rate and tighten the step.

For comparison, the default settings are --u_init 120000000, --dt 2e-06, --max_steps 6000.

python main.py --u_init 1.1e8 --dt 5e-7 --max_steps 24000

Observe. The starting radius of 15000 m is now the apoapsis. The prediction gives a periapsis of 13171.1 m and an advance of 4.07222 rad; the run finds 13170.9 m and 4.07277 rad. Beat 0's predicted advance was 2.44037 rad.

Your turn. Explain why going closer to the hole makes the excess angle larger. The predicted turning points and advance follow from energy and angular momentum, without taking integration steps. Compare the measured and predicted values as a numerical check; agreement does not independently prove the Schwarzschild model.

Beat 3 · Move to a weak field

Run. Compare the two models farther from the mass.

python main.py --x_init 1e6 --u_init 1.153e7 --dt 1e-4 --max_steps 20000 --model schwarzschild
python main.py --model newtonian --x_init 1e6 --u_init 1.153e7 --dt 1e-4 --max_steps 20000

Observe. The Schwarzschild run measures 0.0280654 rad per radial period; the exact prediction is 0.0280972 rad and the weak-field approximation is 0.0279099 rad. Newtonian gravity predicts zero, while its numerical run measures 0.000315267 rad.

Your turn. Compare each measured angle with its own prediction. Why is the Newtonian nonzero result an estimate of numerical contamination? The weak-field formula \(6\pi GM/(c^2p)\), with \(p\) from the predicted turning points, is an approximation; the exact advance is a different output line.

Beat 4 · Disturb two circles

Run. Compare a stable circle at \(8GM/c^2\) with a near-circle at \(5GM/c^2\).

For comparison, the default settings are --x_init 15000, --u_init 120000000, --dt 2e-06, --max_orbits 10.

python main.py --x_init 11813.0003044 --u_init 134071263.04595919 --max_orbits 4 --max_steps 8000 --dt 2e-06
python main.py --x_init 7383.12519025 --u_init 212197265.2804 --dt 1e-7 --max_orbits 12 --max_steps 40000

Observe. The first predicts circular (stable) at 11813 m and 6.28319 rad as the advance of a small radial disturbance; a perfectly circular path itself has no periapsis sequence to measure. The second begins inside the ISCO but about 0.1% above its circular proper-time rate: it travels out to 15002.2 m, predicts 24.4301 rad of advance and measures 24.4374 rad.

Your turn. Look at the two plots. Why is a small disturbance mild outside the ISCO but dramatic on the unstable inner circle? Do not start a circular run by copying the six-significant-figure circular dy/dtau line; use the full input value shown here or compute it with circular_proper_time_speed().

Beat 5 · Cross the inner circular boundary and return

Run. Start at 20000 m and compare the default numerical settings with a tighter integration.

python main.py --x_init 20000 --u_init 8e7 --max_steps 8000 --dt 2e-06 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
python main.py --x_init 20000 --u_init 8e7 --dt 2e-7 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 20000

Observe. The predicted orbit is bound, with periapsis 7564.95 m, inside the ISCO at 8859.8 m. The coarse run finds periapsides near 7476 m and a measured advance of 10.0873 rad against the predicted 9.40306 rad. The tighter run finds 7564.13 m and 9.40766 rad, with energy drift falling from 4.988e-03 to 5.746e-05.

Your turn. Which picture deserves more trust, and what two numbers justify it? The ISCO is a limit for stable circular paths, not a wall for eccentric orbits. The closer passage is where errors in a coarse integration become most conspicuous.

Beat 6 · Separate a plunge from a whirl

Run. Test two nearby initial rates with tight settings, then repeat the second at default settings.

python main.py --u_init 1.044e8 --dt 1e-7 --eps1 0.005 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 12000
python main.py --u_init 1.045e8 --dt 1e-7 --eps1 0.005 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 40000
python main.py --u_init 1.045e8 --dt 2e-06 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001 --max_steps 6000

Observe. The first prediction is plunge and the run crosses the horizon after 9266 steps and 2.844796 turns. The second is predicted bound, with periapsis 7710.01 m and advance 13.5506 rad; the tight run measures 13.5564 rad. The default-step run from that same second start crosses the horizon instead, with energy drift 2.686e-02.

Your turn. Why can the coarse calculation make a bound input appear to plunge? Near the unstable potential peak, a small change in conserved energy changes the fate. Read the termination, drift and prediction together; the horizon end point is interpolated between the last two accepted states and omitted from drift maxima.

Beat 7 · Refine before trusting a digit

Run. Halve the default maximum step twice, then run at one tenth of it with tighter tolerances.

For comparison, the default settings are --dt 2e-06, --max_steps 6000.

python main.py --dt 1e-6 --max_steps 12000 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
python main.py --dt 5e-7 --max_steps 24000 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
python main.py --dt 2e-7 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6 --max_steps 60000

Observe. The energy drifts for default, half, quarter and tenth steps are 1.573e-04, 3.928e-05, 9.875e-06 and 1.587e-06. Measured advances in the same order are 2.44005, 2.44109, 2.44014 and 2.44033 rad; the prediction is 2.44037 rad.

Your turn. The drift shrinks almost fourfold on each halving, yet the measured angle first moves farther from the prediction. Why? Periapsides are identified only at accepted points; an angular sampling error of order \(\omega_p\Delta\tau/(n-1)\) can change sign. Look for stable agreement under refinement as well as a smaller conserved-quantity drift.

Beat 8 · Escape, Mercury and rejected inputs

Run. Watch escape, compare a tiny predicted Mercury advance with two numerical readings, then try an invalid starting radius.

python main.py --u_init 1.8e8 --max_steps 12000 --x_init 15000 --dt 2e-06 --max_orbits 10 \
  --model schwarzschild
python main.py --x_init 4.6e10 --u_init 58980 --dt 1e4 --max_steps 4000 --max_orbits 5 --model schwarzschild
python main.py --model newtonian --x_init 4.6e10 --u_init 58980 --dt 1e4 --max_steps 4000 --max_orbits 5
python main.py --x_init 2000 --u_init 120000000 --max_steps 6000 --dt 2e-06 --max_orbits 10 \
  --model schwarzschild

Observe. The first prediction is escape, although the run ends at the 12000-step limit because the program has no escape detector. The Mercury input predicts 5.01831e-07 rad of relativistic advance, while the Schwarzschild and Newtonian integrations report 0.000121605 and 0.000119678 rad. The final command is expected to fail: 2000 m lies inside the horizon.

Your turn. Why is neither measured Mercury angle evidence of 43 arcseconds per century? The control that should have zero advance produces nearly the same spurious angle. Use the predicted angle for this small effect; the coarse integration and accepted-step periapsis sampling cannot resolve it. The invalid input stops before a plot opens.

Try next

Check a plot marker. Run python main.py --show_periapsides and inspect the marked accepted-step minima. Compare a marker's radius with the printed periapsis range. The cursor coordinate display can help you locate it.

Run a clockwise path. Try python main.py --u_init -1.2e8. Compare its turn count, predicted fate and advance with Beat 0; what changes sign, and what should remain a magnitude?

Measure the other boundary. Use python main.py --u_init 1.0444e8 --max_steps 10 and python main.py --u_init 1.04445e8 --max_steps 10 to bracket the plunge/bound threshold from the prediction alone. Ten steps do not trace the eventual fate.

Command-line parameters

Options override defaults for one run. --show_isco and --show_periapsides only change the picture; --no-show_isco and --no-show_periapsides undo those switches. Negative values for --u_init are allowed.

ParameterDefaultDescription
--x_init15000Starting areal radius \(x_0\) in m, on the \(+x\) axis. It must be positive, and outside the horizon, 2953.3 m, in Schwarzschild mode.
--u_init1.2e8Starting \(dy/d\tau\), \(u_0\), in m/s; any finite value, including zero or a negative value. It is a proper-time rate, not a measured speed.
--dt2e-6Largest proper-time step \(\Delta\tau_{\max}\) in s.
--max_steps6000Largest number of accepted steps; a run can stop here before it reaches --max_orbits.
--max_orbits10Number of turns, after which the run stops; the last step can overshoot it slightly.
--eps10.05Largest fractional change of the acceleration in one step, \(\varepsilon_1\); strictly between 0 and 1.
--eps21e-4Convergence threshold \(\varepsilon_2\) of the corrector; strictly between 0 and --eps1.
--modelschwarzschildschwarzschild uses relativistic gravity; newtonian uses Newtonian gravity from the same start.
--show_iscoonDraw the ISCO circle at \(6GM/c^2\) in Schwarzschild mode; --no-show_isco hides it.
--show_periapsidesoffMark the periapsides found with an x; --no-show_periapsides hides them.

Reading the output

termination says why integration stopped, while motion in the predicted block classifies the ideal orbit from the constants. accepted steps, proper time, and azimuthal turns describe the integrated path. periapsides found counts discrete minima; a circular path has no sequence of such events. The advance is printed only when at least two periapsides are found. The exact predicted advance and the weak-field approximation are separately labeled.

python main.py --version
python main.py --help

The first command prints the shared version/build and the second lists the parser's options; neither starts an orbit. Python 3.10 or later is required. The program runs offline; MathJax typesets this page's equations when online.

Description

Atmosphere models the vertical pressure, density, and temperature structure of a planetary atmosphere in hydrostatic equilibrium. Starting from a measured sea-level (or reference-level) pressure and a user-supplied temperature profile, the program integrates upward in altitude until the numerical integration reaches its zero-pressure boundary, then plots whichever of the three atmospheric quantities the user selects. This zero-pressure boundary is a numerical stopping condition for the simplified model, not a sharply defined physical surface in a real atmosphere.

It is suitable for modeling Earth's atmosphere, comparing planetary atmospheres across the Solar System, and exploring how scale height depends on gravity and mean molecular mass.

Every run also prints pressure, density, (p/T), and temperature at every supplied altitude checkpoint, independently of the quantity selected for the plot. Numerical table entries use five significant figures.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: pressure holds up the weight of the air above, the gas law turns pressure into density, and the ratio of the two is a length, the scale height, that temperature, gravity and mean molecular mass set between them. Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument, a command whose printed table or figure illustrates that argument, and then the equation that the printed numbers follow from.

Textbook reference: Bernard Schutz, Gravity from the Ground Up, Cambridge University Press (2003), Chapter 7 — Investigations 7.1 and 7.3.
Default Earth profile: The lower-atmosphere temperatures use U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 values. The thermospheric portion is a representative NRLMSIS 2.0 thermospheric profile through 500 km; see Parameters for the run conditions and limitations.
What this program is, and what it does not model. Atmosphere is a teaching model, not a weather or research code. Gravity and the mean molecular mass are held constant with height; the air is an ideal gas; and the temperature is not computed: you supply it at chosen altitudes and the program interpolates. The pressure is found by a first-order (Euler) step that accumulates error with height, and the top of the atmosphere is where that calculation stops, not a physical surface. Use it for scale heights, ratios and the way pressure and density respond to gravity, mean molecular mass and temperature, rather than for absolute values high in the atmosphere. Beat 6 measures how well, including for profiles with very cold layers.

How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation that the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equation at work, not independent evidence that the equation is right. Where a beat compares the program with something outside it, such as the U.S. Standard Atmosphere in Beat 6 or published Venus values in Beat 5, it says so.

Every beat uses the same program and the same printed table. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Beats 0 and 1 use the default Earth atmosphere, Beat 2 an isothermal one, Beat 3 changes one dial at a time, Beat 4 changes the temperature profile, Beat 5 moves to other planets, and Beats 6 and 7 examine the numerical method and the top of the model.

Every run prints the checkpoint table and then a Numerical top line giving the last stored altitude and temperature (a stopping rule, not a physical top). A plot window then opens; close the window to end the program. Each beat names the columns and rows to look at. In several beats the table is what to study and the plot can be left until later.

Give lists with commas and no brackets. A list that contains spaces must be put in quotation marks, and a list may begin with a negative altitude, as in --h_points -1000,0,10000.

What is integrated and what is prescribed. Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag saying what kind of statement it is. A green ODE tag marks a differential equation that the program integrates. A blue DERIVED tag marks an algebraic result that follows from stated assumptions; the ideal gas law, Eq. (7.2), is here a consequence of treating the air as an ideal gas of one mean molecular mass. A grey PRESCRIPTION tag marks a rule the program adopts, and a violet CLOSURE tag marks an assumption that stands in for physics the model does not have. The numerical method itself, the Euler step of Eq. (7.7) under Algorithm, is an algorithm rather than physics and is tagged only in the Algorithm section. Equation (7.6) is a closure and Eq. (7.1) is a physical law, and the tags are there so that the two are never mistaken for each other.

Beat 0 · Pressure is the weight of the air above you

Stand at sea level and the whole column of air above you, all the way to space, presses down on every square meter of ground. Climb, and there is less air overhead, so the pressure is smaller. That is the entire idea of atmospheric structure: the pressure at any height is the weight of the air above it. Ask the program only to show what that looks like for the Earth.

python main.py

Look at the first two rows of the checkpoint table, altitudes 0 and 11019 m, and at the pressure curve in the plot window. Ignore the other columns for now.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the surface pressure, 1.013e+05 Pa, and divide it by g = 9.81 m s−2: about 10,300 kg. That is the mass of the air standing on each square meter of ground, roughly ten tonnes. Then read the pressure at 11019 m, the top of the lowest layer: 22499 Pa, which is 22% of the surface value, so about 78% of the atmosphere's mass lies below that height. Then look at the plot: the curve is steep near the ground and nearly flat above 50 km. Write down, before reading on, why the pressure should change fastest where the air is densest.

Then the answer, which is a weighing. Climb through a thin layer of thickness \(dh\). Per unit area that layer holds a mass \(\rho\,dh\), which weighs \(g\rho\,dh\), and the pressure you leave behind is smaller by exactly that weight.

Hydrostatic equilibrium ODE
\[ \frac{dp}{dh} = -g\,\rho \tag{7.1} \]

Here \(p\) is pressure (Pa), \(h\) is altitude (m), \(g\) is the local gravitational acceleration (m s−2), taken as constant with height in this program, and \(\rho\) is the mass density (kg m−3). The negative sign says that pressure decreases with increasing altitude, and the slope is steepest where \(\rho\) is largest. Beat 6 measures what holding \(g\) constant costs.

Equation (7.1) cannot be solved by itself, because it has two unknowns, \(p\) and \(\rho\). Beat 1 supplies the missing relation.

Beat 1 · Density is not free: the gas law supplies it

Equation (7.1) needs \(\rho\) at every height, and \(\rho\) is not an independent input. Air is a gas, and for a gas the density follows from the pressure and the temperature. Run the same atmosphere again, this time plotting the density.

python main.py --output_type density

Look at the density plot and, in the table, the density and p/T columns. The table is the same as in Beat 0; only the plot has changed.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the surface density the program prints, 1.2249 kg m−3, which is within 0.01% of the 1.2250 kg m−3 of the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976. Then divide the density by p/T in two different rows: 1.2249 / 351.55 at the surface and 4.3785e-07 / 0.00012566 at 100 km both give 3.484×10−3 (kg K J−1), and so does every other row, whatever the altitude. Then compare the rows at 71802 m and 86000 m: the pressure falls by a factor of 11.3 but the density falls only by a factor of 9.9, and the temperature ratio between the two rows is 1.148. Say in one sentence why the density falls more slowly than the pressure.

Then the relation you have just measured. For an ideal gas, density is proportional to pressure divided by temperature, and the constant is fixed by the mean molecular mass of the gas.

Ideal gas law DERIVED
\[ \rho = \frac{p\,\mu\,m_u}{k_B\,T} \tag{7.2} \]

Here \(\mu\) is the mean molecular mass of the gas in atomic mass units (dimensionless as a number, e.g. 28.97 for dry air), \(m_u = 1.66053906660\times10^{-27}\;\text{kg}\) is the atomic mass unit, \(k_B = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\;\text{J K}^{-1}\) is the Boltzmann constant, and \(T\) is the temperature in kelvin. The constant you measured is \(\mu m_u/k_B = 3.484\times10^{-3}\) in these units, so the p/T column is density with the constant factor \(\mu m_u/k_B\) stripped off, not density in other units. At a fixed pressure, colder air is denser, which is the answer to the last task.

Substituting Eq. (7.2) into Eq. (7.1) eliminates the density and gives the complete governing equation for atmospheric structure:

Governing equation ODE
\[ \frac{dp}{dh} = -\frac{g\,\mu\,m_u}{k_B\,T(h)}\,p \tag{7.3} \]

Equation (7.3) is a single first-order equation for \(p(h)\). All that remains is to say what \(T(h)\) is, and the program does not compute it: it is an input. Beat 2 solves the equation in the simplest case.

Beat 2 · One number sets the size of an atmosphere: the scale height

If the temperature is the same at every height, Equation (7.3) has a simple solution: over any equal step upward the pressure is multiplied by the same factor. Test that with an isothermal atmosphere, sampled every 8 km.

python main.py --h_points 0,8000,16000,24000,32000 --T_points 288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15

Look at the pressure column and the five rows of the table. The temperature is 288.15 K in every row. Above 32 km the program switches to the closure of Beat 7, so read only the table and ignore the top of the curve.

Three tasks, in order. First, divide each pressure by the one above it: 39124 / 1.013e5, 15110 / 39124, 5835.9 / 15110 and 2253.9 / 5835.9 all equal 0.3862. It is the same factor for every 8 km, including the last step, which ends at the last supplied altitude. Then find the height over which the pressure falls by a factor of e: with a factor of 0.3862 per 8000 m, \(\ln(1/0.3862)\) = 0.951 per 8000 m, so the pressure falls by e in 8000 / 0.951 = about 8410 m. Then compute \(p_0/(g\rho_0)\) from the first row of the same table: 1.013e5 / (9.81 × 1.2249) = 8430 m. The two lengths agree to 0.25%. They are two readings of the same model output, not independent checks: the program computes its density from the same gas law and sets its own step from this length, so the agreement shows that \(H\) can be read from a printed table, not that the model is right.

Then the length itself. It is called the scale height \(H\), and the pressure in an isothermal atmosphere is

Barometric formula, isothermal atmosphere DERIVED
\[ p = p_0\,e^{-h/H} \]
Scale height DERIVED
\[ H = \frac{p_0}{g\,\rho_0} = \frac{k_B\,T_0}{g\,\mu\,m_u} \tag{7.4} \]

where \(\rho_0\) is the density computed from Eq. (7.2) at the surface pressure \(p_0\) and surface temperature \(T_0\). For the default Earth parameters at 288.15 K, \(H = 8.430\) km, usually quoted as about 8.4 km. The scale height is the yardstick of the whole program. It also sets the program's own step: the first altitude step is \(\Delta h = H_{\min}/200\), where \(H_{\min}\) is the scale height at the coldest temperature of the profile, which for this isothermal run is just \(H/200\), as Beat 6 explains.

A quantity that falls by the same factor over equal heights plots as a straight line when its logarithm is plotted. Experiment 2 asks you to make that plot.

Beat 3 · What the scale height depends on, and what it does not

Equation (7.4) names three things that set the scale height: the temperature, the gravity and the mean molecular mass. It does not contain the surface pressure. That is a prediction, and this beat tests it, one dial at a time, with the default Earth temperature profile in every run.

python main.py --mu 18
python main.py --mu 44
python main.py --g_accel 19.62
python main.py --p0 1.013e6

Look at the pressure column, rows 0 and 11019 m, in each run. Compare each run with the default run of Beat 0, where the pressure at 11019 m is 22499 Pa out of 1.013e+05 Pa at the ground.

Three tasks, in order. First, compute the fraction \(p(11019\ \text{m})/p_0\) for each run. The default gives 0.2221; --mu 18 gives 39782 / 1.013e5 = 0.393; --mu 44 gives 10304 / 1.013e5 = 0.102; --g_accel 19.62 gives 4994.3 / 1.013e5 = 0.0493; and --p0 1.013e6 gives 2.2499e+05 / 1.013e6 = 0.2221 again. Then predict two of them before you look: the natural logarithm of the default fraction is −1.505, and Eq. (7.4) says it is proportional to \(\mu g\), so for \(\mu = 44\) you expect \(\exp(-1.505\times 44/28.97) = 0.102\), and doubling \(g\) should square the fraction, \(0.2221^2 = 0.0493\). Both agree with the run to 0.1%. Then say which of the four dials changes the shape of the atmosphere and which one only rescales it.

Then the reading of Eq. (7.4) that the runs support:

Scale height as a proportionality (from Eq. 7.4) DERIVED
\[ H \;\propto\; \frac{T}{\mu\,g} \]

At 288.15 K the runs above correspond to \(H\) = 8.430 km (default), 13.57 km (\(\mu\) = 18), 5.550 km (\(\mu\) = 44) and 4.215 km (\(g\) = 19.62 m s−2). A light gas or weak gravity gives a tall, extended atmosphere; a heavy gas or strong gravity gives a shallow one. At fixed gravity, the surface pressure \(p_0\) multiplies every pressure by the same factor and so changes no ratio of pressures: it sets the pressure normalization and, together with \(g\), the mass of air above each square meter, \(p_0/g\) (Beat 0), but not how tall the atmosphere is. Comparing different planets, where \(g\) differs, needs both numbers, as Beat 5 shows.

Beat 4 · Real air is not isothermal

The real atmosphere is not at one temperature: solar heating, convection and radiation give it a layered structure. It cools with height in the troposphere, warms in the stratosphere where ozone absorbs sunlight, cools again in the mesosphere, and warms strongly in the thermosphere. The program does not compute any of this. You give it temperatures at chosen altitudes, and it joins them with straight lines. Equation (7.3) is then the same equation with a scale height that changes with height.

python main.py --output_type temperature
python main.py --h_points 0,50000,100000 --T_points 288,200,350

In the first run look at the temperature plot and the temperature column. In the second run look at the pressure, density and temperature columns.

Three tasks, in order. First, on the temperature plot of the first run find the four layers in order: cooling to 216.65 K at 11019 m, warming to 270.65 K at 47–51 km, cooling to 186.95 K at 86 km, then the steep rise to 1240 K. Then compare the local scale heights from Eq. (7.4): at 216.65 K, \(H\) = 6.34 km, and at 1240 K, \(H\) = 36.3 km. The pressure falls by a factor of 4.50 over the first 11 km, but only by a factor of about 4700 from 200 km (6.7556e-05 Pa) to 500 km (1.437e-08 Pa), so per kilometer it falls nearly five times more slowly in the hot thermosphere. Then turn to the second run, a cold layer below a warm one. Between 50 km and 100 km the pressure falls by a factor of 595 and the density falls by a factor of 1042. Show that the second factor is the first multiplied by the temperature ratio 350 / 200 = 1.75.

Then the rule the program uses between two supplied points:

Piecewise-linear temperature profile PRESCRIPTION
\[ T(h) = T_{i-1} + \frac{T_i - T_{i-1}}{h_i - h_{i-1}}\,(h - h_{i-1}), \qquad h_{i-1} \le h < h_i \tag{7.5} \]

That is, temperature is linearly interpolated between adjacent supplied altitudes. Below the first supplied point the temperature is held constant at \(T_0\), and above the last one the program uses the closure of Beat 7. The default Earth profile combines U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 values through about 86 km with a representative NRLMSIS 2.0 thermospheric profile to 500 km; the Parameters section gives the sources and their limits.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 1 and Experiment 6 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 5 · Other worlds, same equations

Nothing in Eqs. (7.1) to (7.5) is specific to the Earth. Change \(g\), \(\mu\), \(p_0\) and the temperature list and the same program describes another planet. Which of these worlds has the thickest atmosphere depends on what you mean by thick: how much air stands above each square meter, or how tall the atmosphere is.

python main.py --planet_name Mars --g_accel 3.72 --mu 44 --p0 610 \
  --h_points 0,10000,25000,45000 --T_points 210,170,145,130

python main.py --planet_name Venus --g_accel 8.87 --mu 44 --p0 9.2e6 \
  --h_points 0,10000,20000,30000,40000,50000,60000 \
  --T_points 735,658,579,495,416,348,263

python main.py --planet_name Jupiter --g_accel 24.8 --mu 2.2 --p0 1e5 \
  --h_points 0,20000,50000,100000 --T_points 165,130,110,150

Look at the surface row and the pressure and density columns of each table. The plots are the same kind of curve as for the Earth and can be left for later.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the surface densities: Mars 0.015372, Venus 66.24 and Jupiter 0.16036 kg m−3, against 1.2249 for the Earth. Then compute two lengths and a mass for each world. The base scale height \(p_0/(g\rho_0)\) is: Mars 610 / (3.72 × 0.015372) = 10.7 km, Venus 9.2e6 / (8.87 × 66.24) = 15.7 km, Jupiter 1e5 / (24.8 × 0.16036) = 25.1 km, against the Earth's 8.4 km; Jupiter's is the largest of the four even though its gravity is the strongest, because its mean molecular mass is so small. The mass of air above each square meter, \(p_0/g\), is: Mars 164 kg, Venus 1.04×106 kg, Jupiter 4032 kg, against the Earth's 10,300 kg. Notice that Mars has about 1/166 of the Earth's surface pressure but about 1/63 of its column mass, because its gravity is weaker, and that it has a taller scale height than the Earth. Then, for Venus only, compare the model with published values, which are 4.80 MPa at 10 km and 23.9 kPa at 60 km: the run gives 4.6835e+06 Pa (2% low) and 20022 Pa (16% low).

Then the point that the equations make, with no new equation needed: \(H \propto T/(\mu g)\), so how tall an atmosphere is comes from its temperature, gravity and molecular mass, while the surface pressure and gravity together, as \(p_0/g\), say how much air stands above each square meter. Mars has little air but a large scale height; Venus has about a hundred times the Earth's column mass and a scale height less than twice the Earth's. Total atmospheric mass needs the planet's surface area as well, which this program does not use.

Where the numbers come from. The Venus temperatures are taken from the table of temperature and pressure against altitude in the Wikipedia article Atmosphere of Venus (385 °C at 10 km, 306 °C at 20 km, and so on to −10 °C at 60 km), rounded and converted to kelvin; the published pressures quoted above (47.39 atm and 0.2357 atm, converted to pascals) come from the same table. That table is a secondary compilation, so treat the comparison as a rough check, not as a comparison with a reference atmosphere. The Mars and Jupiter profiles are round-number illustrations, not measured profiles. The 16% deficit for Venus at 60 km comes partly from the rounded inputs (a surface pressure of 9.2 MPa and \(\mu\) = 44) and partly from holding the gravity and the composition constant and from the first-order step, the effects that Beat 6 examines for the Earth.
Jupiter is a local model. Jupiter has no solid surface, so the run takes the 1-bar level as the reference and follows only 100 km of the atmosphere above it. The program assumes constant gravity and a fixed-composition ideal gas; it is not a model of the whole planet or of its interior.

Beat 6 · How far to trust the numbers

Every number so far has come from a first-order finite-step calculation, two idealizations (constant gravity and constant composition) and inputs rounded to a few figures. The isothermal atmosphere of Beat 2 gives you something rare: an exact answer to compare with. This beat measures the error there, and then compares the default Earth run with the standard atmosphere it is meant to mimic, which is a reference from outside the program.

python main.py --h_points 0,100000 --T_points 288.15,288.15
python main.py

In the first run look at the pressure in the row at 100000 m. In the second run look at the pressure column of the default table.

Three tasks, in order. First, compute the exact isothermal pressure at 100 km, \(p_0\,e^{-h/H}\) with \(H\) = 8430 m, which is 0.7144 Pa, and compare it with the 0.69344 Pa the program prints: the program is 2.93% low. Then compare the pressures of the default run with the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 values at the same altitudes, in the table below. Then decide which assumptions of the model would produce an error that grows with altitude, and in which direction.

The deficit grows steadily with height, to 18% at 86 km. Gravity falls by about 2.6% between the ground and 86 km, and the program ignores that; Experiment 9 lets you measure how much of the deficit this explains.

Then the source of the first error. At every step the program applies the forward Euler update of Eq. (7.7), shown under Algorithm, which uses the density at the bottom of the step. In an isothermal atmosphere it multiplies the pressure by \(1-\Delta h/H\) at each step, which is \(1 - 1/200\) with the program's step, while the exact solution multiplies it by \(e^{-\Delta h/H}\). Their ratio at height \(h\) is

Size of the Euler error, isothermal atmosphere DERIVED
\[ \frac{p_{\text{Euler}}}{p_{\text{exact}}} \;\approx\; 1 - \frac{h/H}{2N}, \qquad N = \frac{H}{\Delta h} = 200 \]

At 100 km, \(h/H = 11.86\), which gives 1 − 11.86/400 = 0.970, a 3.0% deficit; the 2.93% you measured differs only by the higher-order terms that this approximation drops. The error grows in proportion to the height in scale heights and falls in proportion to the step: Experiment 8 asks you to halve the step three times, and the error falls to 1.5%, 0.74% and 0.37%.

The same estimate holds in a layered atmosphere as long as the step is small compared with the smallest local scale height, and the local scale height is proportional to the temperature, so a cold layer is where a step sized for the surface would be too coarse. The program therefore sizes its first step from the coldest temperature the profile reaches at or above the surface, \(\Delta h = H_{\min}/200\), which costs more steps for a colder profile but keeps at least 200 steps per local scale height everywhere inside the supplied profile. It also lands on every supplied temperature breakpoint, so a layer thinner than that step cannot hide between Euler samples. When temperature varies slowly across a step and the run has not doubled the step, the error in \(\ln p\) is then about \(\ln(p_0/p)/400\) or less: 3.2% at 86 km in the default run, where the pressure has fallen by a factor of 3.3×105. Experiment 8 tests this on a profile that falls from 300 K to 20 K, where the program is 1.8% low. If a very tall or very cold profile needs more than the 50,000-point budget, the program doubles the step and starts again (see Beat 7 and Algorithm). If the enlarged step then reaches zero pressure at or below the highest supplied altitude, the program stops with an error instead of reporting that numerical crossing as the top of the atmosphere.

The Euler step is not the main source of the deficit against the standard atmosphere. An exact integration of Eq. (7.3), done outside the program, removes the sources one at a time. At 86 km it raises the ratio to the standard atmosphere from 0.815 to 0.836 when the Euler step is replaced by the exact integral, to 0.993 when gravity is also allowed to fall as \(g_0[R/(R+h)]^2\) with \(R\) = 6371 km, and to 1.000 when the inputs of the standard atmosphere itself are used as well (\(g_0\) = 9.80665 m s−2, 101325 Pa at sea level and a mean molecular mass of 28.9644 atomic mass units, in place of 9.81, 101300 and 28.97). Constant gravity is by far the largest source, the Euler step is second and the rounded inputs third; because the effects multiply, the shares depend a little on the order of removal, so this is one accounting, not the only one. The program itself uses the exact SI value of \(k_B\) and the atomic mass unit, so the coefficient of Eq. (7.3) carries only the rounding already present in \(\mu\) and in the measured atomic mass constant.

Beat 7 · The top of the model is a stopping rule, not a discovery

A real atmosphere fades gradually into space with no sharp top. The program needs two things the physics does not give it: a temperature above the highest altitude you supplied, and a place to stop. This beat shows both, because they can be mistaken for results.

python main.py --output_type temperature
python main.py --h_points 0,20000 --T_points 288.15,216.65 --output_type temperature

Look at the temperature plot in each run, and at the right-hand end of the curve. In the second run the last supplied altitude is 20 km.

Three tasks, in order. First, in the default run see that the temperature stays at 1240 K up to 500 km, the last supplied altitude, and then falls steeply to less than 1 K where the curve ends near 572 km. Read the printed Numerical top line; it should name that last stored altitude and temperature. Then in the second run see that the curve follows the supplied straight line down to 216.65 K at 20 km, and then falls toward zero, ending near 33 km. The atmosphere the program computes extends about 13 km above the last supplied point; the Numerical top line for that run is the number to quote. Then decide, before reading on, whether the falling curve above the last supplied altitude is a discovery about the atmosphere or an assumption of the program, and what a real atmosphere at 30 km would do.

Then the assumption. Above the highest supplied altitude the program switches to a power-law relation between temperature and pressure. On the first computed step above that altitude, the coefficient \(\beta\) is chosen so that the extrapolated temperature there equals the last supplied temperature:

Upper-atmosphere closure CLOSURE
\[ T = \beta\,p^{\,\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = 0.5 \tag{7.6} \]

The exponent \(\alpha = 0.5\) means that temperature falls as the square root of pressure. This closure is a simple mathematical extrapolation, not a model of the upper atmosphere. As pressure approaches zero it drives the modeled temperature toward zero, and with it the local scale height, so the pressure reaches zero at a finite altitude. That is the endpoint you see. It is a numerical stopping condition, not a sharply defined physical surface. If 50,000 steps do not reach it, the program doubles the step and starts again, unless every step in that pass was already shorter than the nominal increment, in which case it raises a budget error instead (see Algorithm), so a very extended profile can end with a coarser step than the \(H_{\min}/200\) of Beat 6. A zero pressure reached by such a coarser step at or below the highest supplied altitude would be a numerical failure, not this closure's endpoint, so the program stops with an error in that case. The default Earth profile extends to 500 km, so the closure takes over only above that altitude.

Algorithm

Finite-Difference Integration

Equation (7.1) is discretized using a first-order forward (Euler) step:

\[ p_j = p_{j-1} - g\,\rho_{j-1}\,\Delta h \tag{7.7} \]

At each altitude step the density \(\rho_{j-1}\) is obtained from the ideal gas law (Eq. 7.2) using the current pressure and the temperature profile at the current altitude. The integration terminates as soon as \(p_j \le 0\). That non-positive-pressure point is discarded, so the returned arrays end at the last valid positive-pressure step.

The first step is \(\Delta h = H_{\min}/200\), the Schutz choice of 200 steps per scale height, where \(H_{\min}\) is the scale height of Eq. (7.4) at the coldest temperature that the profile reaches at or above the surface (the temperature at altitude 0 and every supplied temperature at a positive altitude). The scale height is proportional to the temperature, so this is the smallest local scale height, and a colder profile costs more steps. For an isothermal profile the step is simply \(H/200\). The driver allows up to 50 000 points so that the default Earth calculation and the planetary comparisons keep this resolution.

Step-Size Restart (dh Doubling)

If 50 000 Euler steps do not reach the model's zero-pressure boundary, the driver doubles \(\Delta h\) and restarts the calculation from the surface, unless every step in that pass was already shorter than the nominal increment (a temperature-capped or breakpoint-limited profile). Doubling cannot enlarge those steps, so the driver then raises a budget error instead of retrying. Otherwise this is a coverage mechanism rather than an error-controlled adaptive integrator: it ensures that the calculation eventually spans a sufficiently large altitude range, but the successful run can be considerably coarser than the initial step. On every restart, the upper-atmosphere extrapolation state is reset so that \(\beta\) is recomputed from the pressure profile of the new run. A retry limit prevents pathological parameter choices from causing an indefinite sequence of step-size doublings.

A restart enlarges the step, and an enlarged step can be too coarse for the coldest layer. If, after a restart, the step reaches zero pressure at or below the highest supplied altitude, that crossing belongs to the step size and not to the model's closure boundary above the profile. The driver then raises a RuntimeError asking for a shorter or warmer profile instead of returning a false top. 

rho0  = ideal_gas_density(p0, mu, T0)      # surface density
coldest = min(T0, coldest supplied T above the surface)
scale_ref = p0 / (g * ideal_gas_density(p0, mu, coldest))  # H_min
dh    = scale_ref / 200.0                  # initial step

last_step = 0
while last_step == 0:
    profile.reached_top = False            # reset extrapolation state
    profile.beta = 0.0                     # for this fresh attempt
    node_index = 0                         # restart walks the nodes again
    for j in range(1, max_steps):          # max_steps = 50 000
        next_node = next positive h_points entry after alt[j-1], or none
        step = min(dh, next_node - alt[j-1])   # land on each breakpoint
        # shrink step if |Delta T| would exceed about 5% of the current T
        # (a step shorter than about 10^{-5} of the local scale height is left)
        alt[j] = alt[j-1] + step
        p[j] = hydrostatic_step(p[j-1], rho[j-1], g, step)
        if p[j] <= 0.0:
            if retry_count > 1 and alt[j-1] <= h_points[-1]:
                raise RuntimeError(...)    # enlarged step: false top
            last_step = j
            break                          # atmosphere top reached;
                                            # T[j], rho[j] never computed
                                            # for this (discarded) point
        T[j] = profile._temperature_at(alt[j], p[j])  # after one validate()
        rho[j] = ideal_gas_density(p[j], mu, T[j])
    if last_step == 0:
        if no unconstrained Euler step was taken:
            raise RuntimeError(...)        # cap already binds; do not double
        dh *= 2.0                          # retry with a larger step

Output Arrays

After the loop, the result arrays are trimmed to [:last_step], excluding the non-positive boundary point. The extract_output() function then selects whichever quantity the user requested (pressure, density, or temperature) and packages it with axis labels, units, and a title for the plotter.

Numerical note: This is a first-order Euler calculation intended to illustrate hydrostatic equilibrium, so error accumulates with altitude even at the normal resolution of 200 steps per smallest local scale height (Beat 6 gives the size of the error). A coverage restart, if one is needed for an unusually extended profile, increases that error further. The isothermal convergence experiment below provides an exact exponential solution against which the error can be measured.

Data Class

@dataclass
class TemperatureProfile:
    h: List[float] # altitude breakpoints (m)
    T: List[float] # temperatures at breakpoints (K)
    power: float = 0.5 # exponent α in T = β·p^α
    beta: float = 0.0 # coefficient β (set on first pass above the highest supplied breakpoint)

Stores the supplied temperature profile and state for the upper-atmosphere power-law extension. The profile is validated before integration: the altitudes and temperatures must be finite, the altitudes strictly increasing and the temperatures positive, and the whole altitude range must be small enough for the interpolation arithmetic (h_points[-1] - h_points[0] must be finite). beta is fixed on the first computed step above the highest supplied breakpoint so that the extrapolated temperature there equals the last supplied temperature.

Functions

TemperatureProfile.get_temp(self, altitude: float, pressure: float) -> float

Returns the temperature at the given altitude and current pressure. Uses linear interpolation (Eq. 7.5) between breakpoints while inside the measured range; below the first breakpoint returns \(T_0\) (constant); above the highest breakpoint applies \(T = \beta\,p^{\alpha}\) (Eq. 7.6), fixing \(\beta\) on the first call in that regime so the extrapolated temperature there equals the last supplied temperature. Every public call re-validates the whole profile (O(n) in the number of supplied points). A valid edit of the last altitude, last temperature or power drops the cached upper-atmosphere coefficient so the continuation is recomputed. The driver validates once and then calls the trusted interpolator. An interpolated value that is not finite is reported as a ValueError.

ideal_gas_density(pressure: float, mu: float, temperature: float) -> float

Returns the mass density \(\rho = p\,\mu\,m_u\,/\,(k_B\,T)\) in kg m−3, with \(\mu\) in atomic mass units and \(m_u\) the atomic mass unit. Implements Eq. (7.2) directly. Requires pressure ≥ 0 and temperature > 0, with finite numerical inputs.

hydrostatic_step(pressure_prev: float, rho_prev: float, g_accel: float, dh: float) -> float

Advances the pressure by one Euler step: \(p_j = p_{j-1} - g\,\rho_{j-1}\,\Delta h\). Implements Eq. (7.7). Non-positive results signal that the model's zero-pressure boundary has been reached or crossed.

Entry Point

main.py

The user-facing command-line entry point. It defines documented Earth defaults, parses and validates every model input, maps lowercase plot selectors to the driver's legacy capitalized values, prints the checkpoint table, and calls the plotter. It also supports python main.py --help and python main.py --version.

The following is an abbreviated view of the entry point; the complete temperature arrays and argument-parser setup are in main.py. The first line printed is a status line giving the version, the build, the planet name and the plotted quantity, followed by the checkpoint table and one Numerical top line. 

def main(argv=None):
    args = parse_args(argv)
    params = AtmosphereParameters(
        planet_name=args.planet_name,
        g_accel=args.g_accel,
        mu=args.mu,
        p0=args.p0,
        h_points=args.h_points,
        T_points=args.T_points,
        output_type=OUTPUT_TYPES[args.output_type],
    )
    try:
        result = AtmosphereModel(params).run()
    except (ValueError, RuntimeError) as exc:
        raise SystemExit(f"Atmosphere input/model error: {exc}") from exc
    print(
        f"Atmosphere {result.model_version} (build {result.build_id}) — "
        f"{result.planet_name}: {result.output_type}"
    )
    print_checkpoints(result, args.h_points, args.T_points)
    save_and_maybe_show(extract_output(result), save_path=args.save_plot, show=not args.no_show)

Temperature profile sources and limitations: From sea level through approximately 86 km, the default temperatures use U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 layer-boundary values. The standard defines those boundaries in geopotential height; the corresponding geometric altitudes are used here.

From 100 km through 500 km, the values are rounded neutral temperatures read from an NRLMSIS 2.0 CCMC Instant Run for 15 October 2024 at 12:00 UTC, geographic latitude 55° N and longitude 45° E. The CCMC run used observational solar and geomagnetic indices for that date; the index values, run identifier and raw output were not archived with this page, so the seven rounded temperatures cannot be regenerated from it and are recorded in the test suite as supplied values. These thermospheric values are therefore a date- and condition-specific example, not universal standard temperatures: the thermosphere varies strongly with solar activity, geomagnetic activity, location, season, and time of day.

The temperature curve is consequently more realistic than a single fixed thermospheric profile, but the rest of this program remains deliberately simple. In particular, g_accel and mu are held constant with altitude, whereas the real thermosphere changes in composition and effective molecular mass. Pressure and density above roughly 100 km should therefore be interpreted as an illustrative continuation of the hydrostatic ideal-gas model, not as an NRLMSIS-quality prediction. Above 500 km, the program begins the simplified power-law extrapolation of Eq. (7.6).

The h_points and T_points lists must have the same length; all numerical inputs must be finite, altitudes must be strictly increasing, and all temperatures must be positive. The span from the first to the last altitude must be finite. g_accel, mu, and p0 must also be positive. The calculation always starts at altitude 0. If the supplied profile spans altitude 0, the reference temperature is interpolated there; if the first measurement lies above 0, T_points[0] is held constant down to the reference level. In normal use, set h_points[0] = 0.0 so the reference condition is explicit. The integration step is sized from the coldest temperature at or above the reference level, so a profile with a very cold layer takes more steps; a profile so tall and cold that the point budget cannot cover it stops with an error (see Algorithm and Beat 6).

Suggested Experiments

The experiments below are arranged in approximate order of difficulty. Most use only command-line parameters; experiments that require a plotting or algorithm change say so explicitly.

1 · Introductory — Explore Earth's Pressure, Density, and Temperature Profiles

Run the default Earth model three times, selecting pressure, density, and temperature in turn. Compare how the three quantities change with altitude. In the temperature plot, identify the familiar lower-atmosphere structure below about 86 km and the strong thermospheric temperature rise in the supplied 100–500 km profile. Above the highest supplied temperature point, remember that the program uses its simplified power-law extrapolation; do not interpret that far-upper-atmosphere continuation as a realistic physical temperature profile.

python main.py --output_type pressure python main.py --output_type density python main.py --output_type temperature

2 · Introductory — Plot Pressure on a Logarithmic Scale

Run with --output_type pressure and add ax.set_yscale("log") in plot_atmosphere.py. Pressure spans many orders of magnitude, so a logarithmic vertical axis makes the structure much easier to inspect. For an isothermal atmosphere an exponential pressure law would appear as a straight line; Earth's changing temperature therefore shows up as changes in slope.

python main.py --output_type pressure # programming change: add ax.set_yscale("log") after ax.plot(x, y)

3 · Introductory–Intermediate — Surface Pressure Changes the Scale, Not the Scale Height

Keep Earth's gravity, mean molecular mass, and temperature profile fixed, but repeat the calculation with surface pressure multiplied and divided by 10. Compare the pressure profiles as fractions of their own surface values. Every absolute pressure in the middle run is 10 times the one in the first run, and every pressure in the last run is 10 times the one in the middle run, so the two extremes differ by a factor of 100. The fractional falloff with altitude is essentially unchanged: at 11019 m the pressure is 22.21% of its surface value in all three runs (2249.9, 22499 and 2.2499e+05 Pa). Relate this to Eq. (7.4), in which the isothermal scale height depends on temperature, gravity, and mean molecular mass but not on surface pressure. At fixed gravity the surface pressure sets the pressure normalization and the mass of air above each square meter, \(p_0/g\).

python main.py --p0 1.013e4 python main.py --p0 1.013e5 python main.py --p0 1.013e6

4 · Intermediate — What Controls Scale Height?

Change one parameter at a time while keeping the others at their Earth values. First try mu = 18, 28.97, and 44; then repeat with g_accel equal to half of Earth's, Earth's and twice Earth's: 4.905, 9.81 and 19.62. Verify that the atmosphere becomes more vertically extended when mean molecular mass or gravity is reduced. For an isothermal atmosphere, Eq. (7.4) predicts \(H\propto T/(\mu g)\), so halving \(g\) doubles \(H\) and takes the square root of the pressure fraction at any height. Check number: at 11019 m the default run gives 22499 Pa, which is 0.2221 of the surface pressure; g_accel 4.905 should give 47753 Pa (0.4714, close to \(\sqrt{0.2221}\)), and g_accel 19.62 should give 4994.3 Pa (0.0493, close to \(0.2221^2\)).

python main.py --mu 18 python main.py --mu 28.97 python main.py --mu 44 python main.py --g_accel 4.905 python main.py --g_accel 19.62

5 · Intermediate — Compare Mars and Venus

Apply the same model to two very different CO₂ atmospheres. Mars has weak gravity and very low surface pressure; Venus has much stronger gravity, an enormous surface pressure, and a very hot lower atmosphere. Compare their pressure profiles, their base scale heights \(p_0/(g\rho_0)\) and their column masses \(p_0/g\), the mass of air above each square meter. Use the comparison to separate the roles of surface pressure, temperature, gravity, and mean molecular mass rather than attributing atmospheric "thickness" to pressure alone: the scale height says how tall the atmosphere is, the column mass how much air stands above each square meter.

# Mars python main.py --planet_name Mars --g_accel 3.72 --mu 44 --p0 610 \ --h_points 0,10000,25000,45000 --T_points 210,170,145,130 # Venus python main.py --planet_name Venus --g_accel 8.87 --mu 44 --p0 9.2e6 \ --h_points 0,10000,20000,30000,40000,50000,60000 \ --T_points 735,658,579,495,416,348,263

6 · Intermediate — Change the Temperature Profile

Replace the default profile with a deliberately simple temperature structure and observe how warm and cool layers affect density through \(\rho\propto p/T\). Introduce a warm layer above a cooler one, then compare the Pressure, Density, and Temperature outputs. This isolates the role of the supplied temperature profile from changes in gravity, mean molecular mass, or surface pressure.

python main.py --h_points 0,50000,100000 \ --T_points 288,200,350

7 · Intermediate–Advanced — Jupiter as a Local 1-Bar-Level Model

Use the 1-bar level as a reference and model only a relatively thin region of Jupiter's outer atmosphere. The low mean molecular mass of hydrogen-helium gas produces a large scale height despite strong gravity. Treat this explicitly as a local exercise: the program assumes constant gravity and fixed-composition ideal gas behavior, so it should not be interpreted as a model of Jupiter's full atmosphere or interior. Schutz warned against applying this simple constant-gravity model to an entire gas giant; the limited calculation here is intended to show why that warning matters.

python main.py --planet_name Jupiter --g_accel 24.8 --mu 2.2 --p0 1e5 \ --h_points 0,20000,50000,100000 --T_points 165,130,110,150

8 · Advanced — Test the Euler Integration Against an Exact Isothermal Atmosphere

Use a constant temperature of 288.15 K from 0 to 100 km, with extra checkpoints at 10, 50 and 90 km, and plot Pressure. In that interval the exact solution is \(p(h)=p_0\exp(-h/H)\), with \(H=k_BT/(g\mu m_u)\) = 8430 m. Compare the numerical pressure with the exact result at 10, 50, and 90 km. The program should print 30843, 265.04 and 2.2775 Pa, against exact values of 30934, 269.01 and 2.3394 Pa: 0.30%, 1.48% and 2.64% low. Only compare points within the supplied constant-temperature interval; above 100 km the program begins its power-law extrapolation and the atmosphere is no longer isothermal.

To test convergence, temporarily change STEPS_PER_SCALE_HEIGHT = 200 in driver_atmosphere.py to 400, 800 and then 1600. The supplied 50 000-point budget is ample for these cases; if you try still finer steps, ensure that MAX_STEPS is large enough to reach 100 km without triggering a restart. The error should decrease roughly in proportion to the step size, as expected for this first-order Euler update: at 100 km the program should be 2.93% low with 200 steps per scale height, then 1.5%, 0.74% and 0.37% low with 400, 800 and 1600.

To test a cold layer, run a profile that falls from 300 K to 20 K over 4400 m and stays at 20 K for another 4400 m. The program sizes its step from the coldest temperature, so it should print 12.6 Pa at 8800 m, against an exact value of 12.83 Pa (1.8% low). Work the exact value out from Eq. (7.3): in a layer where \(T\) changes linearly, \(\ln(p_1/p_0) = -(g\mu m_u/k_B)\,[\Delta h/(T_1-T_0)]\,\ln(T_1/T_0)\), and in an isothermal layer \(\ln(p_1/p_0) = -(g\mu m_u/k_B)\,\Delta h/T\). Then edit driver_atmosphere.py so that the step is sized from the surface temperature alone (use T0 in place of the coldest temperature) and compare the error again.

python main.py --h_points 0,10000,50000,90000,100000 \ --T_points 288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15 --output_type pressure # programming change for convergence: edit STEPS_PER_SCALE_HEIGHT python main.py --h_points 0,4400,8800 --T_points 300,20,20 --output_type pressure # programming change for the last part: size the step from T0 instead of the coldest temperature

9 · Advanced Programming Extension — Let Gravity Decrease with Altitude

For an advanced extension, replace the program's constant value of gravity with a height-dependent gravitational acceleration. Make a separate experimental copy of the program and replace the constant \(g\) in the hydrostatic step by a function such as \(g(h)=g_0[R/(R+h)]^2\), where \(R\) is the planet's radius. The function hydrostatic_step receives \(g\) but not the altitude, so the change belongs in the integration loop of driver_atmosphere.py: evaluate the gravity at the bottom of the step, the same place where the Euler step takes its density, and leave the step size unchanged. Compare the resulting pressure profile with the constant-gravity calculation and identify the altitude range over which the simpler approximation is adequate. To check your implementation, use the default Earth run with \(R\) = 6371 km: the pressure at 11019 m should be about 22560 Pa (0.27% above the constant-gravity 22499 Pa) and the pressure at 86000 m about 0.36195 Pa (19% above the constant-gravity 0.30439 Pa, and 0.969 of the standard atmosphere's 0.3734 Pa). This is a natural bridge to later stellar-structure calculations, where gravity varies with position.

python main.py # programming change in driver_atmosphere.py, in the loop over j: # R = 6.371e6 # planet radius (m) # g_here = g * (R / (R + alt[j - 1])) ** 2 # gravity at the bottom of the step # p[j] = hydrostatic_step(p[j - 1], rho[j - 1], g_here, step) # expected: 22560 Pa at 11019 m and 0.36195 Pa at 86000 m

Related Programs

Atmosphere shares the same layered module architecture — physics / driver / plot / main — with all other programs in this suite. The programs most closely related in physical content are:

Star Neutron
  • Star — integrates the analogous Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium equation for a self-gravitating polytropic star. The structure equation \(dp/dr = -G M(r)\,\rho/r^2\) is the stellar generalization of Eq. (7.1), with gravity varying with radius and mass continuity replacing the ideal-gas closure.
  • Neutron — extends stellar structure to general relativity via the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equation, the relativistic counterpart of Atmosphere's hydrostatic step with a polytropic equation of state.

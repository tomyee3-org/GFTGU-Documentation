Description

Atmosphere is the Chapter 7 teaching model of a planetary atmosphere in hydrostatic equilibrium (Investigations 7.1 and 7.3). You supply a temperature profile. The program integrates pressure upward from a reference surface and plots pressure, density, or temperature.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence:

Pressure holds up the weight of the air above; the gas law turns that pressure into a density; their ratio is a length — the scale height — set by temperature, gravity and mean molecular mass.

What this program is not. It is not a weather code and not a research atmosphere. Gravity and mean molecular mass are constant with height. Temperature is an input, linearly interpolated between the altitudes you give. Pressure is advanced by a first-order Euler step that also lands on every supplied temperature breakpoint, and shortens a step that would jump temperature by more than about 5%, so a thin or steep layer cannot hide between samples. The altitude where pressure hits zero is a stopping rule, not a physical top of the air.

Before the first run

Unpack the program zip so you have a folder named Atmosphere that contains main.py. Open a terminal in that folder. You need Python 3.10 or newer with NumPy and Matplotlib. If you do not already have that environment, follow the project-wide GFTGU Python Setup Guide. Then:

python main.py --version
python main.py

Every command on this page assumes that working directory. Give altitude and temperature lists with commas and no brackets. A list that starts with a negative altitude can be typed as --h_points -1000,0,10000.

Beat 0 · Pressure is the weight of the air above you

Climb, and there is less air overhead. That is the whole idea of atmospheric structure. Ask the program only to show it for the Earth.

python main.py

Look at the first two rows of the checkpoint table — altitudes 0 and 11019 m — and at the pressure curve. Ignore the other columns. Surface pressure over \(g\) is the mass sitting on each square metre of ground. The pressure at 11 km is already a fraction of the surface value; most of the column is below that height. The curve is steepest near the ground. Write one sentence that says why, before you read the equation.

Hydrostatic equilibrium — the ODE
\[ \frac{dp}{dh} = -g\,\rho. \]

The slope is steepest where \(\rho\) is largest. Gravity is held constant. The equation cannot be solved alone: it has two unknowns. Beat 1 supplies the missing relation.

Beat 1 · Density is not free: the gas law supplies it

Air is a gas. Density follows from pressure and temperature. Plot density this time; the printed table is the same as Beat 0.

python main.py --output_type density

Read the surface density (it should sit next to \(1.225\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 value). Then divide density by the p/T column in two different rows. The ratio does not move. That constant is \(\mu m_u/k_B\): the p/T column is not itself the density, but density written without the constant factor \(\mu m_u/k_B\).

Ideal gas — derived; governing equation — the ODE
\[ \rho = \frac{p\,\mu\,m_u}{k_B\,T}, \qquad \frac{dp}{dh} = -\frac{g\,\mu\,m_u}{k_B\,T(h)}\,p. \]

At fixed pressure, colder air is denser, so density need not fall as fast as pressure. \(T(h)\) is an input. Beat 2 solves the equation when that input is a constant.

Beat 2 · One number sets the size of an atmosphere

If the temperature is the same at every height, equal steps upward multiply the pressure by the same factor.

python main.py --h_points 0,8000,16000,24000,32000 --T_points 288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15

Read only the table; above the last supplied altitude the program switches to the closure of Beat 7. Divide each pressure by the one below it. Find the height over which the pressure falls by a factor of \(e\). Then compute \(p_0/(g\rho_0)\) from the first row. Those two lengths are two readings of the same model, not an independent check. Expect them to agree to a few tenths of a per cent; Beat 6 says why they differ.

Barometric formula and scale height — derived
\[ p = p_0\,e^{-h/H}, \qquad H = \frac{p_0}{g\,\rho_0} = \frac{k_B\,T_0}{g\,\mu\,m_u}. \]

For the default Earth numbers at 288.15 K, \(H\approx 8.43\,\mathrm{km}\). The first integration step is \(H_{\min}/200\), sized from the coldest temperature on the profile so a cold layer is not stepped through too coarsely. The integrator also lands on every supplied temperature breakpoint, even when that breakpoint is closer than \(H_{\min}/200\), and shortens a step that would change temperature by more than about 5% of the temperature at the start of that step. A proposed step shorter than about \(10^{-5}\) of the local scale height is left alone.

Beat 3 · What the scale height depends on — and what it does not

\(H\) contains \(T\), \(g\) and \(\mu\). It does not contain the surface pressure. Test that, one dial at a time, on the default Earth temperature profile.

python main.py --mu 18
python main.py --mu 44
python main.py --g_accel 19.62
python main.py --p0 1.013e6

Compare the fraction \(p(11019\,\mathrm{m})/p_0\) with the default run. Light gas or weak gravity stretches the atmosphere; heavy gas or strong gravity makes pressure fall more rapidly with altitude. Raising \(p_0\) by ten multiplies every pressure by ten and leaves the fraction alone. \(p_0/g\) is the column mass; \(H\) is the height.

From the scale-height formula
\[ H \propto \frac{T}{\mu\,g}. \]

Beat 4 · Real air is not isothermal

The program does not compute the layered temperature of a real atmosphere. You give temperatures at chosen altitudes; it joins them with straight lines. The scale height then changes with height.

python main.py --output_type temperature
python main.py --h_points 0,50000,100000 --T_points 288,200,350

On the default temperature plot find the four layers: troposphere cooling, stratosphere warming, mesosphere cooling, thermosphere rising. Local \(H\) tracks \(T\). In the second run, check that the density ratio between 50 and 100 km is the pressure ratio multiplied by \(T(50\,\mathrm{km})/T(100\,\mathrm{km})=200/350\).

Piecewise-linear temperature — prescribed
\[ T(h) = T_{i-1} + \frac{T_i-T_{i-1}}{h_i-h_{i-1}}\,(h-h_{i-1}), \qquad h_{i-1}\le h<h_i. \]

Below the first supplied point the temperature is held at \(T_0\). Above the last one, Beat 7 takes over. The default Earth list combines U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 values through about 86 km with seven rounded NRLMSIS 2.0 thermospheric temperatures (100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 400 and 500 km) for one date and place.

Beat 5 · Other worlds, same equations

Nothing so far is specific to the Earth. Change \(g\), \(\mu\), \(p_0\) and the temperature list. “Thick” can mean column mass \(p_0/g\), or it can mean scale height. Those two rankings need not agree.

python main.py --planet_name Mars --g_accel 3.72 --mu 44 --p0 610 \
  --h_points 0,10000,25000,45000 --T_points 210,170,145,130

python main.py --planet_name Venus --g_accel 8.87 --mu 44 --p0 9.2e6 \
  --h_points 0,10000,20000,30000,40000,50000,60000 \
  --T_points 735,658,579,495,416,348,263

python main.py --planet_name Jupiter --g_accel 24.8 --mu 2.2 --p0 1e5 \
  --h_points 0,20000,50000,100000 --T_points 165,130,110,150

Compute \(H=p_0/(g\rho_0)\) and the column mass \(p_0/g\) from the surface row of each table. Jupiter’s scale height is the largest of the four because \(\mu\) is so small. Venus’s column is about a hundred Earth columns; Mars has little air and a taller scale height than Earth. Jupiter has no solid surface: the run takes the 1-bar level as the reference and the supplied profile ends at 100 km; the plotted curve continues a little above that under the Beat 7 closure.

Beat 6 · How far to trust the numbers

The isothermal atmosphere of Beat 2 has an exact answer. Use it, then compare the default Earth run with the standard atmosphere it is meant to mimic.

python main.py --h_points 0,100000 --T_points 288.15,288.15
python main.py

At 100 km the exact isothermal pressure is \(p_0 e^{-h/H}\). The printed value will be a few per cent low. That is the Euler step: each increment multiplies \(p\) by \(1-\Delta h/H\) instead of by \(e^{-\Delta h/H}\). With \(N=H/\Delta h=200\),

Size of the Euler error, isothermal case — derived
\[ \frac{p_{\mathrm{Euler}}}{p_{\mathrm{exact}}}\approx 1-\frac{h/H}{2N}. \]

That Euler estimate is for a slowly varying temperature and a run that did not have to enlarge the step. Against the U.S. Standard Atmosphere the default run is already a fraction of a per cent low at 11 km and about 18 per cent low at 86 km. Do not read the isothermal Euler estimate (a few per cent at 100 km) as the 18% figure in that comparison: most of the growing deficit against the standard is constant gravity, not the Euler step. Real \(g\) falls by about 2.6 per cent to 86 km. Rounded inputs (\(g=9.81\), \(p_0=101300\,\mathrm{Pa}\), \(\mu=28.97\)) take a smaller third share. The factor \(\mu m_u/k_B\) inherits the rounding already present in \(\mu\) and in the measured atomic mass constant. Use the program for scale heights and ratios, not for an 86 km pressure to three figures.

Beat 7 · The top of the model is a stopping rule

A real atmosphere fades into space. The program needs a temperature above the last altitude you supplied, and a place to stop. Neither is a discovery about the air.

python main.py --output_type temperature
python main.py --h_points 0,20000 --T_points 288.15,216.65 --output_type temperature

In the default run the temperature holds at 1240 K to 500 km and then falls toward zero where the curve ends. Read the printed Numerical top line for the last stored altitude and temperature; it is a stopping rule, not a physical top. In the short profile the fall begins at 20 km. That falling tail is the closure, not the mesosphere.

Upper-atmosphere closure — not a physical model
\[ T=\beta\,p^{\alpha},\qquad \alpha=0.5. \]

\(\beta\) is chosen so the first step above the last supplied altitude matches that last temperature. As \(p\to 0\), \(T\to 0\) and the local scale height collapses, so pressure reaches zero at a finite height. If 50,000 steps do not get there, the program doubles \(\Delta h\) and starts again. A zero pressure reached by that coarser step at or below the last supplied altitude is refused as a numerical failure.

Output

Every run prints a heading with version and build, then a checkpoint table of altitude, pressure, density, \(p/T\), and temperature at every supplied altitude that still lies in the positive-pressure domain. After the table it prints one Numerical top line: the last stored altitude and temperature, which is the stopping rule, not a physical top of the air. The density is \(p\,\mu m_u/(k_B T)\), so \(p/T\) is the density stripped of that constant, not the density in other units. Numbers use five significant figures. A plot window then opens unless you pass --no_show; --save_plot PATH writes the same figure to a file. The plotted quantity is the only thing --output_type changes.

Experiments

EXP-1 · First run

Weigh the column

From the default table, compute \(p_0/g\). What fraction of that mass sits below 11 km? Why is the pressure curve steepest near the ground?

EXP-2 · Beginner

A straight line on log paper

Run Beat 2’s isothermal command. Plot \(\ln p\) against \(h\) from the table. What slope do you expect, and what length is minus one over that slope?

EXP-3 · Beginner

One dial at a time

From Beat 3, which change moves \(p(11\,\mathrm{km})/p_0\), and which one only rescales every pressure? Predict the \(\mu=44\) fraction from the default fraction before you look.

EXP-4 · Intermediate

Column versus height

For Earth, Mars, Venus and the Jupiter slice, tabulate \(p_0/g\) and \(H\). Which world is “thickest” on each ranking? Why do the rankings disagree?

EXP-5 · Intermediate

Cold layer, warm layer

Run the three-point profile of Beat 4. Show from the table that \(\rho_2/\rho_1=(p_2/p_1)\,(T_1/T_2)\) between 50 and 100 km.

EXP-6 · Intermediate

Grade the Euler step

Compare the isothermal 100 km pressure with \(p_0 e^{-h/H}\). Does the Beat 6 formula account for most of the deficit?

EXP-7 · Advanced

What constant \(g\) costs

At 86 km, \(g\) has fallen by about 2.6 per cent. Argue, without writing a new integrator, why holding \(g\) fixed makes the model pressure run low, and in which direction the deficit should grow.

EXP-8 · Advanced

Do not name the tail

Run Beat 7’s short profile. Read the printed Numerical top line. Where does the supplied line end, and where does the curve end? Invent one sentence that uses the word “top” only for the stopping rule.

Domain of Validity

  • Constant \(g\), constant \(\mu\), ideal gas. Composition does not change with height.
  • Temperature is prescribed, not computed. Linear interpolation between checkpoints; a power-law closure above the last one.
  • Forward Euler on \(p(h)\). Absolute pressures high in the atmosphere are not the point of the program.
  • Planetary examples are teaching profiles. Jupiter is a local 100 km slice above 1 bar, not a model of the planet.
  • No winds, no photochemistry, no escape, no magnetic field.

Atmosphere

Representative Sample Outputs

Current program version: 1.4.0   Build: 95f43226e01f
Guide prepared: 2026-09-20

Purpose. These eight figures, one for each Beat of the Help file, give a compact visual survey of what Atmosphere computes. The program integrates hydrostatic equilibrium upward from a surface pressure, using the ideal-gas law and a temperature profile that the user supplies; it does not compute the temperature. Each run shows one plot and prints a checkpoint table. Where a Beat uses several commands, the figure shows one of them and the headline values also cover the others.

Beat 0 — Pressure versus altitude for the default Earth

python main.py

The default Earth atmosphere: pressure against altitude from the surface to where the calculation stops, with constant gravity and the hybrid standard-atmosphere and NRLMSIS temperature profile.

What it shows

  • Pressure falls fastest near the ground, where the air is densest, and is almost flat on the linear axis above about 50 km.
  • Each thin layer of air adds its weight to the pressure below it, dp/dh = −gρ, so the slope at any height is set by the density there.
  • About 22% of the surface pressure remains at 11 km, so about 78% of the air's mass lies below that height.

Headline values

  • Surface pressure 1.013×105 Pa; divided by g = 9.81 m s−2 this is about 10,300 kg of air above each square metre.
  • 22499 Pa at 11019 m, 0.30438 Pa at 86000 m, 0.023876 Pa at 100 km, and 1.437e-08 Pa at 500 km.
  • First altitude step 27.347 m; 20,933 stored points; the curve ends at 572.4 km, where the pressure is 4.1e-15 Pa.

Beat 1 — Density versus altitude for the default Earth

python main.py --output_type density

Mass density from the same run: the ideal-gas law applied at every step to the pressure and the supplied temperature.

What it shows

  • Density falls with height much as pressure does, but it is not a rescaled copy of it, because it also depends on the temperature profile.
  • The printed p/T column is directly proportional to density; multiplying it by μmu/kB = 3.484×10−3 kg K J−1 gives the density in kg m−3.
  • Between 71802 m and 86000 m the pressure falls by a factor of 11.3 but the density by only 9.9, because the air is cooler at 86 km.

Headline values

  • Surface density 1.2249 kg m−3, within 0.01% of the 1.2250 of the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976.
  • 0.36184 kg m−3 at 11019 m, 5.6731e-06 at 86000 m, 4.3785e-07 at 100 km, and 4.0378e-14 at 500 km.
  • Surface p/T = 351.55 Pa K−1; times 3.484×10−3 this is the printed 1.2249.

Beat 2 — An isothermal atmosphere and its scale height

python main.py --h_points 0,8000,16000,24000,32000 --T_points 288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15,288.15

An isothermal 288.15 K atmosphere with checkpoints every 8 km. The isothermal part ends at 32 km; the curve then continues to 48.7 km along the program's upper closure (Beat 7).

What it shows

  • Equal steps in altitude multiply the pressure by the same factor, which is what an exponential looks like.
  • That factor gives the scale height, and so does p0/(gρ0): two readings of the same model output, not independent checks.
  • In one scale height the pressure falls by a factor of e.

Headline values

  • Pressures at 0, 8, 16, 24 and 32 km: 1.013e+05, 39124, 15110, 5835.9, 2253.9 Pa.
  • Every 8 km step multiplies the pressure by 0.3862.
  • H = 8409 m from that factor and 8430 m from p0/(gρ0), the value of kBT/(gμmu).

Beat 3 — A heavier gas: mean molecular mass 44

python main.py --mu 44

The default Earth profile with μ = 44, a carbon-dioxide-like gas. The curve has the same form as the Beat 0 figure but falls off faster. The other three dials of Beat 3 (μ = 18, g = 19.62, p0 = 1.013×106 Pa) give the same kind of figure. On this full-range linear axis every such curve hugs the vertical axis, so the evidence for this Beat is in the printed pressure column and the values below.

What it shows

  • Heavier molecules or stronger gravity shrink the scale height, so the atmosphere is squeezed toward the ground.
  • For a fixed temperature profile, ln(p/p0) is proportional to μg: doubling g squares the fraction of surface pressure that remains at a given height.
  • Changing the surface pressure rescales the pressure but leaves the fraction, and so the shape, unchanged.

Headline values

  • Fraction of the surface pressure left at 11019 m: 0.2221 for air, 0.393 for μ = 18, 0.102 for μ = 44 (this figure), 0.0493 for g = 19.62, and 0.2221 for ten times the surface pressure.
  • Surface scale heights: 8.430 km for air, 13.57 km for μ = 18, 5.550 km for μ = 44 and 4.215 km for g = 19.62.
  • For μ = 44 the surface density is 1.8604 kg m−3 and the pressure at 11019 m is 10304 Pa.

Beat 4 — The supplied temperature profile

python main.py --output_type temperature

The default temperature profile: an input, not a computed result, joined by straight lines. The steep fall above 500 km belongs to the program's closure, not to the supplied data.

What it shows

  • Four layers in order: cooling through the troposphere, warming in the stratosphere, cooling in the mesosphere, and strong warming in the thermosphere.
  • The local scale height is proportional to the temperature, so the same equation describes a thin atmosphere where it is cold and a thick one where it is hot.
  • The program does not compute any of the layering; it interpolates the temperatures it is given.

Headline values

  • 288.15 K at the surface; 216.65 K at 11019 m; 270.65 K at 47.35–51.41 km; 186.95 K at 86 km; 1240 K at 400–500 km.
  • Local scale height 6.34 km at 216.65 K and 36.3 km at 1240 K.
  • The pressure falls by a factor of 4.50 over the first 11 km but only by about 4700 from 200 km to 500 km.
  • Second Beat 4 run (288, 200, 350 K at 0, 50, 100 km): from 50 km to 100 km the pressure falls by 595 and the density by 1042, their ratio being 350/200 = 1.75.

Beat 5 — Another world: Venus

python main.py --planet_name Venus --g_accel 8.87 --mu 44 --p0 9.2e6 --h_points 0,10000,20000,30000,40000,50000,60000 --T_points 735,658,579,495,416,348,263

A Venus profile: p0 = 9.2 MPa, μ = 44, g = 8.87 m s−2, and seven temperatures from 735 K to 263 K over 60 km, rounded from a secondary compilation (the Wikipedia article “Atmosphere of Venus”). The curve ends at 71 km, where the closure ends the calculation. The Mars and Jupiter runs of Beat 5 give figures of the same kind.

What it shows

  • Nothing in the equations is specific to the Earth: the same program describes another planet once g, μ, p0 and the temperatures are changed.
  • “Thickest atmosphere” has two meanings: how much air stands above each square metre (p0/g) and how tall the atmosphere is (H). Venus has far more air overhead but a scale height less than twice the Earth's.
  • The model comes out below the published Venus values, more so higher up, for the reasons Beat 6 examines on the Earth.

Headline values

  • Surface densities: Venus 66.24, Mars 0.015372, Jupiter 0.16036 and Earth 1.2249 kg m−3.
  • Base scale heights p0/(gρ0): Venus 15.7 km, Mars 10.7 km, Jupiter 25.1 km, Earth 8.4 km.
  • Column mass p0/g: Venus 1.04×106 kg m−2, Mars 164, Jupiter 4032 and Earth about 10,300 kg m−2.
  • Venus pressure at 10 km and 60 km: 4.6835e+06 Pa and 20022 Pa, against published values of 4.80 MPa and 23.9 kPa (2% and 16% low).

Beat 6 — The program against an exact isothermal atmosphere

python main.py --h_points 0,100000 --T_points 288.15,288.15

An isothermal 288.15 K atmosphere from 0 to 100 km, for which the exact answer p0e−h/H is known. The second Beat 6 run is the default Earth run of Beat 0.

What it shows

  • With an exact answer available, the error of the forward-Euler step can be measured: the program is slightly low, and the gap grows with height.
  • The size of the gap follows the estimate 1 − (h/H)/(2N), where N = 200 is the number of steps per scale height.
  • Against the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 the default run is low by 18% at 86 km, mostly because gravity is held constant; the Help's table and accounting show the split.

Headline values

  • At 100 km (11.86 scale heights): the program prints 0.69344 Pa against an exact 0.7144 Pa, 2.93% low.
  • The estimate gives 1 − 11.86/400 = 0.970, a deficit of about 3.0%.
  • With 400, 800 and 1600 steps per scale height (Experiment 8, a source edit) the deficit falls to 1.5%, 0.74% and 0.37%.
  • Default run at 86000 m: 0.30438 Pa, against 0.3734 Pa in the standard atmosphere, a ratio of 0.815.

Beat 7 — Where the curve stops

python main.py --h_points 0,20000 --T_points 288.15,216.65 --output_type temperature

A profile supplied only to 20 km. The curve follows the straight line down to 216.65 K at 20 km, then falls to nearly zero at 32.6 km, where the calculation stops. The default temperature run (Beat 4) ends the same way above 500 km.

What it shows

  • Above the last supplied altitude the temperature is the program's closure T = βp0.5, an assumption, not a result: it drives the temperature toward zero as the pressure approaches zero.
  • The endpoint is where the numerical pressure reaches zero. It is a stopping rule, not a sharp physical top of the atmosphere.
  • A real atmosphere at 30 km is not near zero kelvin: the standard atmosphere's 228.65 K at 32162 m appears in the default table.

Headline values

  • Last supplied point 20 km at 216.65 K; the curve ends at 32.58 km, about 13 km above it, at 0.58 K.
  • Default run: the temperature stays at 1240 K to 500 km and the curve ends at 572.4 km at 0.66 K, less than 1 K.
  • Above its last supplied point the default run's closure adds 72.4 km; the 20 km run's adds 12.6 km.

Notes for evaluation

  • The model holds gravity and the mean molecular mass constant with height, takes the temperature profile as input, and integrates with a first-order Euler step. Its pressures are illustrations of the equations, not predictions.
  • The default Earth profile combines U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 layer values through about 86 km, converted to geometric altitude, with seven rounded NRLMSIS 2.0 temperatures from 100 to 500 km for one date and place. The Venus profile is from a secondary compilation; the Mars and Jupiter profiles are round-number illustrations.
  • Above the highest supplied altitude the temperature follows a mathematical closure that ends the calculation at a finite altitude. That endpoint is a stopping rule.
  • Every headline value was read from the checkpoint table printed by the run or computed from it, as the Help does; the Help gives the sources and limits of the input profiles.

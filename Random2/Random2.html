Description

Random2 explores the square-root law of random walks and uses it to picture how radiation leaks out of a star. Energy released in the Sun's core does not stream outwards; it is absorbed and re-emitted so often that its path is a random walk, and its net displacement increases much more slowly than its total path length. For many independent, zero-mean steps of finite variance, the typical distance from the start grows approximately as \(\sqrt N\), rather than \(N\).

The program has two displays. scaled_distance runs many three-dimensional random walks of different lengths and prints and plots their average distance from the start. walk2d draws a few fixed-length, equally-likely-in-every-direction walks from the centre of a circle, a schematic star, and counts the steps each one takes to reach the edge. Every run prints a summary of the numbers before it opens its plot.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: independent, zero-mean steps give a mean distance from the starting point that grows approximately as \(\sqrt N\) when the step count \(N\) is large; in the fixed-step circular model, reaching radius \(R\) takes on the order of \((R/\lambda)^2\) steps of length \(\lambda\), and the printed standard errors help you judge an average made from a finite number of random trials.

Model boundary: the circle is a diffusion picture, not a solar model. There is no varying opacity or mean free path, no convection, and the displayed paths are two-dimensional. The escape-time spread therefore cannot be used as the Sun's three-dimensional distribution.

Reading the two summaries

scaled_distance prints a row for each step count: mean scaled distance, standard error, and ratio to \(\sqrt N\). Read the large-\(N\) prediction and fitted slope below the rows. walk2d prints the radius, step length, \((R/\lambda)^2\), step cap, escaped count and escape statistics. It lists individual paths only when there are at most 12. The plot repeats a selection of these quantities.

Beat 0 · Random steps add up as √N

A walker takes steps in three dimensions, each one in a random direction and of random length, with no memory of the steps before. The program sends out 100 such walkers for each of twelve walk lengths, from 2 steps to 4096, and averages how far they end up from where they started.

python main.py --seed 12 --display scaled_distance --max_steps 4096 --n_trials 100 \
  --step_distribution uniform

In the last row, 4096 steps produce a mean scaled distance of 63.0073, with a fitted log-log slope of 0.4964. Compare the rows at 256, 1024 and 4096 steps: four times the steps roughly doubles the endpoint distance. The scale divides each walk's net displacement by that walk's own mean step length.

Point to two rows in the table that support \(d\propto\sqrt N\). Does a single trajectory need to follow that relation exactly? The exact independent-step identity is \(\langle r_N^2\rangle=N\langle |s|^2\rangle\). The printed mean of scaled distance follows \(C\sqrt N\) only as a large-\(N\) approximation. In particular, a two-step row need not equal its large-\(N\) prediction.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 1 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 1 · Put uncertainty beside an average

An average of random numbers is itself random. Run the same experiment with a quarter as many trials, and then with ten times as many.

For comparison, the default settings are --n_trials 100.

python main.py --seed 12 --n_trials 25
python main.py --seed 12 --n_trials 1000

At 4096 steps the standard errors are 4.2617 (25 trials), 2.3473 (100), and 0.8231 (1000). The large-\(N\) predicted ratio to \(\sqrt N\) is 0.9591; measured rows scatter around it.

How many times did the standard error fall as trial count rose from 25 to 1000? Compare that finite-sample answer with the ideal scaling \(\mathrm{SE}=s/\sqrt n\), where \(s\) is the measured spread and \(n\) is the number of trials. More trials narrow uncertainty but cannot remove the short-walk bias of the large-\(N\) formula.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 2 and Experiment 3 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 2 · Only the variance matters

The uniform steps of Beat 0 are one choice among many. Replace them with steps whose components follow the bell-shaped Gaussian distribution, which gives some very long steps and many short ones.

For comparison, the default settings are --step_distribution uniform.

python main.py --seed 12 --step_distribution gaussian --n_trials 100

The fitted slope is 0.4986, close to 0.4964 for uniform components. The large-\(N\) constant changes from 0.9591 to 1.0000. The two last-row means, 63.0073 and 65.1655, differ by less than their combined standard error.

Which conclusion concerns the exponent and which concerns the constant? Independent, zero-mean steps with finite variance give the square-root scaling. The step distribution and the definition of scaled distance set its prefactor. Uniform components fill a cube; they are not direction-isotropic, unlike the steps used in the star picture.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 4 and Experiment 5 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 3 · A photon in a schematic star

Now picture the walk. A photon starts at the centre of a circle, the schematic star, and takes steps of one fixed length in directions chosen with no preference, until it crosses the edge. The program draws four such walks.

python main.py --display walk2d --seed 12 --n_walks 4 --mean_free_path 1 --reference_steps 2000 \
  --radius_factor 2 --step_cap 200000

The default radius is 89.4427 step-length units, so \((R/\lambda)^2=8000.0\). The four paths escape after 11564, 7519, 8246 and 2479 steps; their mean is 7452.0 with standard error 1876.9. A path ends at the circle's exit point, and its outward ray is only a display convention.

Why do four independent paths give such a noisy mean? In this display each step has fixed length \(\lambda\) and a uniform random angle. The default radius follows \(R=f\lambda\sqrt{N_{\rm ref}}\) with \(f=2\) and \(N_{\rm ref}=2000\); --reference_steps sizes the circle and does not cap a walk.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 7 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 4 · Escape takes (R/λ)² steps

Use a smaller circle and 200 walkers so the sample mean is more useful.

For comparison, the default settings are --n_walks 4.

python main.py --display walk2d --radius 30 --n_walks 200 --seed 12 --mean_free_path 1 --step_cap 200000

The geometric scale is \((R/\lambda)^2=900.0\). All 200 escape; the sampled mean is 902.7 with standard error 44.7, and the median is 742.5. Individual counts are suppressed when there are more than 12 walks, but the summary keeps their statistics.

Is the measured mean consistent with the theoretical expectation between 900 and 961 steps? Account for its standard error. The bound applies to the expected uncapped escape count: \((R/\lambda)^2\leq\langle N_{\rm escape}\rangle<(R/\lambda+1)^2\). A finite sample mean can fall outside the bound by chance; capped walks are excluded from the printed mean.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 8 and Experiment 10 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 5 · Twice the radius, four times the steps

Run the 200 walks of Beat 4 again in a star of half the radius.

python main.py --display walk2d --radius 15 --n_walks 200 --seed 12 --mean_free_path 1
python main.py --display walk2d --radius 30 --n_walks 200 --seed 12 --mean_free_path 1

The mean escape counts are 227.8 ± 10.5 and 902.7 ± 44.7, a ratio of about 3.96. The ideal large-radius comparison tends toward four, rather than two, because the walker repeatedly changes direction.

Check the two printed \((R/\lambda)^2\) values, 225.0 and 900.0. How much uncertainty should you assign to the observed ratio? The radius-dependent one-step overshoot and Monte Carlo error matter for these finite circles.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 9 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 6 · Only R/λ matters

Change the step length and leave the radius to the default rule of Beat 3, which scales it with the step length.

For comparison, the default settings are --mean_free_path 1.

python main.py --display walk2d --mean_free_path 0.5 --seed 12 --reference_steps 2000 --radius_factor 2 \
  --n_walks 4
python main.py --display walk2d --mean_free_path 2 --seed 12 --reference_steps 2000 --radius_factor 2 \
  --n_walks 4

The radii are 44.7214 and 178.8854; Beat 3 gave 89.4427 at step length 1. In all three runs \(R/\lambda=2\sqrt{2000}\), \((R/\lambda)^2=8000.0\), and the same seed gives counts 11564, 7519, 8246 and 2479. The axes change physical scale.

Why do the counts match despite the different radii? The same angular draws give the same paths in step-length units. The exact count identity here uses binary-exact scale factors 0.5 and 2; other factors can differ at a boundary because of floating-point rounding.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 6 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 7 · The step cap

A walk could in principle go on for ever, so the program stops any walk that has not escaped after --step_cap steps (200000 by default). Set the cap to the mean escape time of Beat 4, and then to three times it.

For comparison, the default settings are --step_cap 200000.

python main.py --display walk2d --radius 30 --n_walks 200 --step_cap 900 --seed 12 --mean_free_path 1
python main.py --display walk2d --radius 30 --n_walks 200 --step_cap 3000 --seed 12 --mean_free_path 1

At cap 900, 130 of 200 escape; their mean is 515.7. At cap 3000, 196 escape; their mean is 837.5. With the generous default, all 200 escape and their mean is 902.7. A stopped path ends in an x and has no outward ray.

Why is 515.7 not an estimate of the uncapped mean near 900? The printed escape statistics omit stopped walks, preferentially discarding slow paths. Read both escaped walks and step cap before interpreting the mean.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 8 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 8 · Seeds and the hard cases

Every beat so far used --seed 12. Run the experiment of Beat 0 with another seed and then with none. The two commands below are valid; the separate one-step input check at the end of this beat is rejected.

python main.py --seed 28
python main.py

At seed 28 the last-row mean is 62.2140 with standard error 2.5542, versus 63.0073 ± 2.3473 at seed 12. The difference is only 0.23 of their combined standard error \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_1^2+\mathrm{SE}_2^2}\). The unseeded summary says its results differ from run to run.

How would you decide whether two independent random estimates disagree meaningfully? Try python main.py --max_steps 1: this command is expected to fail because the minimum is 2. A rejected input is different from a random difference between valid runs.

Suggested Experiments

The experiments are ranked from introductory to advanced, and each is cited by the beat it extends. Each command is run from the program's folder. Leave out --seed to see how much the results change from run to run.

1 · Introductory — Verify the √N Law

Run the default experiment several times without a seed. Check that the ratio column stays near the large-\(N\) prediction, and that the plotted points lie close to a line of slope 1/2, one vertical decade for two horizontal decades.

python main.py --display scaled_distance --max_steps 4096 --n_trials 100

2 · Introductory — Reduce Statistical Scatter

Repeat the calculation with more and more trials. Record the standard error of the last row each time and check that it falls roughly as one over the square root of the number of trials. Notice the trade-off between precision and run time.

python main.py --n_trials 25
python main.py --n_trials 100
python main.py --n_trials 500
python main.py --n_trials 1000

3 · Introductory to Intermediate — Measure the Log-Log Slope by Hand

Take two well-separated rows of the table and compute the exponent \(\alpha\) in \(\langle d\rangle\propto N^\alpha\) from them, as \(\log(d_2/d_1)/\log(N_2/N_1)\). Compare it with the printed fitted slope, which uses all the rows, and repeat with more trials.

python main.py --max_steps 4096 --n_trials 500

4 · Intermediate — Change the Step Distribution

Compare uniform and Gaussian step components with the same seed. The exponent stays at 1/2 and only the constant changes, from 0.9591 to 1. This is one of Schutz's suggested modifications.

python main.py --step_distribution uniform --seed 12
python main.py --step_distribution gaussian --seed 12

5 · Intermediate Programming — Change the Definition of Scaled Distance

Test whether the square-root law depends on dividing by each walk's mean step length. In a separate copy of the program, average net_distance instead of net_distance / mean_step in _perform_trials_3d_with_error(). The constant changes, to \(2\sqrt{2/(3\pi)}\simeq0.9213\) for uniform components and \(2\sqrt{2/\pi}\simeq1.5958\) for unit-variance Gaussian components, but the exponent stays near 1/2.

6 · Intermediate — Mean Free Path and Geometric Scaling

Leave --radius out and change --mean_free_path. With the same seed and the binary-exact factors 0.5 and 2 used here, the escape step counts match (Beat 6); without one, compare the statistics of many walks.

python main.py --display walk2d --mean_free_path 0.5
python main.py --display walk2d --mean_free_path 1.0
python main.py --display walk2d --mean_free_path 2.0

7 · Intermediate — Individual Walks and Statistical Variety

Display a few walks at a time and compare their shapes and escape step counts. Individual paths look very different although they follow the same rules. Keep the number small enough that the paths can be told apart.

python main.py --display walk2d --n_walks 4
python main.py --display walk2d --n_walks 6
python main.py --display walk2d --n_walks 10

8 · Intermediate to Advanced — Explore the Escape-Time Distribution

Run many walks in a small star and compare the mean, median, minimum and maximum escape step counts. Then lower the step cap until some walks are stopped, and watch the mean of the escaped walks fall (Beat 7).

python main.py --display walk2d --radius 20 --n_walks 500
python main.py --display walk2d --radius 20 --n_walks 500 --step_cap 400

9 · Advanced — Verify Escape Steps ∝ R²

Hold the mean free path fixed and use several radii. For each, divide the mean escape step count by \((R/\lambda)^2\), which the summary prints as mean escape steps / (R / mean free path)^2. The expected uncapped mean, divided by this scale, lies between 1 and \((1+\lambda/R)^2\) (Beat 4); a finite sample mean can fall outside that interval by chance; check it, using the standard error, and check that every walk escaped. Then repeat one radius with --mean_free_path 2: only \(R/\lambda\) matters (Beat 6).

python main.py --display walk2d --radius 30 --mean_free_path 1 --n_walks 100
python main.py --display walk2d --radius 40 --mean_free_path 1 --n_walks 100
python main.py --display walk2d --radius 50 --mean_free_path 1 --n_walks 100
python main.py --display walk2d --radius 60 --mean_free_path 1 --n_walks 100
python main.py --display walk2d --radius 60 --mean_free_path 2 --n_walks 100

10 · Advanced — A Photon's Escape from the Sun

Take a solar radius of \(7\times10^{8}\) m and suppose, very roughly, that the photon mean free path were 1 cm everywhere. How many steps would escape take, and how long would that be if each step took \(\lambda/c\)? Then say which assumptions of the estimate are the weakest, using the limitations in the Overview: the mean free path changes enormously with depth, and the outer part of the Sun carries its energy by convection. Treat the answer only as an order of magnitude.

Command-line parameters

All ordinary settings are command-line options described in main.py. Options for the display that is not selected may be given, but only the selected display uses them. Omitted options return to their defaults on each run.

ParameterDefaultDescription
--displayscaled_distancescaled_distance runs the three-dimensional statistics of Beats 0 to 2; walk2d draws the schematic star of Beats 3 to 7.
--max_steps4096Longest walk in scaled_distance, at least 2; the shorter walks are found by repeated halving. Alias --maxSteps.
--n_trials100Independent walks averaged for each row; the standard error falls approximately as the inverse square root of the trial count. Alias --nTrials.
--step_distributionuniformuniform draws each component from \([-1,1]\); gaussian from the standard normal distribution (Beat 2).
--reference_steps2000\(N_{\rm ref}\) in the default-radius rule \(R=f\lambda\sqrt{N_{\rm ref}}\). It sizes the drawing and does not limit the walk.
--n_walks4Number of star walks; they are listed one per line in the summary when there are at most 12.
--mean_free_path1.0Step length \(\lambda\) of every star walk, in arbitrary units.
--radius_factor2.0\(f\) in the default-radius rule \(R=f\lambda\sqrt{N_{\rm ref}}\).
--radiusomittedExplicit positive star radius; when omitted, the default-radius rule above sets it.
--ray_length_factor0.6Length of the outgoing ray as a fraction of the radius; 0 draws no ray.
--step_cap200000Largest number of steps in one star walk; a walk that reaches it is stopped and not counted as escaped (Beat 7).
--cornerupper_rightPosition of the annotation box on the star plot: upper_right, upper_left, lower_right or lower_left.
--seedomittedNonnegative integer that makes a run repeatable; when omitted, every run uses different random numbers (Beat 8).

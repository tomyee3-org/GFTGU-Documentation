Overview

Binary simulates two point masses interacting only through Newtonian gravity. The masses, initial positions, and initial velocities are all editable, so the same program can represent bound binaries, extreme mass-ratio systems, and unbound gravitational encounters.

The program integrates the coupled equations of motion with an Euler predictor and an iterated trapezoidal corrector. It records both bodies' positions and velocities together with the system's kinetic, gravitational potential, and total mechanical energy.

By default the calculation stops after the relative position vector has completed one revolution. Use --no-stop_after_one_orbit when you want a long integration for energy-conservation tests or when studying an unbound orbit. In all cases max_steps remains a safety ceiling.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: one force law gives both bodies their accelerations, exact motion under it conserves energy, and the printed energy is how you tell how far to trust a step-by-step calculation of that motion. Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument, a command whose printed summary or figure illustrates that argument, and then the equation that the printed numbers follow from.

Scope: Binary is a Newtonian two-body program. Multiple extends the Newtonian calculation to many gravitating bodies, while RelativisticOrbit treats relativistic orbital motion later in the book.
What this program is, and what it does not model. Binary is a teaching model of two Newtonian point masses in a plane. It has no tides, collision physics, relativity or third-body effects. The motion is computed step by step with a predictor and a corrector, so it carries a numerical error that is largest at close approach, and a very close encounter can end with an error message when the step needed becomes smaller than the program's safety floor. The one-revolution stop is a geometric rule about the angle swept by the line joining the bodies, not a proof that the path has closed. Use it to see how orbits depend on mass, speed and separation, and to learn how to judge a numerical result. Beats 5 to 7 show how, and where, the numbers stop being trustworthy.

How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation that the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equation at work, not independent evidence that the equation is right. The one outside check the program offers is the pair of exact two-body results that it prints for the starting conditions, the Keplerian elements. Beats 4 and 7 compare the integrated orbit with them.

Every beat uses the same program and the same printed summary. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Beat 0 runs the default pair, Beat 1 gives the bodies different masses, Beat 2 moves and boosts the whole pair, Beat 3 changes the speed until the orbit is no longer bound, Beat 4 reads the shape of an orbit from the summary, and Beats 5 to 7 open up the numerical method: the corrector, the step control, and how far to trust the result.

Run every command from the program's folder. Each run prints a summary and then opens a plot window; close the window to end the program. Each beat names the lines of the summary and the rows of the energy table to look at. In several beats the printed numbers are what to study and the plot can be left until later. Lengths are in metres, speeds in metres per second and energies in joules; times in the summary are in days.

What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag. A red LAW tag marks a law of nature that the model adopts and does not derive; Eq. (1) is the only one. A green ODE tag marks a differential equation that the program integrates. A grey DEFINITION tag marks a quantity that is defined by a formula. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows from the equations already stated. A violet ALGORITHM tag marks a rule of the numerical method, which is a choice made by the program rather than physics: Eqs. (8) to (10) and the one-revolution stopping rule. The tags are there so that a law, a definition and a numerical rule are never mistaken for one another. Ten equations are numbered; a few unnumbered ones are derived where they are needed.

Beat 0 · Two bodies, one force

Two bodies, each with about the mass of the Sun (2×1030 kg), start 9.2×1010 m apart on opposite sides of the origin. Each is given a sideways speed of 13 km s−1, in opposite directions, and then left alone with nothing to act on it but the other's gravity. Ask the program what happens.

python main.py

Look at the orbits plot and at the six summary lines from Accepted steps to Maximum separation (sampled). Ignore the rest of the printout for now.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the two separations. The maximum, 9.2e+10 m, is the separation the bodies started with (2 × 4.6×1010 m), so they begin as far apart as they ever get. The minimum, 1.2127e+10 m, is 13% of that. The bodies swing in to about an eighth of their starting distance and back out again, so their paths cannot be circles. Then look at the plot: each body traces one closed curve, and the two curves are mirror images of each other through the origin. Then read how long the trip took. The line Termination: one relative revolution says that the program stopped when the line joining the bodies had turned once; Accepted steps: 2284 steps of 2000 s is 4.568×106 s, which is the Total time of 52.87 days. Before reading on, write down what the pull between two bodies should depend on, and how it should change if you doubled their separation.

Then the law that produced those curves. Each body pulls on the other with a force that grows with both masses and falls as the square of the distance between them.

(1)
Newton's law of gravitation LAW
\[ F=\frac{GM_AM_B}{r^2}, \qquad r=\sqrt{(x_A-x_B)^2+(y_A-y_B)^2}. \]

Here \(M_A\) and \(M_B\) are the masses (kg), \((x_A,y_A)\) and \((x_B,y_B)\) are the positions (m), and \(r\) is the separation. The program uses \(G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\). Following the notation used in the program, \(v=\dot x\) and \(u=\dot y\). The force is attractive, acts along the line joining the bodies, and is equally strong on both (Newton's third law). Doubling \(r\) cuts it to a quarter. Two point masses interacting only through Eq. (1) is the whole model: the equations of motion, the energy and the numerical method that follow are consequences of Eq. (1) and Newton's second law, or ways of computing what they imply.

The force depends on the two positions only through \(r\). That is a strong statement, and Beat 2 tests it. First, though, Beat 1 asks what a force of this kind does to bodies of different mass.

Beat 1 · Who moves, and how much

Equation (1) gives the same force on both bodies, pointing in opposite directions. But a force produces an acceleration in inverse proportion to the mass it acts on, so if the masses differ, the lighter body should be thrown about more. Make body A twice as heavy as body B, start them so that they circle a common point, and see who moves further.

python main.py --MA 2e30 --MB 1e30 --xInitA 3.0666666667e10 --xInitB -6.1333333333e10 \
  --uInitA 15550.5265 --uInitB -31101.053

Look at the two blocks of the summary that begin Body A about centre of mass and Body B about centre of mass, and at the orbits plot.

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the semi-major axes: 3.0666e+10 m for body A and 6.1332e+10 m for body B. Their ratio is 2.0000, the inverse of the mass ratio \(M_A/M_B=2\), so the heavier body's orbit is half the size. Multiply each by its own mass: 2×1030 × 3.0666×1010 and 1×1030 × 6.1332×1010 both come to 6.1332×1040 kg m. Then compare the speeds on the Speed at periapsis lines, 15551 m/s and 31102 m/s, which are also in the ratio 1 to 2, and the periods, which are the same, 143.41 days: the bodies go round together. Then look at the plot: two circles, one inside the other, both centred on the origin. The origin is the point they circle. It lies between them, twice as far from B as from A, and it is where the heavier body's smaller circle and the lighter body's larger circle balance.

Then Newton's second law. Write \(\Delta x=x_A-x_B\) and \(\Delta y=y_A-y_B\). The force on A points toward B, and the force on B points toward A. Dividing each force by the mass it acts on gives the accelerations, and the mass of the body being accelerated cancels:

(2)
Equations of motion, body A ODE
\[ \ddot x_A \;=\; -\frac{G\,M_B\,\Delta x}{r^3}, \qquad \ddot y_A \;=\; -\frac{G\,M_B\,\Delta y}{r^3} \]
(3)
Equations of motion, body B ODE
\[ \ddot x_B \;=\; +\frac{G\,M_A\,\Delta x}{r^3}, \qquad \ddot y_B \;=\; +\frac{G\,M_A\,\Delta y}{r^3} \]

These are four coupled second-order ODEs. Each body's acceleration is proportional to the other body's mass, so A accelerates half as fast as B here. The sign flip between bodies reflects Newton's third law: the force on B is equal and opposite to the force on A. These accelerations are computed by accelerations() in physics_binary.py and evaluated at every stage of the predictor–corrector loop (Beat 5). Multiply Eq. (2) by \(M_A\), Eq. (3) by \(M_B\) and add: the right-hand sides cancel, so \(M_A\mathbf a_A+M_B\mathbf a_B=0\). The mass-weighted mean position of the pair therefore moves without acceleration:

(4)
Centre of mass and its velocity DEFINITION
\[ \mathbf R_{\rm CM}= \frac{M_A\mathbf r_A+M_B\mathbf r_B}{M_A+M_B}, \qquad \mathbf V_{\rm CM}= \frac{M_A\mathbf v_A+M_B\mathbf v_B}{M_A+M_B}. \]

Because the system is isolated, \(\mathbf V_{\rm CM}\) is constant. With the default equal masses and equal-and-opposite velocities it is zero, so the centre of mass remains at the origin. Equal masses alone do not require \(\mathbf v_A=-\mathbf v_B\); that relation additionally requires zero centre-of-mass velocity. Measured from the centre of mass, body A is always the fraction \(M_B/(M_A+M_B)\) of the separation away, and body B the fraction \(M_A/(M_A+M_B)\). Here those are 1/3 and 2/3 of 9.2×1010 m, the two radii you read off. The printed Body A and Body B blocks are these fractions of the relative orbit, and the ratio of their sizes is the inverse of the mass ratio.

The complete system state at any instant is the eight-component vector \((x_A,\,y_A,\,v_A,\,u_A,\,x_B,\,y_B,\,v_B,\,u_B)\), stored in the BinaryState dataclass and advanced one accepted timestep at a time by integrate_binary(). Beat 2 asks what would change if the whole pair were moved, or set moving.

Beat 2 · Only the separation matters

Equation (1) contains only the separation \(r\), and Eqs. (2) and (3) contain only the difference \(\Delta x,\Delta y\). So where the pair sits in space, and how fast the pair as a whole drifts, should make no difference to how the two bodies move relative to one another. Test both: shift the pair, then set the whole pair moving.

python main.py --xInitA 5e10 --xInitB -4.2e10 --yInitA 1e10 --yInitB 1e10
python main.py --vInitA 60000 --vInitB 60000

In each run look at the lines from Accepted steps to Speed at apoapsis and compare them with the same lines in Beat 0. In the second run look also at row 0.0 of the energy table and at the orbits plot, in which the pair drifts.

Three tasks, in order. First, the shifted run: every number down to the Keplerian block is the one from Beat 0, which is 2284 steps, 52.87 days, a minimum separation of 1.2127e+10 m and eccentricity 0.76705. Only the plot has moved: the centre of mass is now at (4×109, 1×1010) m, which is where the pattern is centred. Then the second run: the same printed numbers again, but in the energy table the kinetic energy at row 0.0 is 7.538e+39 J instead of 3.38e+38 J. The difference, 7.2×1039 J, is \(\tfrac12(M_A+M_B)V^2\), which is \(\tfrac12\times4\times10^{30}\times(6\times10^4)^2\), the kinetic energy of the pair as a whole, and the potential energy, −2.9019e+39 J, has not changed. Then read the Total E column: it is +4.6361e+39 J, positive, although the orbit is exactly the same ellipse with eccentricity 0.76705, which is bound. Say why the sign of the printed total energy cannot tell you here whether the pair is bound. One printed number does change: row 0.5 of the last column reads −6.7536e-04 in this run and −1.2814e-03 in the first, because the same absolute change in \(E\), about \(3\times10^{36}\) J, is divided by a different \(|E_0|\).

Then the reason. Add the same vector \(\mathbf d\) to both positions and \(r\) is unchanged. Add the same velocity \(\mathbf V_0\) to both bodies and the motion \(\mathbf r_i(t)\to\mathbf r_i(t)+\mathbf V_0t\) satisfies the same Eqs. (2) and (3), because \(\mathbf V_0t\) has no acceleration. The bodies' motion relative to one another does not change; only the kinetic energy does, by exactly the amount of the motion of the whole:

Kinetic energy of a pair whose centre of mass moves DERIVED
\[ K \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,(M_A+M_B)\,V_{\rm CM}^{\,2} \;+\; K_{\rm CM\text{-}frame} \]

Only the relative separation controls the mutual force. Translating both positions by the same vector therefore leaves the relative orbit unchanged, and adding the same constant velocity to both bodies produces a Galilean boost without changing their motion relative to one another. The energy the program prints uses the velocities in the frame you supply, so it includes the first term above. The energy that says whether the orbit is bound is the second term, measured in the frame in which the centre of mass is at rest (Beat 3).

In the program the boost invariance holds to the accuracy of the integration, not to rounding error. The corrector's convergence test (Beat 5) compares each body's change in velocity with that body's own speed, and a boost changes those speeds, larger for one body and possibly smaller for the other, so the test is applied differently. For the boost above the corrector repeats in 192 steps instead of 127, and the two relative paths agree to about 5 parts in 106 of the starting separation. For a boost of 106 m s−1, far faster than any orbital speed here, they differ by about 7 parts in 104. For a boost of 500 m s−1, as in Experiment 3, the corrector makes the same decisions as without it, and the two paths differ by about 2 parts in 1015, which is rounding error.

Beat 3 · Energy separates bound from unbound

The force of Eq. (1) can be derived from a potential energy, so exact motion under it conserves the total energy, the sum of the kinetic and potential energies. The sign of that total, measured in the frame in which the centre of mass is at rest, says whether the bodies can ever separate to infinity. Run three orbits that differ only in the starting speed, and read the three energies the program prints.

python main.py --output_type energy_vs_time
python main.py --uInitA 38091.14 --uInitB -38091.14 --no-stop_after_one_orbit --max_steps 1000 --output_type energy_vs_time
python main.py --uInitA 60000 --uInitB -60000 --no-stop_after_one_orbit --max_steps 1000 --output_type energy_vs_time

Look, in each run, at the Initial Keplerian orbit line, at row 0.0 of the energy table (Potential U, Kinetic K, Total E), and at the last column. The plot shows U, K and E against time. The second and third runs stop at max_steps because their commands turn the one-revolution stop off; an unbound orbit would never complete a turn anyway (Beat 4).

Three tasks, in order. First, read row 0.0 in all three runs. The potential energy is −2.9019e+39 J in each, as it must be: the starting separation is the same, and \(-GM_AM_B/r\) is \(-6.6743\times10^{-11}\times(2\times10^{30})^2\) divided by \(9.2\times10^{10}\). The kinetic energy differs: 3.38e+38, 2.9019e+39 and 7.2e+39 J, for speeds of 13000, 38091.14 and 60000 m s−1 for each body. Add them: the totals are −2.5639e+39, +3.2778e+32 and +4.2981e+39 J. Then compare each total with the Initial Keplerian orbit line: the negative total is elliptic with eccentricity 0.76705, and the large positive total is hyperbolic with eccentricity 3.9623. The middle run has a total of only 1.1×10−7 of its kinetic energy. It starts at the escape speed rounded to seven digits, so the program calls it hyperbolic, with an eccentricity that prints as 1 (it is 1.0000002). Then look at the last column, (E-E0)/|E0|. In the bound run it is 3.2 parts in 106 at the end. In the middle run it grows to 4.0631, that is 406%, yet Total E has changed by only 1.3×1033 J, which is 4.6×10−7 of the kinetic energy. Say why a change of that size in a total that is nearly zero gives such a large printed number.

Then the three energies. Each body has kinetic energy, and the pair has a mutual potential energy.

(5)
Kinetic energy DEFINITION
\[ K \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,M_A\!\left(v_A^2 + u_A^2\right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,M_B\!\left(v_B^2 + u_B^2\right) \]
(6)
Gravitational potential energy DERIVED
\[ U \;=\; -\frac{G\,M_A\,M_B}{r} \]
(7)
Total mechanical energy DEFINITION
\[ E \;=\; K + U \]

Equation (6) follows from Eq. (1): \(-U\) is the work needed to pull the bodies apart to infinite separation, so \(U\) is zero at infinity and negative at any finite separation. A pair with \(E<0\) has too little kinetic energy to reach infinity and stays bound. A pair with \(E>0\) escapes. \(E=0\) is the marginal, parabolic case. In the centre-of-mass frame, a gravitationally bound orbit has \(E<0\), and the total mechanical energy is conserved. Any systematic drift in \(E\) over the run is one important diagnostic of accumulated numerical error, although a small energy error does not by itself bound the error in the positions, the period or the phase.

The program accurately sums the two separately computed kinetic-energy terms and the potential-energy term together. This preserves a small representable residual when much larger positive and negative terms nearly cancel. Exact zero remains valid when the floating-point component values cancel exactly.

The plotted energy uses the velocities in the chosen inertial frame and therefore includes any kinetic energy of the centre of mass. When using the sign of \(E\) to classify the internal orbit as bound or unbound, keep the centre of mass at rest or subtract its translational kinetic energy. Two cautions about reading the energy output. The energy plot shows the exchange between \(K\) and \(U\) at a glance, and \(E\) as a nearly flat line; departures as small as those in Beats 5 to 7 are far too small to see against the scale of \(K\) and \(U\), so the printed (E-E0)/|E0| column is the quantitative diagnostic. And that column divides by \(|E_0|\), so it is meaningful only when \(E_0\) is not small compared with \(K\) and \(U\): for a nearly parabolic start, compare the change in \(E\) with \(K\) instead.

Beat 4 · Reading the shape of an orbit

Beat 3 sorted orbits by the sign of the energy. The summary says more than that. From the starting positions and velocities alone, before any integration, the program works out the exact two-body orbit that those starting conditions define, and prints its size, shape and period as the Keplerian block. That block is the one outside check this page has, because it comes from formulas and not from the step-by-step calculation. Read it for two orbits that start with the same masses and the same separation, a circle and the default ellipse, and compare each with what the integration actually did.

python main.py --uInitA 26934.5 --uInitB -26934.5 --output_type velocity_space
python main.py --output_type velocity_space

Look at Minimum separation (sampled) and Maximum separation (sampled), at Initial Keplerian orbit and the Body A about centre of mass block, and at Total revolutions and Total time. Then look at the velocity-space plot, in which each body's velocity \((v,u)\) is a point that moves as the bodies orbit.

Three tasks, in order. First, the circular run. The minimum and maximum separations are both 9.2e+10 m, so the distance never changes. The Keplerian block agrees: the eccentricity is 1.393e-07, which is zero apart from the rounding of the starting speed, periapsis and apoapsis are both 4.6e+10 m, and the two speeds, 26935 and 26934 m/s, differ only by that same rounding. The period is 124.2 days, and the run took 5366 steps of 2000 s, which is the Total time of 124.21 days. Then the default run. The separation swings between 1.2127e+10 and 9.2e+10 m and the eccentricity is 0.76705. Body A's periapsis is 6.0643e+09 m, where it moves at 98610 m/s, and its apoapsis is 4.6e+10 m, where it moves at 13000 m/s. Multiply each distance by the speed at that distance: the product is 5.980×1014 m2 s−1 at periapsis and 5.980×1014 at apoapsis, where the body is 7.585 times farther away and 7.585 times slower. Then compare the integration with the formulas. The Keplerian period is 52.874 days and the run stopped after 52.87 days. Each body's Keplerian periapsis is 6.0643×109 m from the centre of mass, so at closest approach the bodies are 2 × 6.0643×109 ≈ 1.2129×1010 m apart, and the sampled minimum separation from the integration, 1.2127e+10 m, is about 0.015% below that (from the unrounded values; the rounded digits printed here give about 0.013%). Then look at the plot. The circular run gives one circle of radius 26934.5 m/s about the origin, because the two velocities are opposite and trace the same curve. The default run gives two circles, one for each body. Each has a radius of 55805 m/s, half of 98610 + 13000, and its centre is 42805 m/s from the origin, half of 98610 − 13000, so the origin lies inside each circle. Divide the second number by the first: 42805/55805 = 0.76705, the eccentricity.

Then why these numbers are tied together. Measured from the centre of mass, each body lies on the line to the other body, so the force of Eq. (1) points along the body's position vector. A force along the position vector exerts no torque about the centre of mass, so it cannot change the angular momentum per unit mass of each body about the centre of mass, \(\ell\), which is therefore constant. It is the cross product of the body's position and velocity measured from the centre of mass. At periapsis and apoapsis the velocity is perpendicular to the position, so \(\ell\) is simply distance times speed at those two points:

Angular momentum per unit mass, body A about the centre of mass DERIVED
\[ \ell_A \;=\; (\mathbf r_A-\mathbf R_{\rm CM})\times(\mathbf v_A-\mathbf V_{\rm CM}) \;=\; \text{constant}, \qquad r_p\,v_p \;=\; r_a\,v_a \;=\; \ell_A \]

In the two commands above the centre of mass is at the origin and at rest, so \(\ell_A=x_Au_A-y_Av_A\). The relation \(r_p v_p=r_a v_a\) is the first relation you checked: the body is fastest where it is closest. Second, the period depends only on the size of the relative orbit. The semi-major axis \(a\) of the relative orbit is the sum of the two printed body semi-major axes, 2.6032e+10 + 2.6032e+10 = 5.2064e+10 m for the default orbit and 9.2e+10 m for the circle, and Kepler's third law gives the period:

Kepler's third law DERIVED
\[ T \;=\; 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{G\,(M_A+M_B)}}, \qquad a \;=\; a_A + a_B \]

which is 52.874 days for the default orbit and 124.198 days for the circle, which the block prints as 124.2. Third, the distances at the two apsides are \(a(1-e)\) and \(a(1+e)\), so the angular-momentum relation gives \(v_p/v_a=(1+e)/(1-e)\): the two printed speeds, which are measured relative to the centre of mass, fix the eccentricity. In velocity space they fix a circle, the hodograph, whose diameter is \(v_p+v_a\) and whose centre is \((v_p-v_a)/2\) from the origin:

The velocity-space circle DERIVED
\[ R_v \;=\; \tfrac12\,(v_p+v_a), \qquad d_v \;=\; \tfrac12\,(v_p-v_a), \qquad e \;=\; \frac{d_v}{R_v} \;=\; \frac{v_p-v_a}{v_p+v_a} \]

The origin of velocity space lies inside the circle when \(e<1\), on it when \(e=1\) and outside it when \(e>1\), so, when the centre of mass is at rest, a glance at the velocity-space plot tells you whether an orbit is bound. The plot shows the velocities in the frame you supply. Adding a common velocity \(\mathbf V_0\) to both bodies moves every point of the hodograph by \(\mathbf V_0\) (Beat 2 and Experiment 3), so with a moving centre of mass the origin test applies only after \(\mathbf V_{\rm CM}\) has been subtracted, and a large enough boost puts the origin outside the circle of a bound orbit. Finally, the line Total revolutions is the angle that the line joining the bodies has swept, divided by \(2\pi\). The program adds up the signed angle between the relative position at one accepted step and the next, and stops the first time the total reaches a full turn:

One-revolution stopping rule ALGORITHM
\[ \Theta \;=\; \sum_n \Delta\theta_n, \qquad \Delta\theta_n \;=\; \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(\mathbf r_n\times\mathbf r_{n+1},\;\mathbf r_n\cdot\mathbf r_{n+1}\right), \qquad \text{stop when } |\Theta|\ge 2\pi \]

Here \(\mathbf r_n=\mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B\) at accepted step \(n\). The test is made after each accepted step, so the run can end a little past the full turn. That is why the circular run reports 1.0001 revolutions, the last 2000 s step having carried it past the turn, while the default run's overshoot is too small to show in five figures. It is a rule about geometry and not about the orbit closing (see Algorithm).

Beat 5 · The corrector

The equations of motion, Eqs. (2) and (3), do have an exact solution for two point masses: each body moves on a conic section, and the Keplerian block of the summary is computed from that solution. Binary deliberately integrates the equations step by step instead, so that you can see a method that also works for systems with no such solution, and the Keplerian block then serves as an outside check on the integration. The program advances the bodies in small steps of time. To take one step it needs the accelerations at the end of the step, but the end positions depend on those accelerations. The program's way out has two stages. An Euler predictor makes a first estimate of where the bodies will be at the end of the step, and a trapezoidal corrector uses the average of the acceleration at the start and at the end to improve it. The improved positions give a better end acceleration, so the corrector can be repeated. The option eps2 decides when to stop repeating. Run the default problem with a very strict eps2 and then with one so loose that the corrector never repeats.

python main.py --eps2 1e-8
python main.py --eps2 0.5

Look at Accepted steps and Total time, and at rows 0.5 and 1.0 of the last column of the energy table. Row 0.5 is half way through the run; every run here starts at apoapsis, so that row falls at or near closest approach. The default run, eps2 = 1e-4, gives −1.2814e-03 in row 0.5 and −3.1646e-06 in row 1.0. Row 0.5 is the row at closest approach, where the bodies move fastest and the acceleration changes fastest.

Three tasks, in order. First, read Accepted steps: 2284 for the strict run and 2285 for the loose one, against 2284 for the default. The tolerance changed by a factor of 5×107 and the number of steps hardly changed. The strict run's Total time is 52.87 days, which is 2284 steps of 2000 s, and the loose run's is 52.894 days, which is 2285 steps of 2000 s. The option eps2 does not set the step size. Then read row 0.5: −1.1854e-03 for the strict run, −1.2814e-03 for the default and −1.784e-03 for the loose run. In all three the total energy has dipped by 0.1% to 0.2% at closest approach, and the loosest corrector makes the dip only 1.5 times as deep as the strictest. Then read row 1.0, the end of the orbit: 3.2091e-12 for the strict run, −3.1646e-06 for the default and 8.1292e-05 for the loose one. After one orbit the strict run's energy is back to within 3 parts in 1012 of its starting value, the default run's to 3 parts in 106, and the loose run ends 8 parts in 105 above it. So eps2 does control something, but not the depth of the dip. Say what it controls, and why the energy might come back after closest approach with a strict corrector and not with a loose one.

Then the two stages. The predictor is a straight-line (Euler) estimate of where each body will be, and how fast it will be moving, at the end of a step of length \(\Delta t\), using the acceleration at the start:

(8)
Euler predictor ALGORITHM
\[ \tilde{\mathbf r}_A=\mathbf r_A^n+\mathbf v_A^n\Delta t, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf v}_A=\mathbf v_A^n+\mathbf a_A^n\Delta t, \]

with equivalent equations for body B. The program evaluates the accelerations at the predicted positions and then applies the trapezoidal corrector, which replaces the acceleration over the step by the average of its values at the two ends:

(9)
Iterated trapezoidal corrector ALGORITHM
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf v_A^{n+1} &= \mathbf v_A^n + \frac{\mathbf a_A^n+\mathbf a_A^{n+1}}{2}\,\Delta t,\\[2pt] \mathbf r_A^{n+1} &= \mathbf r_A^n + \frac{\mathbf v_A^n+\mathbf v_A^{n+1}}{2}\,\Delta t. \end{aligned} \]

The end acceleration \(\mathbf a^{n+1}\) depends on the end position \(\mathbf r^{n+1}\), which is what Eq. (9) is computing. The program therefore starts with the acceleration at the predicted position, computes the new velocity and position, and asks whether the velocity has moved by more than eps2 since the previous estimate. If it has, it recomputes the acceleration at the improved position and applies Eq. (9) again, up to ten passes in all:

Corrector convergence test ALGORITHM
\[ \delta_A^{(k)} \;=\; \frac{\left\lVert\mathbf v_A^{(k)}-\mathbf v_A^{(k-1)}\right\rVert} {\max\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf v_A^{(k)}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf v_A^{(k-1)}\right\rVert\right)}, \qquad \text{accept when } \max\!\left(\delta_A^{(k)},\delta_B^{(k)}\right)\lt\varepsilon_2 \]

where \(\mathbf v^{(0)}\) is the predictor's velocity and the same test is made for body B. The test compares successive estimates of the velocity within one step. It is not a tolerance on the error of the orbit, the period or the energy. On the default orbit it is usually satisfied by the very first pass, because the predicted and corrected velocities differ by less than 1 part in 104. The default run repeats the correction in 127 of its 2284 steps, the steps 1080 to 1206 around closest approach and no others. The strict run repeats it in every step, 2878 repetitions in all, and the loose run never does. Counting the calls that the driver makes to accelerations(), the default run makes 4696, the strict run 7447 and the loose run 4571. A strict corrector costs about 1.6 times as much here.

What a repeated correction buys is a step that is closer to solving Eq. (9) exactly. Solved exactly, Eq. (9) is unchanged if the step is run backward in time (\(\Delta t\to-\Delta t\) and the two ends swapped). For a rule with that symmetry, and an orbit that starts at apoapsis, where the motion is its own mirror image, the second half of the orbit retraces the first, so the error made as the bodies plunge toward closest approach is largely undone as they climb away. That is the likely reason why row 1.0 of the strict run returns to 3 parts in 1012. A corrector that stops early does not have the symmetry, and its error does not return. The size of the dip in row 0.5, on the other hand, is a property of the step size, which is the subject of Beats 6 and 7. If ten passes are not enough to satisfy eps2, the program halves the step and tries again. In the runs of Beats 5 to 7 the corrector always converges within ten passes.

The program never takes a step longer than dt, and it takes a shorter one when the acceleration is changing too fast during a step for the predictor to be trusted. Then the rule. Before the corrector runs, the program compares the acceleration of body A at the start of the trial step, \(\mathbf a_A^n\), with the acceleration \(\tilde{\mathbf a}_A\) at the position that the Euler predictor of Eq. (8) gives for the end of the step. Since \(M_A\mathbf a_A=-M_B\mathbf a_B\) (Beat 1), the fractional change of body B's acceleration is the same, so testing body A is sufficient:

(10)
Acceleration-change test ALGORITHM
\[ \Delta_a= \frac{\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}_A-\mathbf a_A^n\right\rVert} {\max\!\left(\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}_A\right\rVert, \left\lVert\mathbf a_A^n\right\rVert\right)}. \]

If \(\Delta_a>\varepsilon_1\), the attempt is rejected and the working timestep is halved. After an accepted step, the working timestep is allowed to grow by 10% toward the user-specified ceiling dt. If repeated halving would drive the working timestep below a tiny numerical safety floor, the program stops with a clear error instead of continuing with effectively zero time advance. The floor is dt × 10−12, which a step falls below at the fortieth halving in a row.

In the default run the largest \(\Delta_a\) over the whole orbit is 0.0325, at closest approach, which is below \(\varepsilon_1\)=0.05, so no trial is ever rejected. At dt = 20000 the controller has to act. The step sizes in the 387-step run are 20000 s for the first 88 steps and then 10000, 11000, 12100, 13310, 14641, 16105 and 8858 s: the step is halved, grows by 10% a step until the test fails again, and is halved again. With eps1 = 0.5 the largest \(\Delta_a\) among the accepted steps is 0.318, a trial that the default value would have rejected. The predictor takes the acceleration at the start of the step as if it were constant, and the acceleration of a close pair changes quickly, so \(\Delta_a\) is the program's own measure of how good that assumption is. The extra steps are spent only where they are needed, around closest approach.

Algorithm

This section collects in one place the rules of the numerical method that Beats 4 to 7 introduced one at a time. Equations (8) and (9) are in Beat 5, Eq. (10) is in Beat 6 and the stopping rule is in Beat 4.

One accepted step

  1. Evaluate the accelerations of both bodies at the start of the step from Eqs. (2) and (3).
  2. Take a trial step of the working length Δt with the Euler predictor, Eq. (8), and evaluate the accelerations at the predicted positions.
  3. Form Δa from Eq. (10). If it exceeds eps1, halve Δt and return to step 2.
  4. Apply the trapezoidal corrector, Eq. (9), and repeat it until the velocity vectors of both bodies agree to eps2. If ten passes do not suffice, halve Δt and return to step 2.
  5. Accept the step and record the state and the energies of Eqs. (5) to (7). Add the angle swept by the relative vector to the running total, which is summed with compensation so that rounding error does not build up over many steps; if its magnitude has reached 2π and stop_after_one_orbit is on, stop. Otherwise let the working step grow by 10%, up to dt, and begin the next step.

Predictor–Corrector

The end-of-step acceleration is recomputed from the refined positions and the corrector is iterated. Convergence is tested from the dimensionless relative change of each body's velocity vector, rather than from individual Cartesian components.

If ten corrector iterations are insufficient to meet eps2, the timestep is halved and the same step is retried instead of silently accepting a non-converged result. eps2 is a convergence test within one trial step; it is not a direct tolerance on the global position, period, or energy error.

Adaptive Timestep

Before the corrector is run, the program compares body A's acceleration at the beginning of the step with the acceleration at the Euler-predicted position. Since the two bodies' accelerations are proportional in an isolated two-body system, their fractional changes are identical and testing one body is sufficient.

If \(\Delta_a>\varepsilon_1\), the attempt is rejected and the working timestep is halved. After an accepted step, the working timestep is allowed to grow by 10% toward the user-specified ceiling dt. If repeated halving would drive the working timestep below a tiny numerical safety floor, the program stops with a clear error instead of continuing with effectively zero time advance.

Interpretation: For sufficiently eccentric or rapidly varying trajectories this mechanism normally uses smaller steps near periapsis. With the default parameters no timestep reduction is needed, because the largest Δa over the orbit is 0.0325, below the default eps1 of 0.05; increasing the eccentricity or choosing a larger dt makes the controller easier to observe (Beat 6). The safety floor is dt × 10−12.

Termination

With stop_after_one_orbit = True, the program follows the angle swept by the relative vector \(\mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B\). Signed angle increments between successive accepted steps are accumulated. Each vector is scaled independently before its dot and cross products are formed, so the angle does not depend on coordinate magnitude. A non-finite orbit-angle diagnostic stops with a clear error rather than silently disabling this termination test. The calculation stops when their magnitude reaches \(2\pi\), provided this occurs before max_steps. This is a geometric stopping rule for one relative revolution; it is independent of the coordinate origin and does not by itself prove that the numerical trajectory is exactly closed.

Use --no-stop_after_one_orbit to continue for the full max_steps accepted steps. This is useful for unbound trajectories and long-term energy-conservation tests. A run can also end earlier with an error, for example when an inward radial start reaches the close-approach safety limit (Beat 7).

Safety limit: max_steps is always enforced. An unbound orbit will generally never satisfy the one-revolution condition.

Tolerances

Two independent dimensionless tolerances control the integration.

ParameterDefaultRole
eps1\(0.05\) Rejects a predictor step when the relative change in the acceleration vector is too large.
eps2\(10^{-4}\) Requires successive corrected velocity vectors to agree to this relative tolerance. Failure to converge after ten iterations causes the timestep to be halved and retried.

Tighter tolerances make the local acceptance and convergence tests more demanding and can require more computation. They do not act on the same error as dt: reducing dt lowers the dip in the total energy at closest approach roughly as the square of dt (Beat 7), while tightening eps2 lowers what remains of the departure at the end of the orbit in the runs of Beats 5 and 7. There is no universal factor relating a tolerance change to an energy-error change, so accuracy should be established by a convergence test.

Tip: If the total energy shows a noticeable dip or drift, first decide which it is (Beat 7). A dip at closest approach that returns by the end of the orbit responds mainly to a smaller dt. In the runs of Beat 7, a departure that remains at the end of the orbit responds to a smaller eps2, and the motion of a very eccentric orbit responds to a smaller eps1 (Experiment 8). Tightening any of these makes the tests more demanding, but the improvement is problem-dependent. The safest test is to repeat the same physical run with stricter numerical controls and check that the result changes less and less.

Program Files

main.py
Entry Point & Configuration

Accepts command-line initial conditions and simulation parameters — masses, starting positions, starting velocities, the maximum timestep dt, the safety limit max_steps, the tolerance thresholds eps1 and eps2, and stop_after_one_orbit. After calling integrate_binary() to run the simulation it prints the run summary (see Printed Summary) and passes the result to plot_binary() for display. All user-adjustable settings are available as command-line options.

physics_binary.py
Physics Engine

Defines the BinaryState dataclass, which stores the current time together with the positions \((x_A, y_A,\, x_B, y_B)\) and velocities \((v_A, u_A,\, v_B, u_B)\) of both bodies. Provides four functions: relative_displacement() returns the separation vector \((\Delta x,\,\Delta y)\) and scalar distance \(r\); accelerations() evaluates the Newtonian gravitational acceleration on each body; energies() computes the instantaneous kinetic, gravitational potential, and total mechanical energy of the system; and orbital_elements() computes the initial Keplerian orbit in the centre-of-mass frame from the starting masses, positions and velocities, and returns it as an OrbitalElements dataclass.

driver_binary.py
Integration Driver

Defines the BinaryResult dataclass (time-series lists of positions, velocities, and energies for both bodies, together with the number of accepted steps, whether the orbit was completed, the accumulated orbit angle, and the version and build that produced it) and the function integrate_binary(). The driver advances the system step by step using the Euler predictor followed by the averaged-acceleration corrector, with vector-based convergence testing and adaptive timestep control. It can stop after one relative revolution or continue to the max_steps safety ceiling. All numerical-method logic lives in this module.

plot_binary.py
Visualisation

Provides the plot_binary() function and the OutputType string literal type. Accepts a BinaryResult and renders one of seven distinct plots through seven command-line selectors (the plotter also accepts a legacy orbit alias) via matplotlib: orbital trajectories in the \(xy\)-plane (equal aspect ratio), velocity-space diagrams, position vs. time, or velocity vs. time for each body individually, and the full energy budget vs. time. All plots carry axis labels, titles, and legends.

A start is called parabolic when the relative speed matches the escape speed to about one part in 1012; a start only slightly above escape speed is hyperbolic even though its eccentricity prints as 1 (Experiment 6). A radial start, with zero angular momentum, prints an extra line saying that its apsides and period are undefined.
  • The blocks Body A about centre of mass and Body B about centre of mass give each body's semi-major axis, eccentricity, period, periapsis and apoapsis distances, and the speeds there. Each is the relative orbit scaled by the other body's share of the total mass (Beat 1). For a hyperbola the semi-major axis is signed and negative; apoapsis and period do not exist. Parabolic and radial cases also omit undefined elements, which print as undefined.
  • The energy table has eleven rows, from fraction 0.0 through 1.0 of the total simulated days, which linearly interpolate the stored potential, kinetic and total mechanical energies in joules between accepted states. The final column is signed (E-E0)/|E0|. If the initial total energy is exactly zero, that fractional departure is undefined and a note says so. Interpolation between numerical states is not an independent exact-energy calculation; inspect the reported departure and repeat with tighter numerical settings when needed. Eleven rows can miss a brief excursion such as the dip at closest approach, and the column loses its meaning when \(E_0\) is close to zero (Beat 3 and Experiment 8).

5 · Unequal Masses and the Centre of Mass — Intermediate

8 · Numerical Convergence and Energy Conservation — Advanced

python main.py --no-stop_after_one_orbit --dt 2000 --max_steps 7000 --output_type energy_vs_time
python main.py --no-stop_after_one_orbit --dt 1000 --max_steps 14000 --output_type energy_vs_time

Use --no-stop_after_one_orbit. For a reproducible three-period comparison using the default physical initial conditions, first use --dt 2000 --max_steps 7000; then repeat with --dt 1000 --max_steps 14000. Because the default case does not trigger adaptive reduction, both runs cover the same \(1.4\times10^7\) s interval. You can then repeat with tighter eps1/eps2. Compare the total-energy curves, for example by estimating the largest fractional deviation \(\max|E(t)-E(0)|/|E(0)|\). A trustworthy result should change less and less as the numerical controls are tightened.

Neither the plot nor the printed table can give that maximum. The plot shows \(E\) as an almost flat line, and the eleven rows of the table fall at multiples of 16.2 days, while the closest approaches are at days 26.4, 79.3 and 132.2. Read every stored state with a short script. Save it in the program's folder, for example as largest_departure.py, and run it with python largest_departure.py: 

from driver_binary import integrate_binary

def largest_departure(**changes):
    settings = dict(MA=2e30, MB=2e30,
                    xInitA=4.6e10, yInitA=0, vInitA=0, uInitA=13000,
                    xInitB=-4.6e10, yInitB=0, vInitB=0, uInitB=-13000,
                    dt=2000, max_steps=7000, eps1=0.05, eps2=1e-4,
                    stop_after_one_orbit=False)
    settings.update(changes)
    r = integrate_binary(**settings)
    return max(abs(e - r.E[0]) / abs(r.E[0]) for e in r.E)

print(largest_departure())
print(largest_departure(dt=1000, max_steps=14000))
print(largest_departure(dt=1000, max_steps=14000, eps2=1e-8))
print(largest_departure(uInitA=1000, uInitB=-1000, max_steps=400000, stop_after_one_orbit=True))
print(largest_departure(uInitA=1000, uInitB=-1000, max_steps=400000, stop_after_one_orbit=True, eps1=0.001))
print(largest_departure(uInitA=1000, uInitB=-1000, max_steps=400000, stop_after_one_orbit=True, eps1=0.001, eps2=1e-8))

The function starts from the default orbit, with the one-revolution stop off, and replaces only the settings you name. The first three lines of output are the three-orbit comparison. The first, for the default settings, is 1.2853e-03, reached at the third closest approach. With dt 1000 and max_steps 14000 it is 4.5163e-04, and with eps2 1e-8 as well it is 2.9623e-04, which is Beat 7's factor of 4 per halving of dt again. The printed Total revolutions is 3.0136 for the first run and 3.0133 for the second. The script prints each fraction to full precision, so the first line begins 0.00128. The last three lines are for the much more eccentric orbit tried next.

Then try an orbit much more eccentric than the default: 

python main.py --uInitA 1000 --uInitB -1000 --max_steps 400000
python main.py --uInitA 1000 --uInitB -1000 --max_steps 400000 --eps1 0.001

The eccentricity is 0.99862 and the Keplerian period is 43.956 days. With the default settings the run takes 2456 steps and stops after 43.231 days, and the fourth line of the script, which uses the same settings and the one-revolution stop that these commands leave on, reports a largest departure of 25.9%. With eps1 = 0.001 the run takes 43303 steps, stops after 43.97 days, and the largest departure, on the fifth line, is 2.0184e-04. Add --eps2 1e-8 as well and it falls to 1.0371e-04, the sixth line. The default eps1 was chosen for the default orbit; an orbit whose acceleration changes as violently as this needs a much smaller one (Beat 7).

Physical Background

Star models a spherically symmetric star in Newtonian gravity with a single polytropic equation of state. Starting from user-supplied central pressure and temperature, it integrates outward while tracking pressure, density, temperature, and enclosed mass until the modeled pressure reaches zero.

The model deliberately omits nuclear energy generation and explicit radiative or convective energy transport. Instead, pressure and density are constrained everywhere by one power law, while the ideal-gas relation is used to set the central density and to recover a temperature profile. This is the same deliberately simple physical picture emphasized in Schutz's Chapter 8 Star investigation.

Gravity is not constant inside a star. The gravitational acceleration at radius \(r\) is \(g(r)=Gm(r)/r^2\), where \(m(r)\) is the mass enclosed within that radius. Hydrostatic equilibrium and mass continuity must therefore be integrated together.

About the default "solar" model: The default parameters give approximately the observed total mass and radius of the Sun, but this should not be confused with a high-accuracy solar-interior model. A single polytrope cannot reproduce the Sun's layered equation of state, composition, energy generation, and energy transport. In particular, the default central temperature and density differ substantially from standard solar-model values. The parameter \(\mu=1.285\) is best regarded as an effective parameter of this simplified model, not as the actual mean molecular weight of the fully ionized solar core.

Governing Equations

Ideal Gas Law

The central density is tied to the supplied pressure, temperature, and effective mean molecular weight by

Ideal Gas Law  (1)
\[ p=\frac{\rho k_{\mathrm B}T}{m_p\mu}. \]

Defining \(q=m_p\mu/k_{\mathrm B}\) gives \(\rho_c=p_cq/T_c\) and \(T=qp/\rho\).

Polytropic Equation of State

The program parameter \(\gamma\) is the polytropic exponent, not the polytropic index \(n\). The two are related by \(\gamma=1+1/n\), or \(n=1/(\gamma-1)\).

Polytropic EOS  (2)
\[ p=K\rho^\gamma, \qquad \rho=Dp^{1/\gamma}. \]
Central normalization  (3)
\[ D=\frac{\rho_c}{p_c^{1/\gamma}}. \]

Once \(D\) has been fixed at the center, the same polytropic relation is used throughout the model.

Hydrostatic Equilibrium and Mass Continuity

Hydrostatic equilibrium  (4)
\[ \frac{dp}{dr}=-\frac{Gm(r)\rho}{r^2}. \]
Mass continuity  (5)
\[ \frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho. \]

These equations are coupled: pressure controls density through the polytropic law, density determines how rapidly enclosed mass grows, and enclosed mass determines the gravitational pressure gradient.

Range of the one-polytrope model: A Lane–Emden polytrope has an ordinary finite zero-pressure radius only for \(n<5\), corresponding here to \(\gamma>6/5\). The Python driver therefore rejects \(\gamma\le6/5\). The familiar Newtonian radial-stability boundary is \(\gamma=4/3\) under the simple case in which this exponent also describes the star's adiabatic response; the program should not be read as claiming that every real star can be classified by one constant \(\gamma\).

Initial Conditions

At the exact center, the expression \(Gm/r^2\) appears indeterminate even though the physical gravitational acceleration tends smoothly to zero. The integration therefore stores the exact central values at \(r=0\), then initializes the first nonzero point at \(r=dr\) using the leading-order central approximation.

Central density  (6)
\[ \rho_c=\frac{p_cq}{T_c}. \]

Characteristic Radial Scale

The program uses the dimensional scale

Radial scale  (7)
\[ \ell=\frac{\sqrt{p_c/G}}{\rho_c}, \qquad dr=\frac{\ell}{N_s}, \]

where \(N_s=\)steps_per_scale. This quantity is a convenient numerical length scale; it should not be interpreted as a literal local pressure scale height at the stellar center, where \(dp/dr\to0\).

First nonzero grid point  (8)
\[ r_1=dr,\quad p_1=p_c,\quad \rho_1=\rho_c,\quad m_1=\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_cdr^3,\quad T_1=T_c. \]

Integration Algorithm

The driver advances the model outward with a first-order forward-Euler scheme. For each ordinary radial step it first updates pressure,

Pressure step  (9)
\[ p_j=p_{j-1}-\frac{G\rho_{j-1}m_{j-1}}{r_{j-1}^2}\,dr, \]

then, if the trial pressure is still positive, updates mass, density, and temperature:

Remaining updates  (10)
\[ m_j=m_{j-1}+4\pi r_{j-1}^2\rho_{j-1}dr, \] \[ \rho_j=Dp_j^{1/\gamma},\qquad T_j=\frac{qp_j}{\rho_j}. \]

Interpolated Surface

When an Euler pressure step first crosses from positive to nonpositive pressure, the revised driver linearly interpolates along that final pressure segment to estimate the radius at which \(p=0\). It appends that surface point with \(p=\rho=T=0\), and advances the enclosed mass over the corresponding fractional shell. The returned arrays therefore include both the center at \(r=0\) and an explicit modeled surface at \(p=0\).

Numerical accuracy: Forward Euler has local truncation error \(O(dr^2)\) and global accumulated error \(O(dr)\). Agreement of the default model's total mass or radius with the observed Sun is therefore a combination of the simplified model parameters and the numerical discretization; it should not be used as a stand-alone measure of physical accuracy.

Step-Size Restart

The initial spacing is radial_scale / steps_per_scale. If the model has not reached zero pressure within max_points grid points, the driver doubles dr and restarts from the center. This increases the radial coverage of the next attempt but sacrifices resolution; it is not an error-controlled adaptive integrator.

max_points determines how far a given spacing can extend before such a restart is necessary. Increasing max_points does not make an already successful run finer. To perform an actual resolution study, increase steps_per_scale (for example, 200, 400, 800, 1600) while keeping enough max_points to avoid a restart.

The returned StarResult reports both radial_step and restart_count, making it possible to tell whether the requested initial resolution was retained.

Output Modes

All four physical profiles are computed in every run; output_type chooses which one is displayed. The horizontal axis begins at the stored center \(r=0\) and ends at the interpolated zero-pressure surface.

output_typeQuantityWhat to look for
"pressure"\(p(r)\), PaFalls monotonically from \(p_c\) to zero. A logarithmic y-axis reveals the low-pressure envelope particularly well.
"density"\(\rho(r)\), kg m\(^{-3}\)Falls from \(\rho_c\) to zero according to the imposed polytropic relation.
"temperature"\(T(r)\), KA derived quantity: \(T=qp/\rho\). It is not obtained from a stellar energy-transport equation.
"mass"\(m(r)\), kgRises from zero at the center to the modeled total stellar mass at the surface.

Suggested Experiments

The experiments below are arranged in approximate order of difficulty. The early exercises require only parameter changes and reading the resulting radius and mass; later exercises test scaling relations, numerical convergence, and substantial extensions of the stellar model.

1 · Introductory — Explore the Baseline Solar-Like Model

Run the default calculation with each of the four output modes: pressure, density, temperature, and enclosed mass. Identify the modeled surface where the pressure reaches zero, and record the total radius and mass there. Compare the shapes of the profiles with the solar model discussed in Chapter 8. Keep in mind that matching the Sun's overall mass and radius does not make a one-polytrope calculation a detailed solar model.

use the default physical parameters
try all four output_type values
try log_y = True for pressure and density

2 · Introductory–Intermediate — Central-Temperature Sensitivity

Change \(T_c\) by ±10% while holding \(p_c\), \(\mu\), and \(\gamma\) fixed. Record the surface radius and total mass for each run. The hotter model should be both larger and more massive. For this one-polytrope family the similarity relations predict \(R\propto T_c\) and \(M\propto T_c^2\); check whether the numerical results agree.

T_c = 0.9 * 2.263e7
T_c = 1.1 * 2.263e7
compare surface radius and mass

3 · Intermediate — Pressure Scaling

Hold \(T_c\), \(\mu\), and \(\gamma\) fixed and vary \(p_c\) by factors of four. Record the resulting stellar radius and mass. The similarity relations for this family give \(R\propto p_c^{-1/2}\) and \(M\propto p_c^{-1/2}\). Thus multiplying the central pressure by four should reduce both quantities by about a factor of two.

p_c = baseline / 4
p_c = baseline
p_c = baseline * 4
record R and M

4 · Intermediate — Composition-Parameter Scaling

At fixed \(p_c\), \(T_c\), and \(\gamma\), vary the effective mean molecular-weight parameter \(\mu\). Verify the mathematical scalings \(R\propto\mu^{-1}\) and \(M\propto\mu^{-2}\). Treat this as a scaling experiment: the different values of \(\mu\) should not all be interpreted as realistic compositions for otherwise identical stars.

mu = 0.6, 1.0, 1.285
record R and M

5 · Intermediate — Compensate for a Temperature Change

Schutz asks how a change in central pressure can restore the original stellar mass after \(T_c\) has been changed. Increase or decrease \(T_c\) by 10%, then adjust \(p_c\) until the total mass is again close to the baseline value. Finally compare the new radius with the original one. The fact that matching the mass does not automatically restore the radius illustrates why simultaneous mass and radius measurements constrain stellar models more tightly than either observable alone.

change T_c by 10%
adjust p_c until M returns to baseline
then compare R

6 · Intermediate–Advanced — Test the Chapter 8 Scaling Relations

Investigation 8.6 derives proportionalities among central conditions, radius, and mass for a fixed polytropic equation of state. Build a small family of models in which one input parameter is changed systematically, record \(R\) and \(M\), and test the predicted power laws. The temperature-, pressure-, and \(\mu\)-scaling experiments above provide three ready-made examples; combine their results into a single table or log-log plot.

construct a table of input parameter, R, and M
compare measured exponents with the Chapter 8 scalings

7 · Advanced — A Schematic Red-Giant Polytrope

Following Schutz, set \(\gamma=1.335\) and try to construct a model having approximately one solar mass but a radius about 100 times larger than the baseline solar-like model. Adjust \(p_c\) and \(T_c\) until both targets are reasonably close. Then try a model with about ten solar masses using the same \(\gamma\) and \(\mu\). These are deliberately schematic one-polytrope models; real red giants have strongly differentiated cores and envelopes.

gamma = 1.335
first target: M ≈ 1 solar mass, R ≈ 100 baseline radii
second target: M ≈ 10 solar masses
vary p_c and T_c

8 · Advanced — Newtonian Neutron-Star Toy Model

Follow Schutz's challenge by setting \(\gamma=2\) and \(\mu=1\), then search for central values that give approximately \(R=10\) km and \(M=1.4M_\odot\). Very large central pressure and a formal ideal-gas temperature will be required. Treat those quantities only as parameters that normalize this Newtonian polytrope: realistic neutron-star matter is not an ideal gas, and relativistic gravity is essential.

mu = 1.0
gamma = 2.0
target R ≈ 1.0e4 m
target M ≈ 2.8e30 kg

9 · Advanced — Numerical Convergence

Keep the physical inputs fixed and increase steps_per_scale. Increase max_points when necessary so that restart_count remains zero; otherwise the automatic step-doubling restart changes the effective radial resolution. Compare the surface radius and total mass. Because the integration uses forward Euler steps, the global error should eventually decrease approximately in proportion to \(dr\) as the grid is refined.

steps_per_scale = 200, 400, 800, 1600
increase max_points as needed
ensure restart_count == 0

10 · Advanced Programming Extension — Use More Than One Polytropic Region

Schutz's original Help proposes allowing the equation of state to change from one region of a star to another. Make a separate experimental copy of the program and divide the model into two or three radial regions with different polytropic exponents or normalizations. At every interface, enforce continuity of pressure and choose the new polytropic normalization so that the density does not jump unless such a discontinuity is intentionally part of the model. Compare the resulting profiles with those of the one-polytrope calculation.

This is a substantial extension: the purpose is not to construct a modern solar model, but to see how allowing different equations of state in different layers changes the global radius, mass distribution, and interior profiles.

advanced: introduce two or three radial EOS regions
preserve pressure continuity at each interface

Restoring Default Parameter Values

If you edit main.py or another program file while experimenting, you can restore the original program files using any of the following methods:

  • Same session: Press Ctrl+Z (Windows) or Cmd+Z (macOS) repeatedly in your editor to undo your edits.
  • If you are using PyCharm: Right-click the edited file in the Project panel → Local History → Show History → select a version from before your changes → click Revert.
  • Full reset: Re-download the ZIP from github.com/tomyee3-org/GFTGU-Programs and replace this program's folder with the fresh copy.

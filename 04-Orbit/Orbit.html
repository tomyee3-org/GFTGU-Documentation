Description

Orbit computes the trajectory of a test particle—planet, satellite, or comet— moving in the gravitational field of a fixed central mass under Newton's inverse-square law. The central mass remains at the origin; the orbiting body's position and velocity are advanced with an adaptive predictor-corrector integrator.

The supplied defaults approximate Mercury at perihelion. Five display modes show the x-y orbit, velocity hodograph, position versus time, velocity versus time, or specific kinetic/potential/total energy.

The solver uses an adaptive predictor-corrector method to control numerical error while following the orbit. Rejected steps may shrink, successful steps can recover toward the user's maximum timestep, and complete revolutions are measured by accumulating the azimuthal angle traveled. This works for clockwise or counter-clockwise motion starting from any direction.

How to Use

  1. Open main.py and edit the initial position, velocity, numerical controls, or output mode.
  2. Run main.py.
  3. Read the console summary for the termination reason and numerical-accuracy diagnostics.
  4. Inspect the selected plot. Try another output mode or change one parameter at a time for comparison.

The parameter k is the central gravitational parameter GM, in m3 s−2. Enter a gravitational parameter, not a mass in kilograms.

Physical Background

Newton's Law of Gravitation

For a fixed central body with gravitational parameter \(\mu=GM\),

Newtonian acceleration
\[ a_x=-\frac{\mu x}{r^3},\qquad a_y=-\frac{\mu y}{r^3},\qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. \]

The default uses the IAU nominal solar gravitational parameter \(\mu_\odot=1.3271244\times10^{20}\ {\rm m^3\,s^{-2}}\). For other central bodies, replace k with the corresponding \(GM\).

Bound and Unbound Kepler Orbits

The specific mechanical energy is

Specific energy
\[ \mathcal E=\frac12v^2-\frac{\mu}{r}. \]
  • \(\mathcal E<0\): bound ellipse (apart from the degenerate radial-collision case).
  • \(\mathcal E=0\): parabolic escape trajectory.
  • \(\mathcal E>0\): hyperbolic escape trajectory.

Bertrand's Theorem

In this ideal fixed-center inverse-square model, every non-collision bound orbit is closed. Bertrand's theorem states that only two central-force families have the stronger property that all bounded orbits are closed: inverse-square gravity and the isotropic harmonic oscillator, whose restoring force is proportional to \(r\).

Kepler's Laws

  • First law: bound inverse-square gravitational orbits are ellipses with the central mass at a focus.
  • Second law: equal areas are swept in equal times because the specific angular momentum \(h=xv_y-yv_x\) is constant and \(dA/dt=h/2\).
  • Third law: for a fixed central mass, \(T^2\propto a^3\).

Governing Equations

The step-by-step formulation of the orbital equations is described in Investigation 4.1 of Gravity from the Ground Up; the adjustable timestep and predictor-corrector ideas are introduced in Investigation 4.2.

Equations of Motion

Four first-order equations
\[ \dot x=v_x,\qquad \dot y=v_y, \] \[ \dot v_x=-\frac{\mu x}{r^3},\qquad \dot v_y=-\frac{\mu y}{r^3}. \]

Predictor

At the start of a proposed timestep \(\Delta t\), the solver predicts the endpoint using the current acceleration:

Constant-acceleration prediction
\[ \tilde{\mathbf v}=\mathbf v_n+\mathbf a_n\Delta t,\qquad \tilde{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r_n+\frac{\mathbf v_n+\tilde{\mathbf v}}{2}\Delta t. \]

Adaptive Timestep Gate

The predicted endpoint acceleration is compared with the starting acceleration using the Euclidean vector norm:

eps1 test
\[ \delta_a= \frac{\|\tilde{\mathbf a}-\mathbf a_n\|} {\max(\|\tilde{\mathbf a}\|,\|\mathbf a_n\|)}. \]

If \(\delta_a>\varepsilon_1\), the attempted step is discarded and the working timestep is halved.

Iterated Corrector

The accepted predictor is refined with the average beginning/end acceleration:

Trapezoidal corrector
\[ \mathbf v_{n+1}= \mathbf v_n+\frac{\mathbf a_n+\mathbf a_{n+1}}{2}\Delta t, \qquad \mathbf r_{n+1}= \mathbf r_n+\frac{\mathbf v_n+\mathbf v_{n+1}}{2}\Delta t. \]

The corrector monitors the change in the estimated velocity increment over the timestep, scaled by the initial predicted increment. Successive correction estimates must agree to eps2. If ten corrector iterations do not converge, the complete step is rejected, \(\Delta t\) is halved, and the step is tried again.

Conserved Quantities

Specific energy and angular momentum
\[ \mathcal E=\frac12(v_x^2+v_y^2)-\frac{\mu}{r}, \qquad h=xv_y-yv_x. \]

The program always reports maximum absolute changes in specific energy and specific angular momentum. It also reports fractional drift when the corresponding initial conserved quantity is safely different from zero. Fractional energy drift is not meaningful when the initial energy is zero or nearly zero, as in a parabolic escape calculation; fractional angular-momentum drift is likewise not meaningful for radial or nearly radial motion. In those cases the console labels the fractional result n/a and the absolute diagnostic remains available.

Algorithm

Adaptive Integration

  1. Compute acceleration at the current position.
  2. Predict position and velocity at the end of the proposed timestep.
  3. Reject and halve the timestep if the acceleration-vector change exceeds eps1.
  4. Iterate the trapezoidal corrector until successive estimates of the velocity increment meet eps2.
  5. If ten corrector passes fail, reject the entire step and retry at half the timestep.
  6. Reject and halve a trial step if its endpoint geometry approaches the singularity guard or if its estimated angular travel exceeds π/2.
  7. Commit the accepted state and record position, velocity, time, energy, and angular momentum.
  8. After a successful step, increase the working timestep by 10% toward the user's dt0, which is the maximum timestep.

Revolution Counting

The solver evaluates \(\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\) after every accepted step and unwraps the signed angular change across the \(-\pi/\pi\) branch cut. The accumulated angular travel gives

Accumulated revolutions
\[ N_{\rm rev}=\frac{|\Delta\phi_{\rm accumulated}|}{2\pi}. \]

This works for clockwise or counter-clockwise motion and for an arbitrary starting direction. maxOrbits may be 1, 1.5, 2, or another positive value. A conservative angular-step safeguard, independent of eps1 and eps2, rejects trials whose endpoint and chord geometry indicate angular travel above π/2. This is designed to keep endpoint-angle unwrapping unambiguous; it is a numerical safeguard rather than a mathematical bound on every point of the curved trajectory.

When a converged trial step overshoots the requested angular travel, the solver retries that step with timesteps bracketed above and below the target until the corrected endpoint is within a small angular tolerance. Position, velocity, time, and the reported revolution count all come from that same predictor-corrector endpoint; state components are not linearly interpolated.

Closure Diagnostics

Crossing the starting angular ray is not, by itself, proof that a numerical orbit has closed. For a run ending after an integral number of revolutions, the solver therefore also reports fractional differences in radius and velocity relative to the initial state. A well-resolved Kepler ellipse should have small closure residuals.

Point-Mass Singularity

The mathematical field diverges at \(r=0\). The code therefore derives a very small numerical guard from the initial orbital scale and floating-point spacing; it does not impose a fixed floor in meters. A coarse trial whose endpoint or straight endpoint segment reaches the guard is rejected and retried at half the timestep rather than being treated as a physical event. The explicit termination reason central_singularity is returned only after accepted states approach the scale-relative stop region. This is not a physical radius for the Sun, Earth, or any other central body.

Module Overview

Physics Layer
physics_orbit.py

Contains the inverse-square acceleration, the IAU nominal solar \(GM\), and helper functions for specific energy and specific angular momentum.

compute_acceleration(x, y, mu) specific_energy(...) specific_angular_momentum(...)
Driver Layer
driver_orbit.py

Implements adaptive predictor-corrector integration, timestep shrink/recovery, revolution counting, singularity protection, explicit termination reasons, and conservation/closure diagnostics. It returns the recorded history of accepted states.

run_orbit(..., maxOrbits=1.0) -> OrbitResult
Plot Layer
plot_orbit.py

Displays one of the five Orbit views from the already-computed OrbitResult. Orbit and hodograph plots use equal aspect ratios.

plot_orbit(result, output="orbit")
Entry Point
main.py

Contains user-editable initial conditions, central \(GM\), numerical controls, maxOrbits, and output selection. It also prints the run diagnostics.

result = run_orbit(...) plot_orbit(result, output=output)

Parameters

vxInit0.0m s⁻¹Initial x-velocity.
vyInit58980.0m s⁻¹Initial y-velocity; default approximates Mercury's perihelion speed.
k1.3271244e20m³ s⁻²Central gravitational parameter \(GM\), not the central mass in kg; default is the IAU nominal solar mass parameter.
dt01e4sMaximum timestep. The working timestep can shrink and later recover toward this value.
maxSteps20000—Maximum number of accepted integration steps, excluding the initial state.
maxOrbits1.0revolutionsMaximum accumulated azimuthal travel in units of \(2\pi\). For unbound or nearly radial trajectories this target may never be reached, so the run may instead stop at maxSteps or the singularity guard.
eps10.05—Positive acceleration-vector change threshold for timestep rejection. Smaller values demand smaller timesteps.
eps21e-4—Positive convergence tolerance for successive estimates of the velocity increment. Smaller values demand tighter corrector convergence.

output is set separately in main.py and controls only the display, not the integration.

Valid numerical inputs. The initial position must not be at r=0; k, dt0, eps1, eps2, and maxOrbits must be positive; and maxSteps must be a positive integer. All numeric inputs must be finite real numbers, and intermediate acceleration and conservation quantities must fit within ordinary floating-point range. The two tolerances are independent and need not be ordered. For experiments on the numerical method, a large value such as 10 for eps1 effectively prevents ordinary timestep rejection, while a similarly large eps2 makes the corrector stop after its first correction rather than iterating further.

The console summary additionally reports termination reason, elapsed simulated time, revolution count, the numbers of angular-step rejections and final-endpoint refinement trials, maximum absolute specific-energy and specific-angular-momentum drift, and—when defined—fractional drift for each conserved quantity. If the initial energy is zero or nearly zero, or the initial angular momentum is zero or nearly zero, the corresponding fractional diagnostic is reported as n/a. Closure residuals are reported when the requested endpoint is an integral number of revolutions.

Specific energy means energy per unit mass of the orbiting body. The mass of the test particle is therefore not needed.

Suggested Experiments

The experiments below are arranged in approximate order of difficulty. The first few require only changes to the parameters or display mode in main.py; later experiments ask you to interpret conservation laws, orbital geometry, or the numerical method.

EXP-1 · Introductory

Reproduce Planetary Orbits

Start with the default Mercury orbit. Then use perihelion distances and maximum orbital speeds from Table 4.3 of Gravity from the Ground Up to model other planets: place the planet on the positive x-axis at the perihelion distance in the second column and give it only the maximum y-direction speed from the third column. From the plotted perihelion and aphelion distances, estimate the eccentricity and compare it with the first column, using the formula in the table caption. This directly tests whether Newton's inverse-square law reproduces the observed shapes of planetary orbits.

EXP-2 · Introductory

Measure Planetary Periods

For several of the planetary orbits from EXP-1, set maxOrbits=1 and compare the reported elapsed simulated time with the observed orbital period. You can also select output="position_time" or output="velocity_time" and read the period from the time axis. Compare your results with the planetary periods in Table 4.2 of the book.

EXP-3 · Introductory–Intermediate

Position and Velocity Both Repeat

A closed orbit must return not only to its starting position but also to its starting velocity. Run one complete bound orbit and select output="velocity". The velocity-space curve should close just as the position-space orbit does. The console closure residuals provide a numerical check: after an integral number of revolutions, both the final radius and velocity should be close to their initial values.

EXP-4 · Intermediate

Energy Conservation

After reading the introduction to gravitational energy in Chapter 6, select output="energy" for a bound orbit. Kinetic and potential specific energy change continually, but their sum should remain nearly constant. Then make an unbound orbit and compare its total specific energy with that of the bound orbit. Bound Kepler orbits have negative specific energy, hyperbolic orbits positive specific energy, and the marginal parabolic case zero. The console's energy-drift diagnostics show the remaining numerical error and provide a quantitative check on energy conservation. Use absolute specific-energy drift for the parabolic case, where a fractional change from zero is undefined.

EXP-5 · Intermediate

Bound, Parabolic, and Hyperbolic Motion

At a fixed starting radius \(r\), vary the initial speed through the escape value \(v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2\mu/r}\). Below it the orbit is bound; at the limiting value it is parabolic; above it the orbit is hyperbolic. For unbound cases, increase maxSteps if necessary because the requested maxOrbits may never be reached. Compare the trajectory class with the sign of the initial specific energy. A decimal value typed for \(v_{\rm esc}\) is rounded to floating-point precision, so its computed energy may be slightly positive or negative rather than exactly zero. Inspect the absolute specific energy and test speeds just below and above the threshold when identifying the limiting parabolic case.

EXP-6 · Intermediate

Direction Independence of Binding

Keep \(v_x^2+v_y^2\) fixed while rotating the initial velocity vector at a fixed starting position. The orbit's shape and angular momentum change, but its bound/unbound classification does not, because the specific energy depends on the speed rather than the direction of the velocity.

EXP-7 · Intermediate

Approximate Moon Around a Fixed Earth

Replace the solar gravitational parameter by \(GM_\oplus\approx3.9860\times10^{14}\ {\rm m^3\,s^{-2}}\), and try xInit=3.626e8 m with vyInit=1082 m/s. This fixed-Earth test-particle model has a Kepler period of about 27.8 days. Compare the result with the model's prediction, remembering that the real Earth–Moon system differs because the Moon's mass is not negligible.

EXP-8 · Intermediate

Repeat Several Revolutions

Set maxOrbits=2 or 5 and examine whether successive numerical ellipses lie on top of one another. This makes accumulated integration error visible. Fractional values such as 1.5 are also allowed, although closure residuals are reported only when the requested endpoint is an integral number of revolutions.

EXP-9 · Intermediate–Advanced

Numerical Convergence

Reduce dt0 and tighten eps1 and eps2. Compare the orbital period, energy drift, angular-momentum drift, and closure residuals rather than judging accuracy from the orbit plot alone. Try changing one numerical control at a time so that you can tell which effect it has. You can also make either tolerance deliberately large to see what happens when that accuracy control is effectively relaxed.

EXP-10 · Advanced

Verify Kepler's Third Law

Run several bound planetary orbits. For each one, obtain the period \(T\) from a maxOrbits=1 run and estimate the semi-major axis \(a=(r_{\rm peri}+r_{\rm apo})/2\) from the trajectory. Compare \(T^2/a^3\) among the different orbits. It should be approximately constant when the central mass is unchanged.

EXP-11 · Advanced

Verify Kepler's Second Law

Use the returned \(x,y,t\) arrays to select positions separated by equal time intervals. Approximate the area swept during each interval by the triangle formed by the origin and the two successive radius vectors. The areas should be nearly equal. Relate this result to the nearly constant specific angular momentum \(h=xv_y-yv_x\) reported by the calculation. Insert your analysis in main.py immediately after run_orbit() returns; the arrays are available there as result.xs, result.ys, and result.ts.

EXP-12 · Advanced

Explore the Velocity Hodograph

Select output="velocity". For a non-radial Kepler orbit the velocity vectors lie on a circle: a bound ellipse traces the full circle, whereas parabolic and hyperbolic trajectories trace only part of it. Compare several eccentricities and then an unbound orbit.

EXP-13 · Advanced

Point-Mass Radial Infall

Give the particle zero or nearly zero angular momentum and an inward velocity. Observe what happens as the trajectory approaches the mathematical point-mass singularity. The program contains a numerical guard to prevent evaluation at \(r=0\); that guard radius is not the physical surface of a star or planet. This limiting case provides a useful test of the numerical treatment of trajectories that approach the point-mass singularity.

EXP-14 · Advanced Programming Extension

Try a Different Central Force Law

As an optional programming exercise, make a copy of the program and replace the Newtonian inverse-square acceleration with other central-force laws. A particularly instructive comparison is a Hooke-law acceleration proportional to \(-\mathbf r\). You can also try small non-Newtonian corrections and look for orbital precession. Historically, modified Newtonian force laws were proposed as explanations for Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession, while modern alternatives such as MOND have been proposed as alternatives to dark matter in some astrophysical settings. After experimenting with the code, research these topics and investigate why modified force laws have not replaced general relativity as the accepted explanation of Mercury's perihelion precession. Keep this as a separate experimental copy so the standard Orbit program continues to represent Newtonian gravity.

EXP-15 · Advanced Programming Extension

Compare Error Measures

Make a separate experimental copy and replace the Euclidean-norm comparisons in _relative_vector_change() and _relative_increment_change() with sums of absolute component differences. Run the same eccentric orbit with each version, then compare accepted-step counts, period error, conservation drift, and closure residuals. Rotate the initial position and velocity together and repeat: which error measure is unchanged by a rotation of the coordinate axes, and does the difference matter at the tolerances you tested?

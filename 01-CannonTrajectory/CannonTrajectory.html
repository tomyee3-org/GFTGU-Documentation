Description

CannonTrajectory is the most elementary program in the GFtGU suite. It computes the trajectory of a projectile — a cannonball, a thrown ball, or any object launched from Earth's surface — under constant gravitational acceleration, with no air resistance. It is introduced in Investigation 1.3 of Schutz's text, where it serves as the reader's first encounter with numerical integration and the concept of a program loop.

Despite its simplicity, the program introduces two genuinely distinct numerical methods: the basic Euler method and the more accurate improved Euler (Heun) method. Comparing their outputs at different timesteps gives immediate, visual intuition for the meaning of numerical accuracy and convergence — essential preparation for the more demanding integrators used in later chapters.

Physical Background

Near-surface approximation

Close to Earth's surface, gravitational acceleration is approximately constant: \(g \approx 9.8\;\text{m s}^{-2}\) downward. This approximation is excellent for trajectories whose dimensions are small compared with Earth's radius (\(R_\oplus \approx 6.4\times10^6\) m). A projectile launched at 100 m/s travels only about one kilometre horizontally.

No air resistance

The program models vacuum flight. Real aerodynamic drag can noticeably shorten the range even at ordinary projectile speeds, although the trajectory still has the same qualitative rising-and-falling form. Omitting drag keeps the equations simple enough to solve analytically, so the numerical result can be checked against an exact answer.

Horizontal and vertical independence

Because gravity acts only vertically and drag is absent, horizontal and vertical motion are independent. The horizontal velocity remains constant, while the vertical velocity changes at the constant rate \(-g\). Both numerical methods preserve this separation of the equations exactly. That does not mean forward Euler reproduces the exact parabola: Euler still has a vertical-position discretization error.

Governing Equations

Equations of motion

With \(x\) the horizontal distance, \(h\) the height, \(u\) the horizontal velocity, and \(v\) the vertical velocity, the continuous equations of motion are:

Eq. 1 — Horizontal kinematics
\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = u, \qquad \frac{du}{dt} = 0. \]
Eq. 2 — Vertical kinematics
\[ \frac{dh}{dt} = v, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = -g. \]

In the code these four rates of change are returned by derivs_cannon(state) as the NumPy array \([u,\; v,\; 0,\; {-g}]\), corresponding to the state vector \(\mathbf{s} = [x,\; h,\; u,\; v]\).

Initial conditions

The projectile is launched from the origin with speed \(v_0\) at angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal:

Eq. 3 — Launch conditions
\[ x_0 = 0, \quad h_0 = 0, \quad u_0 = v_0 \cos\theta, \quad v_{0y} = v_0 \sin\theta. \]

Analytic solution

For this simple case an exact solution exists and can be used to verify the integrators:

Eq. 4 — Analytic trajectory
\[ x(t) = u_0\, t, \qquad h(t) = v_{0y}\, t - \tfrac{1}{2} g\, t^2. \]
Eq. 5 — Range formula
\[ R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}. \]

The range is maximised at \(\theta = 45°\), which is why the default firing angle is 45°. For \(v_0 = 100\) m/s, the analytic range is \(R = 1020.4\) m.

Euler method

The simplest time integrator advances the state by one step using only the derivative at the beginning of the interval:

Eq. 6 — Forward Euler step
\[ \mathbf{s}_{n+1} = \mathbf{s}_{n} + \Delta t\; \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_{n}), \]

where \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}) = [u,\, v,\, 0,\, {-g}]\) is the derivative vector. Euler is first-order accurate: the global error is \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\).

Improved Euler method (Heun's method)

The improved Euler method uses a predictor–corrector pair to achieve second-order accuracy \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\):

Eq. 7 — Heun predictor
\[ \mathbf{s}^* = \mathbf{s}_{n} + \Delta t\; \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_{n}). \]
Eq. 8 — Heun corrector
\[ \mathbf{s}_{n+1} = \mathbf{s}_{n} + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\bigl[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_{n}) + \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}^*)\bigr]. \]

Because the derivatives are linear in this problem, \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}^*) = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_n) + [0,\; -g\,\Delta t,\; 0,\; 0]^T\): only the derivative of height changes during the predictor step. The corrector therefore averages the initial and final vertical velocities. For horizontal motion, where the derivative is constant, both methods give the same result. In fact, for constant gravitational acceleration Heun's method reproduces the exact analytic position and velocity at the sampled times (apart from roundoff); the apparent error in the final plotted range is dominated by locating the ground crossing only at discrete timesteps.

Algorithm

Initialisation

The launch angle is converted to radians and the initial velocity components are calculated. Inputs are validated before trajectory storage is allocated. The first stored point is the launch point \(x=h=0\).

Integration loop

Before each new step, the loop confirms that:

  1. the last stored height is still non-negative, and
  2. space remains below the max_steps safety limit.

It then advances the state with the selected stepper, stores the new horizontal position and height, and increments the stored-point counter.

On the following loop test, a negative height stops the calculation. Thus the returned trajectory intentionally includes the first sampled point below ground.

Termination

Normal termination occurs at the first stored point with \(h<0\). If max_steps is exhausted while the projectile is still at or above ground level, the function raises a RuntimeError instead of silently returning an incomplete trajectory.

Landing interpolation: the last stored point is below ground, not at \(h=0\). Its horizontal coordinate is therefore not the best estimate of the physical range. Experiment 3 shows how to interpolate between the last positive and first negative heights.

Code Modules

physics_cannon.py
Physics & Integrators

Defines \(g=9.8\;\text{m s}^{-2}\), the state derivatives \([u,v,0,-g]\), and the forward-Euler and improved-Euler (Heun) steppers.

driver_cannon.py
Simulation Driver

Validates inputs, converts launch angle to velocity components, selects the requested integrator, stores trajectory samples, and stops after the first below-ground point. Invalid method names are rejected.

run_cannon_trajectory(...) -> stored x and h arrays
plot_cannon.py
Visualisation

Plots the trajectory and launch point using the same physical scale on both axes so the parabolic shape is not visually distorted.

main.py
User Settings

Contains the ordinary experiment settings. Edit the values passed to run_cannon_trajectory(), save the file, and run python main.py.

Parameters

For ordinary use, open main.py, edit the values below, save the file, and run python main.py.

ParameterDefaultUnitsDescription

Analytic check for the defaults: \(v_{0y}=100\sin45^\circ\approx70.71\) m/s, so \(T=2v_{0y}/g\approx14.4308\) s and \(R=v_0^2/g\approx1020.4\) m. With \(\Delta t=0.1\) s, \(T/\Delta t\approx144.31\), so the first sampled time after landing occurs after 145 integration steps. The returned arrays therefore contain 146 stored points: the launch point plus those 145 steps.

Output

The matplotlib plot displays the title CannonTrajectory — Newtonian Projectile Motion and shows:

  • a trajectory line through the first sampled point below ground,
  • an orange marker at the launch point,
  • equal horizontal and vertical physical scales,
  • grid lines and a legend.

run_cannon_trajectory() returns NumPy arrays xs and hs containing the launch point and every stored integration sample through the first below-ground point. Their length is the number of stored points, not the number of completed integration steps.

Suggested Experiments

Experiment 1
Accuracy of the two integrators

Run the simulation twice with the same parameters, once with method="euler" and once with method="improved". First estimate the landing point by the interpolation procedure in Experiment 3; do not use the last element of xs directly, because it is the first sampled point after the projectile has crossed the ground. Compare the interpolated range against the analytic value \(R = v_0^2 \sin 2\theta / g\), then repeat with dt halved and quartered. The Euler trajectory has first-order global error. For this special constant-acceleration problem, however, Heun's method gives the exact analytic state at each sampled time (apart from roundoff), so it is better regarded here as a demonstration of why the improved method is more accurate than as a generic measurement of second-order convergence.

Experiment 2 (Schutz suggestion)
Degrade accuracy — use the final vertical speed

To reproduce Schutz's suggested less-accurate variant, replace the averaged height increment with one that uses the final vertical velocity over the whole interval. In the current Heun notation that velocity is ds2[1], so after computing the normal Heun result, replace its height component by state[1] + dt * ds2[1]. This corresponds to Schutz's \(h \leftarrow h+w\,dt\), where \(w=v-g\,dt\). Use the interpolated landing point from Experiment 3 when comparing ranges; otherwise the first sampled point below ground introduces its own \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\) error. With that endpoint effect reduced, verify that the deliberately degraded height update shows first-order convergence.

Experiment 3 (Schutz suggestion)
Interpolated landing point

The loop terminates at the first step where \(h < 0\). Improve the accuracy of the reported range by linear interpolation: if the last two heights are \(h_{n-1} > 0\) and \(h_n < 0\), the zero crossing is at \(x^* = x_{n-1} + (x_n - x_{n-1}) \cdot h_{n-1} / (h_{n-1} - h_n)\). Add this correction to the driver and verify that it reduces the range error significantly, even for large \(\Delta t\).

Experiment 4
Optimal firing angle

Loop over firing angles from 1° to 89° in 1° steps and record the range for each. Plot range vs. angle. Confirm that the maximum occurs at 45° and that the range curve is symmetric about 45° (i.e., complementary angles give the same range). Use the interpolated landing point from Experiment 3 for the cleanest comparison; raw final xs values can differ slightly because each trajectory crosses the ground at a different phase within a timestep. Try this with both the Euler and improved Euler methods — does the choice of integrator shift the numerically optimal angle?

Experiment 5
Effect of muzzle speed

Fix the angle at 45° and vary speed from 50 to 500 m/s. The range should scale as \(v_0^2\). Use the interpolated landing point from Experiment 3, plot range vs. \(v_0^2\) on a linear scale, and verify that the slope approaches \(\sin 90° / g = 1/g\). At sufficiently high speeds and ranges, the flat-Earth, constant-\(g\) approximation ceases to be adequate. The later EarthOrbit and Orbit programs explore motion over distances where Earth's curvature and radial gravitational field must be considered.

Experiment 6
Overlay multiple trajectories

Call run_cannon_trajectory for angles of 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, and 75° at speed = 100 m/s and overlay all five trajectories on a single matplotlib figure. Observe that the 15°/75° pair and the 30°/60° pair each share the same analytic range. Small differences in their raw endpoints can result from timestep sampling; use the interpolation method of Experiment 3 if you want to compare the landing ranges quantitatively. Add a horizontal line at the maximum range for reference.

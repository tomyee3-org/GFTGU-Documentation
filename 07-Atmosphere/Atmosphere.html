Description

Atmosphere models the vertical pressure, density, and temperature structure of a planetary atmosphere in hydrostatic equilibrium. Starting from a measured sea-level (or reference-level) pressure and a user-supplied temperature profile, the program integrates upward in altitude until the numerical integration reaches its zero-pressure boundary, then plots whichever of the three atmospheric quantities the user selects. This zero-pressure boundary is a numerical stopping condition for the simplified model, not a sharply defined physical surface in a real atmosphere.

It is suitable for modeling Earth's atmosphere, comparing planetary atmospheres across the Solar System, and exploring how scale height depends on gravity and molecular weight.

Textbook reference: Bernard Schutz, Gravity from the Ground Up, Cambridge University Press (2003), Chapter 7 — Investigations 7.1 and 7.3.
Default Earth profile: The lower-atmosphere temperatures use U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 values. The thermospheric portion is a representative NRLMSIS 2.0 thermospheric profile through 500 km; see Parameters for the run conditions and limitations.

Physics & Equations

Hydrostatic Equilibrium

A column of atmosphere in static equilibrium satisfies the condition that the pressure gradient exactly balances the weight per unit volume of the gas:

\[ \frac{dp}{dh} = -g\,\rho \tag{7.1} \]

where \(p\) is pressure (Pa), \(h\) is altitude (m), \(g\) is the local gravitational acceleration (m s−2), and \(\rho\) is the mass density (kg m−3). The negative sign reflects the fact that pressure decreases with increasing altitude.

Ideal Gas Law

The density of an ideal gas is related to its pressure and temperature by

\[ \rho = \frac{p\,\mu\,m_p}{k_B\,T} \tag{7.2} \]

where \(\mu\) is the mean molecular weight (dimensionless, e.g. 28.97 for dry air), \(m_p = 1.67\times10^{-27}\;\text{kg}\) is the proton mass, \(k_B = 1.38\times10^{-23}\;\text{J K}^{-1}\) is the Boltzmann constant, and \(T\) is the temperature in Kelvin.

Substituting Eq. (7.2) into Eq. (7.1) gives the complete governing equation for atmospheric structure:

\[ \frac{dp}{dh} = -\frac{g\,\mu\,m_p}{k_B\,T(h)}\,p \tag{7.3} \]

For an isothermal atmosphere this integrates to the barometric formula \(p = p_0\,e^{-h/H}\), where \(H\) is the scale height.

Scale Height

The scale height \(H\) sets the characteristic altitude over which the pressure drops by a factor of \(e\). At the base of the atmosphere it is

\[ H = \frac{p_0}{g\,\rho_0} = \frac{k_B\,T_0}{g\,\mu\,m_p} \tag{7.4} \]

where \(\rho_0\) is the density computed from Eq. (7.2) at the surface pressure \(p_0\) and surface temperature \(T_0\). For the default Earth parameters, \(H \approx 8.4\;\text{km}\). The initial integration step size is \(\Delta h = H/200\). If 1 000 steps are insufficient to reach the model's numerical upper boundary, the driver restarts with a larger step; therefore the successful run may use substantially fewer than 200 steps per scale height.

Temperature Profile

Real atmospheres have non-uniform temperature structures driven by solar heating, convection, and radiation. The program accepts an arbitrary set of \((h_i,\,T_i)\) measurement pairs and constructs a piecewise temperature profile:

\[ T(h) = T_{i-1} + \frac{T_i - T_{i-1}}{h_i - h_{i-1}}\,(h - h_{i-1}), \qquad h_{i-1} \le h < h_i \tag{7.5} \]

That is, temperature is linearly interpolated between adjacent measured altitudes. Below the first measurement point the temperature is held constant at \(T_0\).

Upper Atmosphere Extension (Power-Law Model)

When the integration surpasses the highest measured altitude, the program switches to a power-law relationship between temperature and pressure. On the first computed step above the highest supplied altitude, the coefficient \(\beta\) is chosen so that the extrapolated temperature at that step equals the last supplied temperature:

\[ T = \beta\,p^{\,\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = 0.5 \tag{7.6} \]

The exponent \(\alpha = 0.5\) means that temperature falls as the square root of pressure. This closure is a simple mathematical extrapolation, not a realistic model of the upper atmosphere. The default Earth profile supplied in main.py extends to 500 km; only above that altitude does this power-law relation take over. As pressure approaches zero it also drives the modeled temperature toward zero, which is a consequence of the chosen extrapolation rather than a real physical effect.

Algorithm

Finite-Difference Integration

Equation (7.1) is discretized using a first-order forward (Euler) step:

\[ p_j = p_{j-1} - g\,\rho_{j-1}\,\Delta h \tag{7.7} \]

At each altitude step the density \(\rho_{j-1}\) is obtained from the ideal gas law (Eq. 7.2) using the current pressure and the temperature profile at the current altitude. The integration terminates as soon as \(p_j < 0\). That negative-pressure point is discarded, so the returned arrays end at the last valid non-negative-pressure step.

Step-Size Restart (dh Doubling)

If 1 000 Euler steps do not cross the model's zero-pressure boundary, the driver doubles \(\Delta h\) and restarts the calculation from the surface. This is a coverage mechanism rather than an error-controlled adaptive integrator: it ensures that the calculation eventually spans a sufficiently large altitude range, but the successful run can be considerably coarser than the initial \(H/200\) step size. On every restart, the upper-atmosphere extrapolation state is reset so that \(\beta\) is recomputed from the pressure profile of the new run. A retry limit prevents pathological parameter choices from causing an indefinite sequence of step-size doublings. 

rho0  = ideal_gas_density(p0, mu, T0)      # surface density
scale = p0 / (g * rho0)                    # scale height H
dh    = scale / 200.0                      # initial step

last_step = 0
while last_step == 0:
    for j in range(1, max_steps):          # max_steps = 1000
        alt[j] = alt[j-1] + dh
        p[j] = hydrostatic_step(p[j-1], rho[j-1], g, dh)
        if p[j] < 0.0:
            last_step = j
            break                          # atmosphere top reached;
                                            # T[j], rho[j] never computed
                                            # for this (discarded) point
        T[j] = profile.get_temp(alt[j], p[j])
        rho[j] = ideal_gas_density(p[j], mu, T[j])
    dh *= 2.0                              # only matters if last_step
                                            # is still 0 -- loop then
                                            # retries with a larger step

Output Arrays

After the loop, the result arrays are trimmed to [:last_step], excluding the unphysical negative-pressure point. The extract_output() function then selects whichever quantity the user requested (pressure, density, or temperature) and packages it with axis labels, units, and a title for the plotter.

Numerical note: This is a first-order Euler calculation intended to illustrate hydrostatic equilibrium. Because the step size may be doubled repeatedly, the final run can accumulate noticeable numerical error with altitude. The isothermal convergence experiment below provides an exact exponential solution against which this error can be measured.

Physics Layer

physics_atmosphere.py

Encodes the physical constants, temperature-profile interpolation and extrapolation, ideal-gas density relation, and one hydrostatic pressure step. It also validates the supplied temperature profile before the integration begins.

Data Class

@dataclass
class TemperatureProfile:
    h: List[float] # altitude breakpoints (m)
    T: List[float] # temperatures at breakpoints (K)
    power: float = 0.5 # exponent α in T = β·p^α
    beta: float = 0.0 # coefficient β (set on first pass above the highest supplied breakpoint)
    reached_top: bool = False

Stores the supplied temperature profile and state for the upper-atmosphere power-law extension. The profile is validated before integration. beta is fixed on the first computed step above the highest supplied breakpoint so that the extrapolated temperature there equals the last supplied temperature.

Functions

TemperatureProfile.get_temp(self, altitude: float, pressure: float) -> float

Returns the temperature at the given altitude and current pressure. Uses linear interpolation (Eq. 7.5) between breakpoints while inside the measured range; below the first breakpoint returns \(T_0\) (constant); above the highest breakpoint applies \(T = \beta\,p^{\alpha}\) (Eq. 7.6), fixing \(\beta\) on the first call in that regime so the extrapolated temperature there equals the last supplied temperature.

ideal_gas_density(pressure: float, mu: float, temperature: float) -> float

Returns the mass density \(\rho = p\,\mu\,m_p\,/\,(k_B\,T)\) in kg m−3. Implements Eq. (7.2) directly. Requires pressure ≥ 0 and temperature > 0, with finite numerical inputs.

hydrostatic_step(pressure_prev: float, rho_prev: float, g_accel: float, dh: float) -> float

Advances the pressure by one Euler step: \(p_j = p_{j-1} - g\,\rho_{j-1}\,\Delta h\). Implements Eq. (7.7). Negative results signal that the model's zero-pressure boundary has been crossed.

Driver / Solver

driver_atmosphere.py

Owns all integration logic: builds the temperature profile, computes the initial scale height, runs the while/for adaptive loop, and packages results into structured dataclasses (AtmosphereResult, then CurveData via extract_output()) ready for the plotter.

Class

class AtmosphereModel:
    def __init__(self, params: AtmosphereParameters) -> None
    def run(self) -> AtmosphereResult

__init__ stores the parameters and constructs a TemperatureProfile from h_points and T_points. run() computes the initial density and scale height, then executes the step-size-restart while/for integration loop (described in the Algorithm section), returning an AtmosphereResult with arrays sliced to [:last_step].

Converts an AtmosphereResult into a plot-ready CurveData. The y array and label depend on result.output_type: pressures (Pa), densities (kg m−3), or temperatures (K). The x array is always the altitude in meters.

Module: plot_atmosphere.py

Visualization

plot_atmosphere.py

Accepts the structured CurveData from extract_output() and produces a matplotlib line graph. The module is intentionally minimal: all axis labels, units, and the title are already embedded in CurveData, so the plotter needs no knowledge of the underlying physics.

Function

plot_atmosphere(curve_data: CurveData) -> None

Reads curve_data.x (altitudes, m), curve_data.y (selected quantity), curve_data.x_label, curve_data.y_label, and curve_data.title. Creates a single-axes figure with ax.plot(x, y), applies labels and a grid, then calls plt.tight_layout() and plt.show().

Module: main.py

Entry Point

main.py

The user-facing entry point. Sets all input parameters to Earth defaults and calls the driver and plotter. Edit the parameters in this file to model a different planet or atmospheric scenario; the other three modules never need to be modified. 

from driver_atmosphere import AtmosphereParameters, AtmosphereModel, OutputType, extract_output
from plot_atmosphere  import plot_atmosphere

planet_name = "Earth"
g_accel     = 9.81          # m s⁻²
mu          = 28.97         # mean molecular weight (dry air)
p0          = 1.013e5       # Pa  (1 atm)

# Hybrid Earth temperature profile:
# U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 through ~86 km,
# then representative NRLMSIS 2.0 thermospheric temperatures to 500 km.
h_points = [
    0.0, 11_019.0, 20_063.0, 32_162.0, 47_350.0,
    51_412.0, 71_802.0, 86_000.0, 100_000.0,
    150_000.0, 200_000.0, 250_000.0, 300_000.0,
    400_000.0, 500_000.0,
]
T_points = [
    288.15, 216.65, 216.65, 228.65, 270.65,
    270.65, 214.65, 186.946, 190.0,
    800.0, 1080.0, 1190.0, 1225.0, 1240.0, 1240.0,
]
output_type: OutputType = "Pressure"    # "Pressure" | "Density" | "Temperature"

params = AtmosphereParameters(
    planet_name=planet_name, g_accel=g_accel, mu=mu, p0=p0,
    h_points=h_points, T_points=T_points, output_type=output_type
)

model      = AtmosphereModel(params)
result     = model.run()
curve_data = extract_output(result)
plot_atmosphere(curve_data)

Temperature profile sources and limitations: From sea level through approximately 86 km, the default temperatures use U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 layer-boundary values. The standard defines those boundaries in geopotential height; the corresponding geometric altitudes are used here.

From 100 km through 500 km, the values are rounded neutral temperatures read from an NRLMSIS 2.0 CCMC Instant Run for 15 October 2024 at 12:00 UTC, geographic latitude 55° N and longitude 45° E. The CCMC run used observational solar and geomagnetic indices for that date. These thermospheric values are therefore a date- and condition-specific example, not universal standard temperatures: the thermosphere varies strongly with solar activity, geomagnetic activity, location, season, and time of day.

The temperature curve is consequently more realistic than a single fixed thermospheric profile, but the rest of this program remains deliberately simple. In particular, g_accel and mu are held constant with altitude, whereas the real thermosphere changes in composition and effective molecular mass. Pressure and density above roughly 100 km should therefore be interpreted as an illustrative continuation of the hydrostatic ideal-gas model, not as an NRLMSIS-quality prediction. Above 500 km, the program begins the simplified power-law extrapolation of Eq. (7.6).

The h_points and T_points lists must have the same length; all numerical inputs must be finite, altitudes must be strictly increasing, and all temperatures must be positive. g_accel, mu, and p0 must also be positive. The calculation always starts at altitude 0 using T_points[0] as the surface temperature; in normal use, set h_points[0] = 0.0.

Output Modes

Set output_type in main.py to select which atmospheric quantity is plotted on the vertical axis (with altitude on the horizontal axis).

"Pressure"
y-axis unit: Pa

Atmospheric pressure vs. altitude. Decreases roughly exponentially with a scale height of ~8.5 km for Earth. On a logarithmic scale, the pressure curve makes changes in effective scale height especially visible.

"Density"
y-axis unit: kg m−3

Mass density vs. altitude, derived from the ideal gas law. Also decreases exponentially, but the profile is additionally modulated by temperature variations through \(\rho \propto p/T\).

"Temperature"
y-axis unit: K

Temperature vs. altitude. With the default Earth data, this shows the U.S. Standard Atmosphere lower profile followed by the strongly rising NRLMSIS thermospheric portion of the profile through 500 km. Above the highest supplied breakpoint, the program uses the \(T = \beta\,p^{0.5}\) power-law extension.

Logarithmic scale tip: Pressure and density span many orders of magnitude. Because altitude is on the horizontal axis and the selected atmospheric quantity is on the vertical axis, use ax.set_yscale("log") in plot_atmosphere.py after the ax.plot() call.

Suggested Experiments

The experiments below are arranged in approximate order of difficulty. Most require only changes to main.py; experiments that require a plotting or algorithm change say so explicitly.

1 · Introductory — Explore Earth's Pressure, Density, and Temperature Profiles

Run the default Earth model three times, selecting "Pressure", "Density", and "Temperature" in turn. Compare how the three quantities change with altitude. In the temperature plot, identify the familiar lower-atmosphere structure below about 86 km and the strong thermospheric temperature rise in the supplied 100–500 km profile. Above the highest supplied temperature point, remember that the program uses its simplified power-law extrapolation; do not interpret that far-upper-atmosphere continuation as a realistic physical temperature profile.

output_type = "Pressure" # then "Density", then "Temperature" # all other parameters at their defaults

2 · Introductory — Plot Pressure on a Logarithmic Scale

Set output_type = "Pressure" and add ax.set_yscale("log") in plot_atmosphere.py. Pressure spans many orders of magnitude, so a logarithmic vertical axis makes the structure much easier to inspect. For an isothermal atmosphere an exponential pressure law would appear as a straight line; Earth's changing temperature therefore shows up as changes in slope.

output_type = "Pressure" # add ax.set_yscale("log") after ax.plot(x, y)

3 · Introductory–Intermediate — Surface Pressure Changes the Scale, Not the Scale Height

Keep Earth's gravity, molecular weight, and temperature profile fixed, but repeat the calculation with surface pressure multiplied and divided by 10. Compare the pressure profiles as fractions of their own surface values. The absolute pressures differ by a factor of 10, but the fractional falloff with altitude is essentially unchanged. Relate this to Eq. (7.4), in which the isothermal scale height depends on temperature, gravity, and molecular weight but not on surface pressure.

p0 = 1.013e4 # then 1.013e5 and 1.013e6 Pa

4 · Intermediate — What Controls Scale Height?

Change one parameter at a time while keeping the others at their Earth values. First try mu = 18, 28.97, and 44; then repeat with several values of g_accel. Verify qualitatively that the atmosphere becomes more vertically extended when molecular weight or gravity is reduced. For an isothermal atmosphere, Eq. (7.4) predicts \(H\propto T/(\mu g)\).

mu = 18.0, 28.97, 44.0 # then vary g_accel while restoring mu = 28.97

5 · Intermediate — Compare Mars and Venus

Apply the same model to two very different CO₂ atmospheres. Mars has weak gravity and very low surface pressure; Venus has much stronger gravity, an enormous surface pressure, and a very hot lower atmosphere. Compare their pressure profiles and base scale heights. Use the comparison to separate the roles of surface pressure, temperature, gravity, and molecular weight rather than attributing atmospheric "thickness" to pressure alone.

# Mars planet_name = "Mars"; g_accel = 3.72; mu = 44.0; p0 = 610.0 h_points = [0.0, 10000.0, 25000.0, 45000.0] T_points = [210.0, 170.0, 145.0, 130.0] # Venus planet_name = "Venus"; g_accel = 8.87; mu = 44.0; p0 = 9.2e6 h_points = [0.0, 10000.0, 30000.0, 60000.0] T_points = [735.0, 500.0, 340.0, 230.0]

6 · Intermediate — Change the Temperature Profile

Replace the default profile with a deliberately simple temperature structure and observe how warm and cool layers affect density through \(\rho\propto p/T\). Introduce a warm layer above a cooler one, then compare the Pressure, Density, and Temperature outputs. This isolates the role of the supplied temperature profile from changes in gravity, molecular weight, or surface pressure.

h_points = [0.0, 50000.0, 100000.0] T_points = [288.0, 200.0, 350.0]

7 · Intermediate–Advanced — Jupiter as a Local 1-Bar-Level Model

Use the 1-bar level as a reference and model only a relatively thin region of Jupiter's outer atmosphere. The low mean molecular weight of hydrogen-helium gas produces a large scale height despite strong gravity. Treat this explicitly as a local exercise: the program assumes constant gravity and fixed-composition ideal gas behavior, so it should not be interpreted as a model of Jupiter's full atmosphere or interior. Schutz warned against applying this simple constant-gravity model to an entire gas giant; the limited calculation here is intended to show why that warning matters.

planet_name = "Jupiter" g_accel = 24.8 mu = 2.2 p0 = 1.0e5 h_points = [0.0, 20000.0, 50000.0, 100000.0] T_points = [165.0, 130.0, 110.0, 150.0]

8 · Advanced — Test the Euler Integration Against an Exact Isothermal Atmosphere

Use a constant temperature of 288.15 K from 0 to 100 km and plot Pressure. In that interval the exact solution is \(p(h)=p_0\exp(-h/H)\), with \(H=k_BT/(g\mu m_p)\). Compare the numerical pressure with the exact result at selected altitudes such as 10, 50, and 90 km. Only compare points within the supplied constant-temperature interval; above 100 km the program begins its power-law extrapolation and the atmosphere is no longer isothermal.

To test convergence, temporarily change dh = scale / 200.0 in driver_atmosphere.py to scale / 400.0 and then scale / 800.0, while increasing max_steps enough to reach 100 km without triggering the step-doubling restart. The error should decrease roughly in proportion to the step size, as expected for this first-order Euler update.

h_points = [0.0, 100000.0] T_points = [288.15, 288.15] output_type = "Pressure"

9 · Advanced Programming Extension — Let Gravity Decrease with Altitude

Schutz's original Help suggested replacing the program's constant value of gravity with a height-dependent gravitational acceleration. Make a separate experimental copy of the program and replace the constant \(g\) in the hydrostatic step by a function such as \(g(h)=g_0[R/(R+h)]^2\), where \(R\) is the planet's radius. Compare the resulting pressure profile with the constant-gravity calculation and identify the altitude range over which the simpler approximation is adequate. This is a natural bridge to later stellar-structure calculations, where gravity varies with position.

advanced: modify the hydrostatic integration to use g(h)

Description

Planck2 is a black-body-radiation investigation for Chapter 10. It plots the user's choice of spectral radiance per unit wavelength \(B_\lambda\), spectral radiance per unit frequency \(B_\nu\), or spectral energy density per unit frequency \(u_\nu\), using physical wavelength or frequency on the horizontal axis.

The wavelength and frequency descriptions are different spectral densities: changing from wavelength to frequency changes the interval represented by each horizontal-axis element, so \(B_\lambda\) and \(B_\nu\) do not peak at corresponding photons. By contrast, \(u_\nu=(4\pi/c)B_\nu\), so the frequency radiance and frequency energy-density curves have the same shape and the same peak frequency.

The graph reports the sampled peak, the area under the dimensionless shape function, and a physical integral over wavelength or frequency. Here, the physical integral means the integral of the plotted spectrum over its physical horizontal coordinate; it is distinct from the dimensionless area under \(f_p(x)\). The physical integral allows a direct numerical check of the Stefan–Boltzmann \(T^4\) law. Investigations 10.1, 10.2, and 10.3 are relevant to this program. The graph and console report these related diagnostics together, allowing a student to compare them without repeating the same calculation.

Physical Background

The Dimensionless Variable x

Following Investigation 10.2, define

Dimensionless variable
\[x=\frac{hc}{\lambda kT}=\frac{h\nu}{kT}.\]

Spectral Radiance per Unit Wavelength

The standard Planck spectral radiance is

Per unit wavelength
\[ B_\lambda(\lambda,T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda kT)}-1} =\frac{2k^5T^5}{h^4c^3}\frac{x^5}{e^x-1}. \]

The corresponding hemispheric spectral exitance is \(M_\lambda=\pi B_\lambda\). Planck2 plots radiance, so its y-axis retains sr\(^{-1}\) and the wavelength prefactor does not contain an extra factor of \(\pi\).

Spectral Radiance and Energy Density per Unit Frequency

Per unit frequency
\[ B_\nu(\nu,T)=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/(kT)}-1} =\frac{2k^3T^3}{h^2c^2}\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}. \]

For isotropic black-body radiation,

Spectral energy density
\[ u_\nu=\frac{4\pi}{c}B_\nu =\frac{8\pi k^3T^3}{h^2c^3}\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}. \]

Why the Wavelength and Frequency Peaks Differ

The wavelength shape \(x^5/(e^x-1)\) peaks at \(x\approx4.965114\), whereas the frequency shape \(x^3/(e^x-1)\) peaks at \(x\approx2.821439\). Therefore the wavelength at which \(B_\lambda\) is greatest is not equal to \(c/\nu_\text{max}\) for \(B_\nu\). This is not a contradiction: a spectral density depends on which variable defines an interval.

Governing Equations

Shape Function and Stable Evaluation

The two dimensionless shapes are special cases of \(f_p(x)=x^p/(e^x-1)\), with \(p=5\) for wavelength radiance and \(p=3\) for the two frequency-based quantities. Planck2 evaluates

\[\ln f_p(x)=p\ln x-\ln(e^x-1).\]

Three numerical regimes are used:

  • small x: \(e^x-1\approx x\);
  • intermediate x: Python's expm1(x) evaluates \(e^x-1\) accurately;
  • large x: \(\ln(e^x-1)\approx x\), avoiding overflow.

The defaults are \(x_\text{low}=0.05\) and \(x_\text{high}=20\).

Peak Finding

The reported peak is the largest sampled point within the selected finite domain \([x_\text{min},x_\text{max}]\). It may therefore be an endpoint if the mathematical peak lies outside that domain. No interpolation or root-finding is used, so peak-location accuracy is limited by \(\Delta x\). Increasing n_steps refines the sampled peak.

Dimensionless Area

The trapezoidal rule integrates the sampled shape over the finite x-domain. Exact references are

\[ \int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx=\frac{\pi^4}{15},\qquad \int_0^\infty\frac{x^5}{e^x-1}\,dx=\frac{8\pi^6}{63}. \]

The \(p=5\) integral is a useful numerical check, but it is not the bolometric integral of \(B_\lambda\), because changing from x to wavelength introduces the Jacobian \(|d\lambda/dx|\propto x^{-2}\).

Physical Integral and the Stefan–Boltzmann Law

Planck2 also integrates the plotted physical quantity using the appropriate coordinate Jacobian:

\[ \int_0^\infty B_\lambda\,d\lambda =\int_0^\infty B_\nu\,d\nu =\frac{\sigma T^4}{\pi}. \]

For the frequency energy density,

\[\int_0^\infty u_\nu\,d\nu=\frac{4\sigma}{c}T^4.\]

Comparing result.physical_integral at different temperatures therefore gives a direct numerical test of the \(T^4\) Stefan–Boltzmann scaling.

Finite numerical domain. The exact reference values above integrate from \(0\) to \(\infty\), whereas Planck2's numerical result covers only x_min through x_max. Its small remaining difference from the exact reference includes finite-domain truncation, trapezoidal discretization, and the selected asymptotic approximations. With all default settings, the relative error of the physical integral is less than \(10^{-6}\) in each of the three quantity modes. Printed digits in the sampled peak are not an uncertainty estimate; peak-location accuracy remains limited by \(\Delta x\).

Algorithm

  1. Validate the temperature, quantity, domain, and resolution.
  2. Divide \([x_\text{min},x_\text{max}]\) into exactly n_steps intervals, giving n_steps + 1 endpoints.
  3. Evaluate the dimensionless shape with stable small-, intermediate-, and large-x formulas.
  4. Multiply by the physical prefactor to obtain \(B_\lambda\), \(B_\nu\), or \(u_\nu\).
  5. Record the largest sampled shape value and its physical wavelength or frequency.
  6. Apply the trapezoidal rule only between actual neighboring sampled endpoints.
  7. Accumulate both the dimensionless shape area and the physical integral using the proper coordinate Jacobian.

Plot Windowing

A uniform x-grid maps non-uniformly into physical wavelength or frequency. The plotter sorts the points by physical coordinate and, by default, displays the region where the curve exceeds a small fraction of its peak. Integration still uses the full x-domain. Set y_frac_window=0 to display the complete computed coordinate range.

Module Overview

Physics Layer
planck2_physics.py

Physical constants, the three-regime log-shape-function evaluator, the quantity-specific physical prefactors, and the \(x\)-to-wavelength/frequency conversions. It also supplies the coordinate Jacobians and exact bolometric reference values.

def shape_function(x, quantity, domain) -> f(x) (dimensionless) def prefactor(quantity, T) -> physical scale factor
Driver Layer
planck2_driver.py

Samples the domain, evaluates the shape function and physical quantity at each point, tracks the peak, and accumulates both dimensionless and physical integrals. Returns a structured Planck2Result ready for plotting.

def run_planck2( T, quantity, n_steps=2000, domain=None ) -> Planck2Result
Plot Layer
planck2_plot.py

Renders the curve against physical units, auto-windows the displayed range around the peak, and annotates peak location/value and area in a corner text box.

def plot_planck2( result, corner="upper right", y_frac_window=0.003 ) -> None (displays figure)
Entry Point
main.py

User-editable entry point. Set temperature, quantity, resolution, domain boundaries, approximation thresholds, and plot controls here, then run the calculation.

from planck2_driver import run_planck2 from planck2_plot import plot_planck2 result = run_planck2(T=..., quantity=...) plot_planck2(result)

All ordinary experiment settings are exposed near the top of main.py. Physical quantities are expressed in SI units throughout.

Positive finite settings whose derived physical values fall outside the representable floating-point range are also rejected rather than returning infinities or unusable zero-valued results.

Quantity Modes

The quantity parameter passed to run_planck2() selects which physical form of Planck's law is computed and plotted.

Mode string Physical quantity Shape function x-axis
"wavelength" Spectral radiance \(B_\lambda\) (W m⁻³ sr⁻¹) \(x^5/(e^x-1)\), peaks at \(x\approx4.9651\) Wavelength (m)
"frequency" Spectral radiance \(B_\nu\) (W m⁻² sr⁻¹ Hz⁻¹) \(x^3/(e^x-1)\), peaks at \(x\approx2.8214\) Frequency (Hz)
"energy_density" Spectral energy density \(u_\nu\) (J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹) \(x^3/(e^x-1)\), peaks at \(x\approx2.8214\) Frequency (Hz)
Verification tip: Run "frequency" and "energy_density" side by side. Both peak at the same \(x\) (and hence the same frequency) since they share the same shape function \(g(x)\) — only the physical prefactor and y-axis units differ. Compare either against "wavelength" and note the peak lands at a different \(x\), and therefore corresponds to a different photon, per the discussion in Physical Background.

Suggested Experiments

EXP-1

Solar Black-Body Approximation

With T=5900 and quantity="wavelength", compare the sampled peak wavelength with Wien's displacement law. The result should be near 490 nm, in the visible range. A real solar spectrum also contains absorption lines and other departures from an ideal black body.

EXP-2

Wien's Displacement Law and Temperature

Run quantity="wavelength" at T = 3000, 5900, 10000 K. Verify that x_peak stays nearly constant while \(\lambda_\text{peak}T\) stays near \(2.898\times10^{-3}\,\text{m K}\). Then try "frequency" and verify that the peak frequency is proportional to \(T\).

EXP-3

Cosmic Microwave Background

Set T=2.725 and quantity="frequency". The peak should be near 160 GHz. As an optional extension, research why the CMB's near-perfect black-body spectrum became important evidence for a hot early universe and against classical steady-state cosmology.

EXP-4

Wavelength vs. Frequency Peaks

At the same temperature, run "wavelength" and "frequency". Record the \(B_\lambda\) peak wavelength, the \(B_\nu\) peak frequency, and \(c/\nu_\text{max}\). The two wavelengths do not agree because the curves are densities with respect to different variables.

EXP-5

Frequency Radiance vs. Energy Density

At one temperature, compare "frequency" and "energy_density". Verify that x_peak and the peak frequency agree. Then calculate the ratios of their peak values and physical integrals, and explain why both ratios equal \(4\pi/c\), with the corresponding change of units.

EXP-6

Stefan–Boltzmann T⁴ Scaling

Use, for example, T = 3000, 6000, 12000 K and record the printed physical integral. For either radiance mode, calculate physical_integral / T**4 and verify that it is nearly constant and agrees with \(\sigma/\pi\). Multiply a radiance integral by \(\pi\) to recover the hemispheric exitance \(\sigma T^4\). For "energy_density", verify agreement with \(4\sigma/c\). When \(T\) doubles, the integral should increase by about a factor of 16.

EXP-7

Resolution and Peak Sampling

Try n_steps = 10, 100, 1000, 2000, 20000. Make a small table of n_steps, x_peak, dimensionless_area, and relative area error, defined as abs(numerical - reference) / abs(reference). Use \(8\pi^6/63\) as the dimensionless reference for "wavelength" and \(\pi^4/15\) for either frequency-based mode. The trapezoidal-rule area and the sampled peak location need not converge at the same rate because the peak is chosen only from grid points, without interpolation. For the physical integral, remember that the exact reference spans \(0<x<\infty\) while the numerical result uses the selected finite domain.

EXP-8

Rayleigh–Jeans and Wien Limits

First record the default peak and integrals. For small \(x\), Planck's law approaches the Rayleigh–Jeans form; for large \(x\), it approaches Wien's approximation. Move x_low upward and x_high downward, for example to 1 and 3, so that these limiting forms are deliberately used too far from their valid regimes. These two settings change only the approximation-switch regions; they do not change the physical domain set by x_min and x_max. Compare with the defaults and explain which limiting approximation is responsible for each change.

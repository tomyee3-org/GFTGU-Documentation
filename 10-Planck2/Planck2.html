Description

Planck2 plots black-body radiation according to Planck's law, in the user's choice of three physically distinct forms: spectral radiance per unit wavelength, spectral radiance per unit frequency, or spectral energy density per unit frequency. All three curves are drawn against physical wavelength or frequency (not the dimensionless variable \(x\) used internally), and the peak location, peak value, and area under the dimensionless shape curve are printed directly on the graph — by default in the upper-right corner, where the falling tail of the curve is least likely to be present.

These distinct renderings of the black-body radiation curve are offered since instruments and textbooks quote intensity per unit wavelength, per unit frequency, or (for radiative-transfer and cosmology work) as an energy density — three quantities that are not simple rescalings of one another and, notably, do not even peak at the same photon energy.

From these curves, the student may numerically confirm Wien's law and the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. The student may also explore such aspects as the discrepancy between the peaks in wavelength-mode versus frequency-mode, compare the Sun's peak wavelength against the theoretical peak given by Wien's law, and can confirm the remarkable near-identity of published Cosmic Microwave Background curves with the ideal black-body curve.

Physical Background

The Dimensionless Variable x

Following Investigation 10.2 (Eq. 10.5), define

Eq. 10.5 — dimensionless variable
\[ x = \frac{hc}{\lambda k T} \]

where \(h\) is the Planck constant, \(c\) the speed of light, \(k\) the Boltzmann constant, \(\lambda\) the wavelength, and \(T\) the black-body temperature in kelvin. As explained in the text, \(x\) is a genuinely dimensionless number because it is the exponent of \(e\) in the Planck function, and exponents cannot carry units.

Spectral Radiance per Unit Wavelength

Substituting Eq. 10.6 (\(\lambda = hc/xkT\)) into the Planck function (Eq. 10.1) gives Eq. 10.7 of the text:

Eq. 10.7 — spectral radiance, per unit wavelength
\[ B_\lambda = \frac{2\pi k^5 T^5}{h^4 c^3}\, f(x), \qquad f(x) = \frac{x^5}{e^x - 1} \]

Spectral Radiance and Energy Density per Unit Frequency

Converting instead to frequency (\(\nu = c/\lambda\), so \(x = h\nu/kT\)) changes the power of \(x\) in the shape function from 5 to 3, because the Jacobian \(|d\lambda/d\nu| = c/\nu^2\) introduces two extra inverse powers of \(x\):

Spectral radiance, per unit frequency
\[ B_\nu = \frac{2 k^3 T^3}{h^2 c^2}\, g(x), \qquad g(x) = \frac{x^3}{e^x - 1} \]

The spectral energy density (energy per unit volume per unit frequency, as used in Investigation 10.3's discussion of the Stefan–Boltzmann law) is related to \(B_\nu\) by a factor of \(4\pi/c\) (integrating specific intensity over solid angle, then dividing by \(c\) to convert flux to density), giving the same shape function \(g(x)\) but a different physical prefactor and different (volumetric) units:

Spectral energy density, per unit frequency
\[ u_\nu = \frac{4\pi}{c} B_\nu = \frac{8\pi k^3 T^3}{h^2 c^3}\, g(x) \]

Why the Peaks Differ

Because \(f(x) = x^5/(e^x-1)\) and \(g(x) = x^3/(e^x-1)\) are different functions of \(x\), they peak at different \(x_\text{peak}\) — approximately \(4.9651\) for \(f(x)\) and \(2.8214\) for \(g(x)\). This is the origin of a classic subtlety: the wavelength at which a black-body spectrum peaks (Wien's law, \(\lambda_\text{max} T = 0.29\) cm, Eq. 10.9) does not correspond to the same photon as the frequency at which the spectrum peaks.

Governing Equations

Shape Function and Its Logarithm

Both dimensionless shape functions above are special cases of \(f(x) = x^p/(e^x-1)\), with \(p=5\) for the wavelength form and \(p=3\) for the frequency and energy-density forms. Following Investigation 10.3's discussion of overflow in \(e^x\) for \(x \gtrsim 200\), Planck2 evaluates the function via its natural log, generalizing Eq. 10.12:

Eq. 10.12 (generalized) — log of the shape function
\[ \ln[f(x)] = p\ln x - \ln(e^x - 1) \]

and then exponentiates only the final result. Three regimes are used for \(\ln(e^x-1)\) itself, matching the text exactly:

  • \(x > x_\text{high}\): \(e^x \gg 1\), so \(\ln(e^x-1)\approx x\).
  • \(x < x_\text{low}\): \(e^x - 1 \approx x\) (Eq. 10.13), so \(\ln(e^x-1)\approx \ln x\).
  • otherwise: no approximation is needed; \(\ln(e^x - 1)\) is evaluated directly.
Note: The program's defaults use \(x_\text{low}=0.01\) and \(x_\text{high}=100\), exposed as explicit parameters of a PlanckDomain object, so a user can directly see how moving the approximation boundaries affects accuracy.

Peak Finding

The peak is found by direct comparison while stepping through \(x\): each new value of \(f(x)\) is compared with the largest value seen so far, and if larger, both the value and its \(x\) are recorded. No calculus or root- finding is used.

Area Under the Curve

The area under the dimensionless curve is accumulated by the trapezoidal rule (Figure 10.5 of the text), summing over each step of width \(\Delta x\):

Trapezoidal area accumulation
\[ \text{area} \;+\!= \; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(f(x) + f_\text{last}\bigr)\, \Delta x \]

Exact closed-form values exist for both cases used here, since \(\int_0^\infty x^p/(e^x-1)\,dx = p!\,\zeta(p+1)\): \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx = \pi^4/15 \approx 6.493939\) (Investigation 10.3's own check) and \(\int_0^\infty x^5/(e^x-1)\,dx = 8\pi^6/63 \approx 122.081167\). Planck2's main.py prints both the numerical and exact values so the accuracy of the chosen n_steps is immediately visible.

Algorithm

Overview

For a given temperature \(T\), quantity, and number of steps, the driver:

  1. Divides \([x_\text{min}, x_\text{max}]\) into n_steps equal intervals.
  2. At each sample \(x\), computes \(\ln[f(x)]\) using the three-regime approximation above, then exponentiates to get the dimensionless shape value.
  3. Multiplies by the quantity-specific physical prefactor (Physical Background) to get the physical spectral radiance or energy density.
  4. Converts \(x\) to physical wavelength or frequency (Eq. 10.6, or its frequency analogue) for plotting.
  5. Updates the running peak and accumulates the trapezoidal area.

Plot Windowing

The domain \([0.01, 100]\) in \(x\) is needed for area-integration accuracy, but maps to an enormous range in physical wavelength or frequency — far wider than where the curve is visually distinguishable from zero. The plotting routine therefore auto-windows the displayed axis range to where the curve exceeds a small fraction (default 0.3%) of its peak value, leaving the underlying data and area computation untouched. This window is computed in physical coordinate space, not sample-index space — because wavelength is a reciprocal (and therefore highly non-uniform) function of \(x\), an index-based margin was found during development to jump unpredictably far in coordinate space.

Tip: Pass y_frac_window=0 to plot_planck2() to see the full computed domain instead of the auto-windowed view.

Module Overview

Physics Layer
planck2_physics.py

Physical constants, the three-regime log-shape-function evaluator, the quantity-specific physical prefactors, and the \(x\)-to-wavelength/frequency conversions. The only file that needs touching to add a fourth quantity.

def shape_function(x, quantity, domain) -> f(x) (dimensionless) def prefactor(quantity, T) -> physical scale factor
Driver Layer
planck2_driver.py

Samples the domain, evaluates the shape function and physical quantity at each point, tracks the peak, and accumulates the area. Returns a structured Planck2Result ready for plotting.

def run_planck2( T, quantity, n_steps=2000, domain=None ) -> Planck2Result
Plot Layer
planck2_plot.py

Renders the curve against physical units, auto-windows the displayed range around the peak, and annotates peak location/value and area in a corner text box.

def plot_planck2( result, corner="upper right", y_frac_window=0.003 ) -> None (displays figure)
Entry Point
main.py

User-editable entry point. Set temperature, quantity, resolution, and annotation corner here, then run this file to compute and display the plot.

from planck2_driver import run_planck2 from planck2_plot import plot_planck2 result = run_planck2(T=..., quantity=...) plot_planck2(result)

Parameters

All parameters are set in main.py as arguments to run_planck2() and plot_planck2(). Defaults reproduce the Sun's spectrum (\(T=5900\) K, as used in the worked example on p.117 of the text).

Parameter Default Units Description
T 5900.0 K Black-body temperature
quantity "wavelength" — Which form of Planck's law to compute; see Quantity Modes
n_steps 2000 — Number of divisions of the dimensionless domain.
domain.x_min 0.01 — Lower bound of the dimensionless domain
domain.x_max 100.0 — Upper bound of the dimensionless domain
domain.x_low 0.01 — Below this, the small-x approximation to \(\ln(e^x-1)\) is used
domain.x_high 100.0 — Above this, the large-x approximation to \(\ln(e^x-1)\) is used
corner "upper right" — Where the peak/area annotation box is placed; also accepts "upper left", "lower right", "lower left"
y_frac_window 0.003 — Fraction of peak value used to auto-window the displayed axis range; 0 shows the full domain
Common substitutions: Room temperature black body: T=300. A red dwarf star: T=3200. Cosmic microwave background: T=2.725. The tungsten filament of an incandescent bulb: T=2800.

Quantity Modes

The quantity parameter passed to run_planck2() selects which physical form of Planck's law is computed and plotted.

Mode string Physical quantity Shape function x-axis
"wavelength" Spectral radiance \(B_\lambda\) (W m⁻³ sr⁻¹) \(x^5/(e^x-1)\), peaks at \(x\approx4.9651\) Wavelength (m)
"frequency" Spectral radiance \(B_\nu\) (W m⁻² sr⁻¹ Hz⁻¹) \(x^3/(e^x-1)\), peaks at \(x\approx2.8214\) Frequency (Hz)
"energy_density" Spectral energy density \(u_\nu\) (J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹) \(x^3/(e^x-1)\), peaks at \(x\approx2.8214\) Frequency (Hz)
Verification tip: Run "frequency" and "energy_density" side by side. Both peak at the same \(x\) (and hence the same frequency) since they share the same shape function \(g(x)\) — only the physical prefactor and y-axis units differ. Compare either against "wavelength" and note the peak lands at a different \(x\), and therefore corresponds to a different photon, per the discussion in Physical Background.

Suggested Experiments

EXP-1

Reproduce the Sun's Spectrum

With the default T=5900 and quantity="wavelength", confirm the peak wavelength printed matches Wien's law (\(\lambda_\text{max} = 0.29/T\) cm \(\approx 4.92\times10^{-7}\) m), in the visible range as the text notes for the Sun.

EXP-2

The Wavelength/Frequency Peak Discrepancy

Run the same temperature with all three quantities. Convert the frequency-mode peak back to a wavelength via \(\lambda = c/\nu\) and compare it to the wavelength-mode peak directly. They will not match — a concrete demonstration of the subtlety described in Physical Background.

EXP-3

Stefan–Boltzmann Scaling

Compute the area for quantity="energy_density" at several temperatures. Because the dimensionless area is temperature-independent (it is the same \(\pi^4/15\) regardless of \(T\)), all of the \(T^4\) scaling of total radiated energy comes from the physical prefactor alone — confirm this by checking that result.y_peak / T**3 is constant across temperatures.

EXP-4

Resolution Sensitivity

Run n_steps at 10, 100, 1000, and 20000 and compare each computed area against the exact closed-form value main.py prints. This procedure should show a visible improvement in accuracy as n_steps increases.

EXP-5

Moving the Approximation Boundaries

Construct a PlanckDomain with x_low and x_high moved much closer together (e.g. \(x_\text{low}=1\), \(x_\text{high}=3\)), forcing more of the domain through the exact \(\ln(e^x-1)\) branch. Confirm the result is unchanged to within numerical precision — the three regimes of Eq. 10.12/10.13 are approximations chosen for speed and overflow-safety, not for accuracy.

EXP-6

Cosmic Microwave Background

Set T=2.725 (the CMB temperature) and quantity="frequency". Confirm the peak frequency falls in the microwave band (order 10¹¹ Hz), consistent with the name. The student is invited to perform Internet research to understand why the near-perfect correspondence of the CMB spectrum with that of an ideal black body is considered powerful evidence for the hot Big Bang model of the early universe — and, in particular, why it was one of the decisive observations that ruled out competing cosmological models (such as the historical Steady State theory), which had no natural mechanism to produce such a spectrum.

Restoring Default Parameter Values

After experimenting with parameter values in main.py, you can restore the originals using any of the following methods:

  • Same session: Press Ctrl+Z (Windows) or Cmd+Z (macOS) repeatedly in PyCharm to undo your edits.
  • After reopening PyCharm: Right-click main.py in the Project panel → Local History → Show History → select a version from before your changes → click Revert.
  • Full reset: Re-download the ZIP from github.com/tomyee3-org/GFTGU-Programs and replace this program's folder with the fresh copy.

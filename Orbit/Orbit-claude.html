Overview

Orbit computes the trajectory of a test particle—planet, satellite, or comet—moving in the gravitational field of a fixed central mass under Newton's inverse-square law. The central mass stays at the origin; the particle's position and velocity are advanced with an adaptive predictor–corrector integrator.

The supplied defaults approximate Mercury at perihelion. All initial conditions, integration controls, and the display can be given on the command line. Five display modes show the x-y orbit, the velocity hodograph, position or velocity against time, or the specific kinetic, potential, and total energy. The console reports numerical diagnostics and the planar Keplerian elements of the starting state, with real-valued results shown to five significant digits.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: one force law fixes the whole orbit before any calculation is made, exact motion under it conserves energy and angular momentum, and the printed drift in those two quantities is how you tell how far to trust a step-by-step calculation of that motion. Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument, a command whose printed summary or figure illustrates that argument, and then the equation that the printed numbers follow from.

Scope: Orbit follows one test particle around a fixed centre. EarthOrbit introduces the numerical method with a uniform field, Binary lets the central body move, MercPert adds a perturbing third body, and RelativisticOrbit replaces the force law.
What this program is, and what it does not model. Orbit is a teaching model of a point particle moving in a plane in the Newtonian field of a fixed point mass. It has no relativity, no drag, no radiation pressure, no tides, no finite size for either body and no second body, so it does not reproduce, for example, the precession of Mercury's perihelion. The motion is computed step by step, so it carries a numerical error that is largest where the acceleration changes fastest. The Keplerian elements it prints describe the starting state exactly; the drift and closure numbers describe the integration. The point-mass singularity guard is a numerical safeguard and not the surface of any real body. Use the program to see how an orbit depends on speed, direction and distance, and to learn how to judge a numerical result. Beats 5 to 8 show how, and where, the numbers stop being trustworthy.

How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation that the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equation at work, not independent evidence that the equation is right. The outside checks the program offers are the exact Keplerian elements it prints for the starting state and the conservation diagnostics it prints for the run. Beats 3, 4 and 7 compare the integrated orbit with them.

Every beat uses the same program and the same printed summary. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Beat 0 runs the default orbit, Beat 1 changes the starting speed until the orbit is no longer bound, Beat 2 changes only its direction, Beat 3 starts the orbit at its far end and changes the central body, Beat 4 asks whether the orbit closes, and Beats 5 to 8 open up the numerical method: the corrector, the step control, how far to trust the result, and the cases where the default settings are not enough.

Run every command from the program's folder. Each run prints a summary and then opens a plot window; close the window to end the program. Each beat names the lines of the summary to look at. In several beats the printed numbers are what to study and the plot can be left until later. Lengths are in metres, speeds in metres per second, times in seconds, and energies and angular momenta are per unit mass of the particle, in J kg−1 and m2 s−1.

What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag. A red LAW tag marks a law of nature that the model adopts and does not derive; Eq. (1) is the only one. A green ODE tag marks a differential equation that the program integrates. A grey DEFINITION tag marks a quantity that is defined by a formula. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows from the equations already stated. A violet ALGORITHM tag marks a rule of the numerical method, which is a choice made by the program rather than physics: Eqs. (11), (13) to (16) and the singularity guard of Beat 8. The tags are there so that a law, a definition and a numerical rule are never mistaken for one another. Seventeen equations are numbered; a few unnumbered ones appear where they are needed.

Beat 0 · A planet in a fixed field

A test particle starts on the positive x-axis 4.6×1010 m from a fixed central mass with the Sun's gravitational parameter. It moves at 58 980 m s−1 in the +y direction, which is roughly Mercury at perihelion. Nothing acts on it but the gravity of the central mass. Ask the program what happens.

python main.py

Look at the orbit plot, at the summary lines from termination to azimuthal revolutions, and at the block that begins Keplerian elements at initial state. Ignore the rest of the printout for now.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the shape. periapsis radius is 4.6000e+10 m, which is where the particle started, and apoapsis radius is 6.9832e+10 m. The particle starts at its closest approach and swings out to 1.52 times that distance before it returns, so its path cannot be a circle. The eccentricity, 0.20575, is (6.9832 − 4.6)/(6.9832 + 4.6), the difference of the two radii divided by their sum. Then read the time. accepted steps: 761 and elapsed simulated time: 7.6025e+06 s is about 88 days, and azimuthal revolutions: 1.0000 says that the run stopped when the line from the central mass to the particle had turned through one full turn. The line Keplerian period, 7.6019e+06 s, is the exact period of the orbit that the starting state defines. The integrated run is about 570 s, or 0.0075%, longer than that (from the unrounded values), which Beat 7 examines. Then look at the plot: one closed, slightly squashed oval, with the central mass marked at the origin, not at its middle but nearer the right-hand end, where the particle started. Before reading on, write down how the pull of the central mass should change if the particle were twice as far away.

Then the law that produced that curve. The central mass pulls the particle toward itself with an acceleration that falls as the square of the distance.

(1)
Newton's law of gravitation, per unit mass LAW
\[ \mathbf a \;=\; -\frac{\mu}{r^{2}}\,\hat{\mathbf r}, \qquad r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}, \qquad \mu = GM . \]

Here \(\mu\) is the option --k, in m3 s−2. The program asks for the gravitational parameter \(\mu=GM\) and not for the central mass in kilograms, because only the product enters the motion, and because the particle's own mass cancels: a heavy and a light particle placed at the same point with the same velocity follow the same path. The default is the IAU nominal solar value, \(\mu_\odot=1.3271244\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\). Doubling \(r\) cuts the acceleration to a quarter. The acceleration always points at the central mass, and that one property makes most of what follows possible. The complete model is Eq. (1) together with Newton's second law, which turns it into four first-order equations:

(2)
Equations of motion ODE
\[ \dot x = v_x,\qquad \dot y = v_y,\qquad \dot v_x = -\frac{\mu\,x}{r^{3}},\qquad \dot v_y = -\frac{\mu\,y}{r^{3}} . \]

The solver advances these four numbers in small steps of time and records them as the arrays xs, ys, vxs, vys and ts of the result. The step-by-step formulation is Investigation 4.1 of Gravity from the Ground Up. The acceleration is evaluated by compute_acceleration() in physics_orbit.py. Beat 1 asks which starting speeds give an orbit that returns at all.

Beat 1 · Energy decides bound or unbound

The particle can be given any starting speed at the same place. Below a certain speed it stays bound to the central mass, and above it the particle leaves for ever. The dividing speed follows from one number, the energy per unit mass. Give the particle three different speeds and read what the program says about each.

python main.py --vyInit 70000
python main.py --vyInit 75961 --maxSteps 600
python main.py --vyInit 85000 --maxSteps 600
python main.py --vyInit 75961 --dt0 100 --eps1 0.001

Look, in each run, at conic classification, specific energy, eccentricity, apoapsis radius and Keplerian period, and at termination, azimuthal revolutions, max fractional energy drift, max absolute specific-energy drift and final specific energy. The second and third runs are capped at 600 accepted steps because a particle that never returns cannot complete a revolution (Beat 4); the fourth is capped by the default maxSteps, 20000, and takes noticeably longer than the others.

Three tasks, in order. First, classify. At 70000 m s−1 the orbit is elliptic with specific energy −4.3505e+08 J/kg, eccentricity 0.69841 and an apoapsis of 2.5905e+11 m, 5.6 times the starting distance, and the run finished its revolution in 3250 steps, elapsed simulated time 3.2499e+07 s. At 85000 m s−1 the orbit is hyperbolic, with specific energy +7.2745e+08 J/kg: the eccentricity is 1.5043, the semimajor axis is negative, −9.1218e+10 m, and apoapsis radius and Keplerian period print as n/a (unbound). After its 600 steps the particle is 3.2231e+11 m from the central mass, moving at 47735 m s−1 and still receding. Then the middle run, 75961 m s−1. Work out the escape speed for the starting distance, \(\sqrt{2\mu/r}\), which is 75961.2 m s−1. The starting speed is 0.2 m s−1 below it, and the program prints elliptic with specific energy −16283 J/kg, eccentricity 0.99999, an apoapsis of 8.1503e+15 m, about 0.86 light-years, and a period of 1.4189e+14 s, about 4.5 million years. On paper a speed three parts in a million below escape is bound. But these are properties of the starting state, worked out from it exactly. They are not results of the run, which stopped after 600 steps and 0.35591 of a revolution (azimuthal revolutions). Then look at max fractional energy drift: 0.00020322 and 0.00016149 in the first and third runs, but 6.0704 in the middle run. The absolute drift in the middle run, 98844 J/kg, is similar to the 88412 J/kg of the first run, but that comparison does not show the integration is equally good. The middle orbit is bound by only 16283 J/kg, and the drift is six times that. The energy of the computed particle has moved from below zero to above it (the line final specific energy, which is ½v2 − μ/r at the last accepted step, prints +82138 J/kg, against −16283 J/kg at the start), so after 600 steps the computed particle is on an unbound path. This run cannot tell you whether the real particle comes back, and you should not believe its trajectory. Say why the fractional number is 6.07, and what it is warning about. The fourth run shows what would be needed. With --dt0 100 --eps1 0.001 the absolute drift is 9.8799 J/kg, small compared with 16283, and the fractional drift is 0.00060676. It still covers only 0.27184 of a revolution in its 20000 steps, so even this run cannot show the return, which takes 4.5 million years of simulated time.

Then the energy. Each unit of the particle's mass has a kinetic energy and a potential energy, and their sum is the number that sorts the orbits.

(3)
Specific mechanical energy DEFINITION
\[ \mathcal E \;=\; \tfrac12\,v^{2}\;-\;\frac{\mu}{r}, \qquad v^{2}=v_x^{2}+v_y^{2}. \]

The potential energy \(-\mu/r\) is minus the work per unit mass needed to pull the particle out to infinite distance, so it is zero at infinity and negative everywhere else. The program prints the value for the starting state as specific energy, and the three runs gave −4.3505e+08, −16283 and +7.2745e+08 J/kg. The sign is the whole classification: \(\mathcal E<0\) is a bound orbit, \(\mathcal E=0\) is the marginal parabolic case, and \(\mathcal E>0\) is an unbound hyperbolic one. Energy is conserved because the force of Eq. (1) can be derived from a potential:

(4)
Energy is conserved DERIVED
\[ \frac{d\mathcal E}{dt} \;=\; \mathbf v\!\cdot\!\mathbf a \;+\; \frac{\mu\,\dot r}{r^{2}} \;=\; -\frac{\mu\,\dot r}{r^{2}} + \frac{\mu\,\dot r}{r^{2}} \;=\; 0, \qquad \mathbf v\!\cdot\!\mathbf r = r\,\dot r . \]

So an exact orbit keeps the energy it started with, and any change in the computed \(\mathcal E\) during a run is numerical error. That change is the drift the summary prints, defined properly in Beat 7. The dividing case follows from setting \(\mathcal E=0\) in Eq. (3):

(5)
Escape speed DERIVED
\[ v_{\rm esc}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}}, \qquad v_{\rm circ}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}} . \]

At the starting distance these are 75961.2 and 53712.7 m s−1. The fractional drift divides the change in \(\mathcal E\) by \(|\mathcal E_0|\), and in the middle run \(|\mathcal E_0|\) is only 1.6×104 J kg−1, which is 6×10−6 of the kinetic energy. A fractional drift of 6 there says that the computed energy has changed by six times the whole binding energy, enough to change its sign, and the sign is what decides the classification. Compare the absolute drift with \(|\mathcal E_0|\) before trusting any trajectory that starts this close to escape. The program prints n/a for the fractional drift only when \(|\mathcal E_0|\) is within one part in 1012 of the larger of \(\tfrac12v^2\) and \(\mu/r\); the middle run is far outside that, so it prints the 6.0704 above. For a nearly parabolic start, use the absolute drift, and compare it with \(|\mathcal E_0|\). The same tolerance decides the classification: a start within that tolerance of \(\mathcal E=0\) is called parabolic, as a typed value such as 75961.2143594 is (Experiment 5).

Beat 2 · Speed sets size, direction sets shape

Equation (3) contains the speed and not the direction of the velocity. Two particles at the same place with the same speed, aimed differently, therefore have the same energy. What can differ between them? Keep the speed of the default run and turn its direction through 45 degrees.

python main.py
python main.py --vxInit 41705.15 --vyInit 41705.15

In each run look at semimajor axis, Keplerian period, eccentricity, periapsis radius, apoapsis radius and periapsis longitude. In the second run look also at max fractional energy drift and closure radius residual.

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the sizes. Both orbits have semimajor axis 5.7916e+10 m and Keplerian period 7.6019e+06 s. The two speeds are the same, since √2 × 41705.15 is 58980.0, so the two energies are the same, and the size and period of an orbit depend on the energy alone. Then compare the shapes. The eccentricity is 0.20575 in the first run and 0.72192 in the second. Periapsis is 4.6000e+10 m in the first and 1.6105e+10 m in the second, 2.9 times closer, and apoapsis is 6.9832e+10 m against 9.9726e+10 m. The second orbit is much more elongated, and the line periapsis longitude: 236.63 deg says that its closest approach is no longer on the x-axis; the particle starts 123.37 degrees past it. Then compare the accuracy. In the second run max fractional energy drift is 0.0019332, 36 times the default run's 5.3444e-05, and closure radius residual is 0.0013742 where the default run's is 5.2282e-09. Say which of the two orbits should be harder for a step-by-step method, and why. Beat 8 returns to this.

Then what direction changes. The quantity that the direction of the velocity does change is the angular momentum per unit mass,

(6)
Specific angular momentum DEFINITION
\[ h \;=\; x\,v_y \;-\; y\,v_x . \]

For the two runs it is 4.6×1010 × 58980 = 2.7131×1015 m2 s−1 and 4.6×1010 × 41705.15 = 1.9184×1015 m2 s−1, since \(y=0\) at the start. It is a conserved quantity, because the acceleration of Eq. (1) points along the position vector and so exerts no torque: \(\dot h = x a_y - y a_x = 0\). The program prints its drift as a second check on the run. Energy and angular momentum together fix the shape of the orbit:

(7)
Orbit size and shape from \(\mathcal E\) and \(h\) DERIVED
\[ a=-\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal E},\qquad p=\frac{h^{2}}{\mu},\qquad e=\sqrt{1+\frac{2\mathcal E\,h^{2}}{\mu^{2}}},\qquad r_p=\frac{p}{1+e},\qquad r_a=\frac{p}{1-e}\ \ (e<1\ \text{only}). \]

The four printed radii and the eccentricity are these quantities: \(a\) is semimajor axis, \(p\) is semilatus rectum, \(r_p\) is periapsis radius and \(r_a\) is apoapsis radius. For the first run \(\mathcal E=-1.1457\times10^{9}\) J kg−1 gives \(a=5.7916\times10^{10}\) m, and \(p=5.5464\times10^{10}\) m gives \(e=0.20575\), \(r_p=4.6000\times10^{10}\) m and \(r_a=6.9832\times10^{10}\) m. For the second run the same \(\mathcal E\) gives the same \(a\), while the smaller \(h\) gives \(p=2.7732\times10^{10}\) m and \(e=0.72192\). For a given energy, \(h\) is largest, and the orbit least eccentric, when the velocity is perpendicular to the position, which is what a circular orbit needs (\(v=v_{\rm circ}\) of Eq. (5) gives \(e=0\)). At the other extreme, \(h=0\) is a straight line through the centre, the radial case of Beat 8. The formulas for a parabola and a hyperbola are the same with \(a\to\infty\) and \(a<0\), except that \(r_a\) exists only for an ellipse, \(e<1\). An unbound orbit has no apoapsis. For the 85000 m s−1 orbit of Beat 1, \(e=1.5043\) would give \(p/(1-e)=-2.2844\times10^{11}\) m, which is not a distance, and the program prints n/a (unbound); the periodic time of Eq. (9) is likewise n/a. The elements are computed once, from the starting state, by keplerian_elements() in physics_orbit.py.

Beat 3 · Equal areas and the period

Because \(h\) is fixed, the particle has to move more slowly where it is farther from the central mass. Test that by starting the default orbit from its far end, at its apoapsis. Then change the central body altogether, and see whether one relation between period and size survives.

python main.py --xInit 6.9832e10 --vyInit 38851.5
python main.py --xInit 3.626e8 --vyInit 1082 --k 3.986e14 --dt0 1000

In the first run look at eccentricity, semimajor axis, periapsis radius, Keplerian period, periapsis longitude and initial true anomaly. In the second look at Keplerian period and elapsed simulated time.

Three tasks, in order. First, find the starting speed for the far end. The default orbit has \(h=4.6\times10^{10}\times58980=2.7131\times10^{15}\) m2 s−1. Divide by the printed apoapsis radius, 6.9832×1010 m, and the speed there is 38851.5 m s−1, which is what the command uses. Then read the first run. The eccentricity 0.20575, the semimajor axis 5.7916e+10 m and the period 7.6019e+06 s are those of the default orbit to all five digits, and periapsis radius is 4.6000e+10 m again, although the particle started 1.52 times farther out. The lines periapsis longitude: 180.00 deg and initial true anomaly (CCW): 180.00 deg say that it started exactly opposite periapsis. It is the same ellipse entered at the other end, and the particle here moves 1.52 times more slowly (38852 against 58980 m s−1) because it is 1.52 times farther away. Then the second run, a Moon-like particle around a fixed Earth: Keplerian period is 2.4034e+06 s, which is 27.8 days, and elapsed simulated time is 2.4034e+06 s. Then form \(T^2/a^3\) for both orbits from their printed periods and semimajor axes, 7.6019e+06 s with 5.7916e+10 m and 2.4034e+06 s with 3.8780e+08 m. You get 2.9747×10−19 and 9.9044×10−14 s2 m−3, which are nothing like each other. Multiply each by its own \(\mu\), 1.3271244×1020 and 3.986×1014, and both come to 39.48. Say what that number is.

Then the two laws. The area swept out by the line from the central mass to the particle grows at a rate that is fixed by \(h\):

(8)
Kepler's second law DERIVED
\[ \frac{dA}{dt}\;=\;\tfrac12\,\lvert\mathbf r\times\mathbf v\rvert\;=\;\tfrac12\,\lvert h\rvert \]

and since \(h\) is constant, equal areas are swept in equal times. For the default orbit that rate is 1.3565×1015 m2 s−1, at every point. Near periapsis the line is short and must turn quickly, and near apoapsis it is long and turns slowly: speed times distance is the same at the two ends, which is the relation you used in the first task. The whole ellipse has area \(\pi a b\), and the sweep rate is constant, so the period is \(T=\pi ab/(h/2)\). With \(b^2=ap\) and \(p=h^2/\mu\) from Eq. (7) this becomes Kepler's third law:

(9)
Kepler's third law DERIVED
\[ T \;=\; 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{\mu}}, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{T^{2}\mu}{a^{3}} \;=\; 4\pi^{2}\approx 39.48 . \]

So \(T^2/a^3\) is the same for every orbit around the same central body, and it changes from body to body only through \(\mu\). That is why the Sun and the Earth give different values above and the same 39.48 once \(\mu\) is included. The printed Keplerian period is Eq. (9). The integration never uses Eq. (9); it counts revolutions by the angle turned (Beat 4). That the integrated elapsed simulated time agrees with Eq. (9) to better than one part in 104 is therefore a check on the integration, and Beat 7 shows how that agreement improves.

Beat 4 · Position and velocity both repeat

An exact orbit around a fixed centre closes on itself: after one revolution the particle is back at its starting position with its starting velocity, and the next revolution retraces the first. The program counts revolutions by the angle the particle has turned through, and then compares the end state of the run with the start state. Run the default orbit for one, two and five revolutions, and look at the velocity-space plot of the first.

python main.py --output velocity
python main.py --maxOrbits 2
python main.py --maxOrbits 5
python main.py --vyInit -58980 --output velocity

Look, in each run, at azimuthal revolutions, closure radius residual and closure velocity residual. In the first and fourth runs look at the plot, which shows the velocity components \((v_x,v_y)\) as a point that moves as the particle orbits. A cross marks the origin, which is zero velocity, and a dot marks the initial velocity. The fourth run starts at the same speed in the −y direction, so the particle circles the central mass clockwise.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the closure lines of the one-revolution run: 5.2282e-09 for the radius and 4.2156e-05 for the velocity. After one revolution the particle is back within 5 parts in 109 of its starting distance, and its velocity vector is within 4 parts in 105 of its starting velocity. Then compare the three runs. The velocity residuals are 4.2156e-05, 8.4305e-05 and 0.00021071, which grow in proportion to the number of revolutions, by 2.00 and 5.00 times. The radius residuals are 5.2282e-09, 2.0868e-08 and 1.3020e-07, which grow as the square of it, by 3.99 and 24.9 times. Say why the two residuals grow differently. Then look at the velocity-space plot of the first run. It is a single circle. Along the \(v_y\) axis it runs from the initial dot at +58980 m s−1, which is periapsis, down to a lowest point near −38852 m s−1, the apoapsis speed of Beat 3, so its diameter is 97832 m s−1 and its radius 48916 m s−1. Its centre is 10064 m s−1 above the origin, half of 58980 − 38852. Divide the second number by the first: 10064/48916 = 0.2057, the eccentricity. The cross at the origin lies inside the circle. Then look at the fourth run. The circle has the same radius and size, but now its lowest point is the initial dot, at −58980 m s−1, and the circle extends up to about +38850 m s−1, so its centre is 10064 m s−1 below the origin. Reversing the direction of motion reverses the side of the origin on which the centre lies, and nothing else; the closure residuals are the same 5.2282e-09 and 4.2156e-05.

Then why the velocity circles. Combine Eq. (1) with the constant \(h\) of Eq. (6). The velocity vector of the particle moves on a circle of fixed radius whose centre does not move:

(10)
The velocity-space circle (hodograph) DERIVED
\[ \bigl\lvert\mathbf v-\mathbf v_c\bigr\rvert \;=\; \frac{\mu}{\lvert h\rvert}, \qquad \lvert\mathbf v_c\rvert \;=\; \frac{e\,\mu}{\lvert h\rvert}, \]

where \(\mathbf v_c\) points at right angles to the direction of periapsis, a quarter of a turn counter-clockwise from it when \(h>0\) and clockwise when \(h<0\). Both radii use \(\lvert h\rvert\), because \(h\) of Eq. (6) is negative for clockwise motion and a radius cannot be. For the default orbit \(\mu/\lvert h\rvert=1.3271244\times10^{20}/2.7131\times10^{15}=48916\) m s−1 and \(e\mu/\lvert h\rvert=10064\) m s−1, which are the two numbers taken from the plot. In the clockwise run \(h=-2.7131\times10^{15}\) m2 s−1 and the numbers are the same. The origin is inside the circle when \(e<1\), on it when \(e=1\) and outside it when \(e>1\), which is a second way to see Beat 1: a bound orbit is one whose hodograph encloses the origin. A circle is a closed curve, so the velocity returns to its starting value exactly when the particle returns to its starting place. For the ideal inverse-square field of Eq. (1), that closing is exact for every bound orbit. It is a special property of this force. Bertrand's theorem says that only two central forces have it for all bounded orbits, the inverse-square law and a spring force proportional to \(r\) (Experiment 14). So the growth of the residuals is not physics but the integration. The likely reason is that the computed orbit is nearly the same ellipse traversed at a very slightly wrong rate, so each revolution leaves the particle behind by about the same small angle (Beat 7 shows the elapsed time is a little longer than the period). The start is at periapsis, where the distance is least, so a small lag changes the distance only as its square, but changes the direction of the velocity in proportion to itself.

The revolution count that started the run is itself a rule of the program, not physics. After every accepted step it takes the direction of the particle, \(\varphi_n=\operatorname{atan2}(y_n,x_n)\), and adds up the signed change between steps:

(11)
Revolution counting and stopping ALGORITHM
\[ N_{\rm rev}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left\lvert\sum_n\Delta\varphi_n\right\rvert, \qquad \Delta\varphi_n\in(-\pi,\pi], \qquad \text{stop when } N_{\rm rev}=\texttt{maxOrbits}. \]

Each \(\Delta\varphi_n\) is wrapped into the interval \((-\pi,\pi]\), which is why the method works for clockwise or counter-clockwise motion and for any starting direction, and why a separate safeguard has to keep one step from turning through more than half a turn (Beat 6). maxOrbits need not be a whole number. If a step would carry the count past the target, the program repeats that step with a shorter timestep, bracketing the target, until the count lands on it. This is the endpoint refinement trials line, 7 in the default run. The final state is therefore a real point of the computed orbit and is not interpolated between two points. Finally, the closure lines compare the end state with the start:

(12)
Closure residuals DEFINITION
\[ \delta_r=\frac{\bigl\lvert\,r_{\rm end}-r_{0}\bigr\rvert}{r_{0}}, \qquad \delta_v=\frac{\lvert\mathbf v_{\rm end}-\mathbf v_{0}\rvert}{\lvert\mathbf v_{0}\rvert}. \]

They mean something only after a whole number of revolutions, so with --maxOrbits 1.5 both lines print n/a (Experiment 8). If the starting speed is zero, \(\delta_v\) is the absolute difference in m s−1. The residuals are not a full error measure: they test where the particle ends, not how far along its orbit it is, and they are largest for the quantities that change fastest at the starting point.

Beat 5 · The corrector

The equations of motion, Eq. (2), have an exact solution for a single fixed centre: the conic sections of Beat 2, from which the printed elements come. Orbit deliberately integrates them step by step instead, so that you can see a method that also works for forces that have no exact solution, and the elements then serve as an outside check. To take one step the method needs the acceleration at the end of the step, but the end position depends on that acceleration. The way out has two stages. A predictor makes a first estimate of the end of the step, and a corrector uses the average of the acceleration at the start and at the end to improve it. The improved position gives a better end acceleration, so the corrector can be repeated. The option eps2 decides when to stop repeating. Run the default orbit with a very strict eps2 and then with one so loose that the corrector is never repeated.

python main.py --eps2 1e-8
python main.py --eps2 10
python main.py --dt0 1e6 --eps1 10
python main.py --dt0 1e6 --eps1 10 --eps2 1e-12

Look at accepted steps, acceleration evaluations, max fractional energy drift and closure radius residual. In the last two runs look at accepted steps only. The default run, eps2 = 1e-4, gave 761 steps, 5.3444e-05 and 5.2282e-09.

Three tasks, in order. First, read accepted steps: 761 in both runs, as in the default. The tolerance changed by a factor of 109 and the number of steps did not change at all, so in these runs eps2 did not change the accepted steps. Then read max fractional energy drift: 5.3444e-05 for the strict run, identical to the default to all five digits, and 5.2647e-05 for the loose one, 1.5% smaller, not larger. Then read closure radius residual: 5.2073e-09 for the strict run, 5.2282e-09 for the default and 4.7641e-07 for the loose one, which is 91 times larger. So eps2 does change something, but not the thing that the energy drift measures. Say what it changes, and why a corrector that does less work can have the smaller energy drift. Then read acceleration evaluations: 1534 for the loose run, 2301 for the default and 3062 for the strict, which is about two, three and four per accepted step. That is what a tighter eps2 costs. Last, the two runs with --dt0 1e6 --eps1 10. With the default eps2 the run takes 14 accepted steps, and with eps2 = 1e-12 it takes 24. So eps2 normally decides only when the corrector stops repeating, but it can also change the steps: a trial step for which the corrector has not converged after ten passes is thrown away and retried at half the length, and a demanding eps2 on a very long step can cause that.

Then the two stages. The predictor is a constant-acceleration estimate of where the particle will be, and how fast it will be moving, at the end of a step of length \(\Delta t\):

(13)
Predictor ALGORITHM
\[ \tilde{\mathbf v}=\mathbf v_n+\mathbf a_n\,\Delta t, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r_n+\tfrac12\,(\mathbf v_n+\tilde{\mathbf v})\,\Delta t . \]

The program evaluates the acceleration at \(\tilde{\mathbf r}\) and applies the trapezoidal corrector, which replaces the acceleration over the step by the average of its values at the two ends:

(14)
Iterated trapezoidal corrector ALGORITHM
\[ \mathbf v_{n+1}=\mathbf v_n+\tfrac12\,(\mathbf a_n+\mathbf a_{n+1})\,\Delta t, \qquad \mathbf r_{n+1}=\mathbf r_n+\tfrac12\,(\mathbf v_n+\mathbf v_{n+1})\,\Delta t . \]

The end acceleration \(\mathbf a_{n+1}\) depends on the end position \(\mathbf r_{n+1}\), which is what Eq. (14) is computing. The program therefore starts from the predicted values, applies Eq. (14), recomputes the acceleration at the improved position and applies it again. It stops when the estimate of the velocity increment over the step stops changing:

(15)
Corrector convergence test ALGORITHM
\[ \rho_k \;=\; \frac{\bigl\lVert\Delta\mathbf v^{(k)}-\Delta\mathbf v^{(k-1)}\bigr\rVert} {\bigl\lVert\Delta\mathbf v^{(0)}\bigr\rVert}, \qquad \text{accept when } \rho_k\le\varepsilon_2 , \]

where \(\Delta\mathbf v^{(k)}=\mathbf v^{(k)}-\mathbf v_n\) is the velocity increment after \(k\) passes and \(\Delta\mathbf v^{(0)}=\tilde{\mathbf v}-\mathbf v_n\) is the predictor's. The test is applied to the increment over the step and not to the velocity itself, so eps2 is tied to the size of the correction and not to the much larger orbital speed. It is not a tolerance on the error of the orbit, the period or the energy. If ten passes (MAX_CORRECTOR_ITERATIONS in driver_orbit.py) do not satisfy it, the whole step is rejected and retried at half the length. In the default run and in the first two runs above the corrector converges long before that limit. In the last run above, and in the third run of Beat 6, it does not always, and some trial steps are halved for that reason.

What the two runs show follows from Eqs. (13) and (14). With eps2 = 10 the test is met after the first pass. That pass is an explicit second-order method, the predictor followed by one averaging of the two accelerations, and it costs two acceleration evaluations a step. With the default the second pass is what confirms convergence, and with 1e-8 a third pass is. Each extra pass solves the implicit trapezoidal equations of Eq. (14) more completely, and that is what makes the closure radius residual 91 times smaller. But the trapezoidal rule and the one-pass rule are both second-order accurate, so the energy drift, which is set by the length of the step (Beat 7), is the same for both to within a couple of per cent. That the loose corrector's drift is slightly the smaller is a feature of this orbit and of the sign of the errors, not a rule. In short, going from the loose setting to the default bought a closure 91 times better for 1.5 times the calls, and going from the default to the strict setting changed the energy drift not at all at five digits and the closure radius residual by only 0.4% (5.2282e-09 to 5.2073e-09), for another 1.3 times the calls.

Beat 6 · The step control

The program never takes a step longer than dt0, and it takes a shorter one when the acceleration changes too much during a step for the predictor to be trusted. The option eps1 sets how much is too much. With the default dt0 of 104 s this never happens: the 761 steps of Beat 0 are 760 steps of exactly 104 s and one last, shortened step that lands the run on the requested revolution (Beat 4, about 2.5×103 s). To watch the controller work, make dt0 ten times longer, then loosen eps1 so far that it cannot object, and then make dt0 a hundred times longer while it still cannot object.

python main.py --dt0 1e5
python main.py --dt0 1e5 --eps1 10
python main.py --dt0 1e6 --eps1 10

Look at accepted steps, elapsed simulated time, longest accepted step, angular-step rejections, max fractional energy drift and the two closure lines. The default run gave 761 steps, 7.6025e+06 s, 0 rejections, 5.3444e-05, 5.2282e-09 and 4.2156e-05.

Three tasks, in order. First, the run with the default eps1, which is 0.05. A step ten times longer should cover the orbit in about a tenth as many steps, or 76. The run took 181, and longest accepted step shows that no step reached 105 s; the longest was 88595 s. Its max fractional energy drift is 0.00083350, 15.6 times the default run's, and elapsed simulated time is 7.6098e+06 s, 0.10% longer than the period. Then the run with eps1 = 10: 77 steps, so nothing was shortened. Its drift is 0.0053649, 6.4 times larger than the run with the controller working, its closure radius residual is 5.3762e-05, and its elapsed time is 7.6598e+06 s, 0.76% longer than the period. Then the third run, with dt0 = 106 s and the acceleration-change test made powerless: angular-step rejections is 1, there are only 14 accepted steps, the drift is 0.21231 and closure velocity residual is 0.24791, so the particle finishes with a velocity that is 25% wrong. Then set the first two runs side by side. Both use steps of up to 105 s. What did the shorter steps buy, and what did they cost? A last observation: run the first command again with --dt0 1e6. You get 182 steps, not 181, and a drift of 0.00084994. With the controller working, raising dt0 a further factor of ten changes almost nothing, because eps1 is already deciding the step.

Then the rule. Before the corrector runs, the program compares the acceleration at the start of the trial step, \(\mathbf a_n\), with the acceleration \(\tilde{\mathbf a}\) at the position that the predictor of Eq. (13) gives for the end of the step:

(16)
Acceleration-change test ALGORITHM
\[ \delta_a \;=\; \frac{\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}-\mathbf a_n\rVert} {\max\!\left(\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}\rVert,\ \lVert\mathbf a_n\rVert\right)} . \]

If \(\delta_a>\varepsilon_1\), the attempt is discarded and the working timestep is halved (TIMESTEP_SHRINK_FACTOR). After an accepted step the working timestep is allowed to grow by 10% (TIMESTEP_GROWTH_FACTOR), up to dt0. The predictor treats the acceleration as constant across the step, and the acceleration changes fastest near periapsis, so \(\delta_a\) is the program's own measure of how good that assumption is. In the default run the largest \(\delta_a\) over the whole orbit is 0.0128, below \(\varepsilon_1=0.05\), so no trial step is ever rejected. In the first run above the largest accepted \(\delta_a\) is 0.0497: the controller holds it just under the limit, and the step size settles wherever that requires. With eps1 = 10 the accepted steps reach \(\delta_a=0.128\), and with dt0 = 106 s they reach 0.65, values that the default would have refused. Setting eps1 = 10 removes only this test. The controller has two more, which eps1 does not change. One is the corrector's limit of ten passes (Beat 5), which still halves some trial steps in the third run. The other is an independent guard on the angle turned. Because Eq. (11) unwraps the direction between one step and the next, a step that could turn the particle through half a turn or more would make the count ambiguous. The program therefore estimates the angular travel of each trial step and rejects it if it exceeds \(\pi/2\) (MAX_ANGULAR_STEP); that estimate is a conservative safeguard and not a proof. It is the angular-step rejections line, and the third run above is the only one among the commands of Beats 0 to 8 in which it acts.

Then the drift: 5.3444e-05, 1.3361e-05, 3.3401e-06 and 8.3504e-07, which falls by a factor of 4.00 at every halving. Then what the numbers say. The Energy drift column of the table is the quantity of Eq. (17), the largest departure of \(\mathcal E\) from its starting value during the run, divided by the starting value:

(17)
Energy drift DEFINITION
\[ \Delta\mathcal E_{\rm abs}=\max_n\,\bigl\lvert\mathcal E_n-\mathcal E_0\bigr\rvert, \qquad \Delta\mathcal E_{\rm frac}=\frac{\Delta\mathcal E_{\rm abs}}{\lvert\mathcal E_0\rvert}. \]

The angular-momentum drift is defined the same way with \(h\) in place of \(\mathcal E\), and the summary prints the absolute and fractional versions of both. A drift in either is direct evidence of numerical error, because Eqs. (4) and (7) say it should be zero. It is only evidence of one kind. A small energy drift does not by itself bound the error in the position, the period or the phase, which is why the summary also prints closure residuals and this beat also compares the elapsed time with the period. And a tolerance is not an error bound. Tightening eps1 or eps2 makes a test more demanding and can cost more computation (Beat 5 measured the cost), but there is no universal factor that turns a change in a tolerance into a change in the energy error. The reliable way to know how good a number is, is to repeat the run with a smaller dt0 and see whether the number you care about stops changing.

The table is a pattern you can use. Because the default drift scales as \(\Delta t^2\), one halving should divide it by four, and you can predict the next row before you run it. That prediction holds only while dt0 is the setting that decides the step size where the error is made. The default orbit is a mild test, with eccentricity 0.2. Beat 8 shows an orbit for which halving dt0 achieves nothing.

python main.py --vyInit 10000

In the fourth look at termination, elapsed simulated time, max fractional energy drift and the lines for periapsis and angular momentum.

Three tasks, in order. First, the shape. The eccentricity is 0.96534 and periapsis radius is 8.1127e+08 m, only 1.8% of the starting distance, and the period is 1.9530e+06 s. With the default settings the run takes 579 steps, and elapsed simulated time is 1.9477e+06 s, 0.27% shorter than the period, max fractional energy drift is 0.011176 and closure velocity residual is 0.032738. With --dt0 1000 the run takes 2176 steps, but the drift is 0.011729 and the velocity closure 0.031595, no better at all. Say which setting was limiting the accuracy, and why shortening dt0 did nothing. Then the fourth run. The elements it prints are those of a straight line: eccentricity 1.0000, semilatus rectum and periapsis radius both 0.0000 m. It ends with termination: point-mass singularity approached after 1171 steps, at elapsed simulated time 9.3547e+05 s, about eleven days, and max fractional energy drift is 1.8426e+05, so the energy has changed by 1.8×105 times its starting size. Say what the run tells you about the program's stopping point.

Then what limits the accuracy. Near periapsis the particle is fast and the acceleration changes rapidly, so the controller of Eq. (16) shortens the steps. In the first run 431 of the 579 steps are shorter than dt0, the shortest is 36.8 s, and near closest approach the largest accepted \(\delta_a\) is 0.0499, just under \(\varepsilon_1\). Steps of a few tens of seconds are already far below both 104 s and 103 s, so dt0 does not decide the step size there. It is only reached far from the centre, where nothing is happening. The error made at periapsis is set by eps1, and tightening it 10-fold shortens the periapsis steps to about 3.6 s. This is the general rule of the numerical beats: tighten the control that actually sets the step size where the error is made, and confirm it by repeating the run. It also settles the puzzle of Beat 2. The rotated orbit of that beat had eccentricity 0.72, and its energy drift was 36 times the default orbit's for the same reason: the closer periapsis makes the acceleration change faster, and that is what eps1 and the step decide. Repeating that run with --eps1 0.005 takes 2853 steps and brings the drift down from 0.0019332 to 1.9263e-05, a factor of 100, while --dt0 5000 reaches only 0.0012935.

The fourth run reaches the singularity of Eq. (1), where \(r=0\) and the acceleration is infinite. The program cannot evaluate it there, so it stops early, using a guard derived from the size of the problem and from floating-point spacing and not a fixed distance:

Singularity guard and stop radius ALGORITHM
\[ r_{\rm guard}=\max\!\bigl(10^{-12}\,r_0,\ 32\,\mathrm{ulp}(r_0)\bigr), \qquad r_{\rm stop}=1024\,r_{\rm guard}. \]

Here \(r_0\) is the starting distance and \(\mathrm{ulp}(r_0)\) is the spacing of floating-point numbers near it. For \(r_0=4.6\times10^{10}\) m the stop radius is 47 m, and the shortest accepted step in this run is about 2×10−10 s. A trial step that touches the guard is retried with half the length; the run ends only when an accepted state comes within \(r_{\rm stop}\) (constants SINGULARITY_GUARD_RELATIVE, SINGULARITY_GUARD_ULPS and SINGULARITY_STOP_FACTOR). That number is a numerical safeguard and not the surface of a star: the Sun's radius is about 7×108 m. And the printed drift is the warning that matters. By the time the run stops, the computed energy has changed by a factor of 105, so nothing computed near the singularity should be believed. This is what the diagnostics are for. A run can end normally, with a complete summary, and still not be trustworthy; only the drift and closure numbers say whether it is.

Algorithm

This section collects in one place the rules of the numerical method that Beats 4 to 8 introduced one at a time. Equations (13) and (14) are in Beat 5, Eq. (16) is in Beat 6, and the revolution count is Eq. (11) in Beat 4.

One accepted step

  1. Evaluate the acceleration at the start of the step from Eq. (2).
  2. Take a trial step of the working length Δt with the predictor, Eq. (13), and evaluate the acceleration at the predicted position. If the trial step reaches the singularity guard, halve Δt and repeat.
  3. Form δa from Eq. (16). If it exceeds eps1, halve Δt and return to step 2.
  4. Apply the trapezoidal corrector, Eq. (14), and repeat it until the velocity increments agree to eps2, Eq. (15). If ten passes do not suffice, halve Δt and return to step 2.
  5. Reject and halve a trial whose straight endpoint segment passes through the singularity guard, or whose estimated angular travel exceeds π/2.
  6. Accept the step and record position, velocity, time, kinetic and potential energy and angular momentum. Add the change in direction to the running total of Eq. (11); if the target would be passed, retry the step with a shorter length until the total lands on it. Otherwise let the working step grow by 10%, up to dt0, and begin the next step.

Termination

A run ends in one of three ways, reported by the termination line and stored as the TerminationReason value max_orbits, max_steps or central_singularity. max_orbits means that the revolution count of Eq. (11) reached maxOrbits. max_steps means that maxSteps accepted steps were taken first, as happens for an unbound or nearly radial orbit, which never completes a revolution. central_singularity means that an accepted state came within the stop radius of Beat 8. Closure residuals are reported only after max_orbits with a whole number of revolutions. Other failures, such as a timestep that can no longer advance the time, end the run with a one-line Orbit: ... message.

Tolerances

Two independent dimensionless tolerances control the integration: eps1 (default 0.05) rejects a trial step when the relative change in the acceleration vector is too large, and eps2 (default 10−4) requires successive corrected velocity increments to agree to that relative tolerance. They need not be ordered. A large value of eps1 such as 10 effectively prevents ordinary step rejection, and a large eps2 makes the corrector stop after its first pass. Neither tolerance is the only limit on the step: the corrector's ten-pass limit and the angular-step safeguard act whatever their values. Tighter tolerances make the local tests more demanding and can require more computation; they do not act on the same error as dt0 (Beats 5 to 8), and there is no universal factor relating a tolerance change to an error change. The safest test is to repeat the same physical run with stricter numerical controls and check that the result changes less and less.

Tip: If the energy drift is larger than you want, first decide which control sets the step size where the error is made. On a nearly circular orbit that is usually dt0. On an eccentric orbit it is eps1, which shortens the step at periapsis (Beat 8). eps2 mainly improves the closure of the orbit at some cost in computation (Beat 5).

Valid numerical inputs. The initial position must not be at r=0; --k, --dt0, --eps1, --eps2 and --maxOrbits must be positive; and --maxSteps must be a positive integer. All numeric inputs must be finite real numbers, and intermediate acceleration and conservation quantities must fit within ordinary floating-point range. An invalid value ends the run with a one-line message beginning Orbit:.

Output Modes

Pass one of these values to --output:

  • orbitThe x-y trajectory with equal axis scaling and the central mass marked at the origin (Beat 0).
  • velocityThe Hamilton hodograph, \((v_x,v_y)\), with equal axis scaling, a cross at the origin (zero velocity) and a dot at the initial velocity. For a non-radial Kepler orbit the velocity vectors lie on a circle; a bound ellipse traces the whole circle, and a parabolic or hyperbolic orbit traces only an arc (Beat 4).
  • position_timeThe position components \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\); useful for reading orbital periods and seeing the non-uniform orbital speed.
  • velocity_timeThe velocity components \(v_x(t)\) and \(v_y(t)\), which show the periodicity.
  • energyThe specific kinetic, potential and total energy against time. The total looks flat on this plot; use the printed drift for small departures (Beat 1).

Real-valued results are shown to five significant digits; counters are exact integers. Line by line:

  • termination is requested revolution count reached when Eq. (11) ended the run, maximum accepted-step count reached when maxSteps did, and point-mass singularity approached when an accepted state came within the stop radius (Beat 8).
  • accepted steps is the number of steps the program kept, excluding the initial state. Trial steps that were rejected and halved (Beat 6) are not counted. elapsed simulated time is the simulated time at the last accepted state.
  • azimuthal revolutions is the total angle turned, divided by \(2\pi\) (Eq. (11)). angular-step rejections counts trial steps refused by the angular safeguard (Beat 6), and endpoint refinement trials counts the trial steps used to place the final endpoint on the requested revolution (Beat 4).
  • acceleration evaluations counts every call the integrator makes to compute the acceleration, including those of trial steps that were rejected; it is the cost of the run (Beat 5). shortest accepted step and longest accepted step are the extremes of the timesteps that were kept (Beat 6); each reads n/a (no step accepted) if no step was accepted.
  • final specific energy is Eq. (3) evaluated at the last accepted state. Compare it with the specific energy of the starting state: for an exact orbit they are equal, and the difference is bounded by the absolute drift (Beat 1).
  • The four drift lines are Eq. (17) and its counterpart for \(h\) (Beat 7). If the starting energy is zero or nearly zero, or the starting angular momentum is zero or nearly zero, the corresponding fractional line reads n/a, with the reason, and the absolute line remains available. A fractional drift can still be large, and uninformative, when the starting energy is small but not that small (Beat 1).
  • The two closure lines are Eq. (12). Both read n/a unless the run ended at a whole number of revolutions.
  • The block Keplerian elements at initial state is computed exactly from the starting position and velocity and --k, with no integration (Beat 2). It gives the conic class, which is elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic, the specific energy of Eq. (3), the eccentricity, the semimajor axis, which is negative for a hyperbola and n/a for a parabola, the semilatus rectum, the periapsis radius, the apoapsis radius and the Keplerian period, which are n/a for an unbound orbit, and the longitude of periapsis and the initial true anomaly measured counter-clockwise in the x-y plane, which are n/a for a circular orbit. Orbit is two-dimensional, so inclination and the longitude of the ascending node are not represented.
  • For a hyperbolic orbit that ends at maxSteps or at a small maxOrbits target, two more lines give the final radius and final speed (Beat 1).

Specific energy means energy per unit mass of the orbiting particle, so the mass of the test particle is never needed.

Suggested Experiments

The experiments are arranged in approximate order of difficulty. The first few need only command-line changes; later ones ask you to interpret conservation laws, orbital geometry, or the numerical method, and the last few are programming exercises. Each cites the beat whose numbers it builds on.

1 · Reproduce Planetary Orbits — Introductory

python main.py
python main.py --xInit 1.471e11 --vyInit 30290

Start with the default Mercury orbit (Beat 0). Then use the perihelion distances and maximum orbital speeds from Table 4.3 of Gravity from the Ground Up to model other planets: place the planet on the positive x-axis at the perihelion distance in the second column and give it only the maximum y-direction speed from the third column. From the plotted perihelion and aphelion distances, estimate the eccentricity and compare it with the first column, using the formula in the table caption. This directly tests whether Newton's inverse-square law reproduces the observed shapes of planetary orbits. As a check on your method, the second command uses commonly quoted values for the Earth, a perihelion of about 1.471×1011 m and a speed of about 30.29 km s−1; it prints an eccentricity of 0.016950 and a period of 3.1570e+07 s, which is 365.4 days. To use the book's values, replace the numbers in this template with those of the planet you choose: 

python main.py --xInit PERIHELION_M --yInit 0 \
    --vxInit 0 --vyInit MAX_SPEED_M_PER_S

2 · Measure Planetary Periods — Introductory

python main.py --maxOrbits 1 --output position_time
python main.py --maxOrbits 1 --output velocity_time

For several of the planetary orbits from Experiment 1, set --maxOrbits 1 and compare the reported elapsed simulated time with the observed orbital period. You can also read the period from the time axis of the position-against-time or velocity-against-time plot. Compare your results with the planetary periods in Table 4.2 of the book. For the default orbit the run reports 7.6025e+06 s against the exact 7.6019e+06 s of Eq. (9), which is 88.0 days (Beat 3).

3 · Position and Velocity Both Repeat — Introductory to Intermediate

python main.py --maxOrbits 1 --output velocity

A closed orbit must return not only to its starting position but also to its starting velocity. Run one complete bound orbit and select --output velocity. The velocity-space curve should close just as the position-space orbit does. The console closure residuals give a numerical check: after an integral number of revolutions, both the final radius and velocity should be close to their initial values. Check the numbers of Beat 4: the circle has a radius of 48916 m s−1 and its centre is 10064 m s−1 from the origin. Try --vyInit -58980 as well: the orbit runs clockwise and the centre moves to the other side of the origin.

4 · Energy Conservation — Intermediate

# Bound default
python main.py --output energy

# Hyperbolic comparison
python main.py --vyInit 85000 --maxSteps 600 --output energy

After reading the introduction to gravitational energy in Chapter 6, select --output energy for a bound orbit. The kinetic and potential specific energies change continually, but their sum should remain nearly constant. Then make an unbound orbit and compare its total specific energy with that of the bound orbit: bound Kepler orbits have negative specific energy, hyperbolic orbits positive, and the marginal parabolic case zero. From Eq. (3) the total is −1.1457×109 J kg−1 for the first command and +7.2745×108 J kg−1 for the second. The console's drift lines show the remaining numerical error and give a quantitative check on conservation. Use the absolute drift for the parabolic case, where a fractional change from zero is undefined (Beat 1).

5 · Bound, Parabolic, and Hyperbolic Motion — Intermediate

python main.py --vyInit 70000
python main.py --vyInit 75961 --maxSteps 600
python main.py --vyInit 75961.2143594 --maxSteps 600
python main.py --vyInit 85000 --maxSteps 600

At a fixed starting radius \(r\), vary the initial speed through the escape value \(v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2\mu/r}\), Eq. (5). Below it the orbit is bound, at it the orbit is parabolic, and above it the orbit is hyperbolic. For unbound cases, increase --maxSteps if necessary, because the requested --maxOrbits may never be reached. Compare the trajectory class with the sign of the initial specific energy. A decimal value typed for \(v_{\rm esc}\) is rounded to floating-point precision, so its computed energy may be slightly positive or negative rather than exactly zero. The program therefore calls a start parabolic when its energy is within one part in 1012 of zero on the energy scale of the problem, which the third command satisfies and 75961 does not (Beat 1). Try speeds just below and above the threshold, and inspect the absolute drift, comparing it with the size of the initial specific energy, since the fractional drift is unreliable there and a trajectory whose drift exceeds that energy cannot be trusted (Beat 1).

6 · Direction Independence of Binding — Intermediate

# These two velocities have the same speed
python main.py --vxInit 0 --vyInit 58980
python main.py --vxInit 41705.15 --vyInit 41705.15

Keep \(v_x^2+v_y^2\) fixed while rotating the initial velocity vector at a fixed starting position. The orbit's shape and angular momentum change, but its bound or unbound classification, its semimajor axis and its period do not, because the specific energy depends on the speed and not on the direction of the velocity. Check numbers from Beat 2: the eccentricity moves from 0.20575 to 0.72192 and the periapsis from 4.6000e+10 m to 1.6105e+10 m, while the semimajor axis stays at 5.7916e+10 m. Try other angles at the same speed. What is the largest eccentricity you can reach?

7 · Approximate Moon Around a Fixed Earth — Intermediate

python main.py --xInit 3.626e8 --yInit 0 \
    --vxInit 0 --vyInit 1082 --k 3.986e14 \
    --dt0 1000 --maxOrbits 1

Replace the solar gravitational parameter by \(GM_\oplus\approx3.9860\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), and try xInit = 3.626e8 m with vyInit = 1082 m s−1. This fixed-Earth test-particle model has a Kepler period of about 27.8 days (the printed 2.4034e+06 s), and an eccentricity of 0.064989. Compare the result with the model's prediction, remembering that the real Earth–Moon system differs because the Moon's mass is not negligible. Beat 3 uses this run to test Kepler's third law.

8 · Repeat Several Revolutions — Intermediate

python main.py --maxOrbits 2
python main.py --maxOrbits 5
python main.py --maxOrbits 1.5

Set --maxOrbits 2 or 5 and examine whether successive numerical ellipses lie on top of one another. This makes accumulated integration error visible: the closure lines of Beat 4 grow with the number of revolutions. Fractional values such as 1.5 are also allowed, although closure residuals are reported only when the requested endpoint is an integral number of revolutions, so the third command prints n/a for both.

9 · Numerical Convergence — Intermediate to Advanced

# Baseline
python main.py

# Smaller maximum step and tighter tolerances
python main.py --dt0 5000 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6

Reduce --dt0 and tighten --eps1 and --eps2. Compare the orbital period, the energy drift, the angular-momentum drift and the closure residuals rather than judging accuracy from the orbit plot alone. Change one numerical control at a time so that you can tell which effect it has. In the second command above, the extra tightening changes nothing: the run is identical to --dt0 5000 alone, 1521 steps and a drift of 1.3361e-05, because at that step size neither the eps1 test nor the eps2 test is the limit. You can also make either tolerance deliberately large to see what happens when that accuracy control is effectively relaxed (Beats 5 and 6).

10 · Verify Kepler's Third Law — Advanced

python main.py
# Repeat with the Table 4.3 perihelion position and speed for other planets.

Run several bound planetary orbits with --maxOrbits 1. For each, use the printed Keplerian period \(T\) and semimajor axis \(a\), then compare \(T^2/a^3\). It should be approximately constant when the central mass is unchanged, and \(T^2\mu/a^3\) should equal \(4\pi^2\approx39.48\) whatever the central body (Beat 3). The calculation itself still requires you to record values from several runs and compare them.

11 · Verify Kepler's Second Law — Advanced Programming

Use the returned \(x,y,t\) arrays to check that equal areas are swept in equal times. Approximate the area swept during each step by the triangle formed by the origin and the two successive radius vectors, and divide by the length of the step, because the steps are not all equal. The rates should be nearly equal, and equal to \(h/2\), Eq. (8). Insert your analysis in main.py immediately after run_orbit() returns; the arrays are available there as result.xs, result.ys and result.ts. For the default orbit the rate should be close to 1.3565×1015 m2 s−1 and stay within a few parts in 105 of it. One way to start: 

import numpy as np
areas = 0.5 * (result.xs[:-1] * result.ys[1:] - result.xs[1:] * result.ys[:-1])
rate = areas / np.diff(result.ts)          # area swept per second
print(rate.min(), rate.max(), 0.5 * result.Hs[0])

12 · Explore the Velocity Hodograph — Advanced

python main.py --output velocity
python main.py --vyInit 85000 --maxSteps 600 --output velocity

For a non-radial Kepler orbit the velocity vectors lie on a circle: a bound ellipse traces the full circle, whereas parabolic and hyperbolic trajectories trace only part of it. Compare several eccentricities and then an unbound orbit. From Eq. (10), the hyperbolic case has a circle of radius 33942 m s−1 whose centre is 51059 m s−1 from the origin, so the origin lies outside the circle, as an eccentricity of 1.5043 requires.

13 · Point-Mass Radial Infall — Advanced

python main.py --vxInit -1000 --vyInit 0 --maxSteps 20000
python main.py --vxInit -1000 --vyInit 0 --maxSteps 20000 --eps1 0.001

Give the particle zero or nearly zero angular momentum and an inward velocity. Observe what happens as the trajectory approaches the mathematical point-mass singularity. The program contains a numerical guard to prevent evaluation at \(r=0\); the guard radius is not the physical surface of a star or planet (Beat 8). The first command stops at the singularity with an energy drift of 1.8426e+05. The second, with --eps1 0.001, uses all 20000 steps before it gets there, and its drift is only 9.9444e-05 up to that point. Try increasing --maxSteps for it, and compare how the energy drift behaves as the particle approaches the centre.

14 · Try a Different Central Force Law — Advanced Programming Extension

As an optional programming exercise, make a copy of the program and replace the Newtonian inverse-square acceleration with other central-force laws. A particularly instructive comparison is a Hooke-law acceleration proportional to \(-\mathbf r\), the other force for which Bertrand's theorem gives closed orbits (Beat 4). You can also try small non-Newtonian corrections and look for orbital precession. Historically, modified Newtonian force laws were proposed as explanations for Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession, while modern alternatives such as MOND have been proposed as alternatives to dark matter in some astrophysical settings. After experimenting with the code, research these topics and investigate why modified force laws have not replaced general relativity as the accepted explanation of Mercury's perihelion precession. Keep this as a separate experimental copy so the standard Orbit program continues to represent Newtonian gravity.

15 · Compare Error Measures — Advanced Programming Extension

Make a separate experimental copy and replace the Euclidean-norm comparisons in _relative_vector_change() and _relative_increment_change() with sums of absolute component differences. Run the same eccentric orbit with each version, then compare accepted-step counts, period error, conservation drift and closure residuals. Rotate the initial position and velocity together and repeat: which error measure is unchanged by a rotation of the coordinate axes, and does the difference matter at the tolerances you tested? Beats 5 and 6 describe what the two tests do.

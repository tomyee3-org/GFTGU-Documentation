Description

Orbit computes the trajectory of a test particle—planet, satellite, or comet—moving in the gravitational field of a fixed central mass under Newton's inverse-square law. The central mass stays at the origin; the particle's position and velocity are advanced with an adaptive predictor–corrector integrator.

The supplied defaults approximate Mercury at perihelion. All initial conditions, integration controls, and the display can be given on the command line. Five display modes show the x-y orbit, the velocity hodograph, position or velocity against time, or the specific kinetic, potential, and total energy. The console reports numerical diagnostics and the planar Keplerian elements of the starting state, with real-valued results shown to five significant digits.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: one force law fixes the whole orbit before any calculation is made, exact motion under it conserves energy and angular momentum, and the printed drift in those two quantities is how you tell how far to trust a step-by-step calculation of that motion.

How to use this session

Run each command from the GFTGU-Programs/Orbit folder with Python 3.10 or later, NumPy and Matplotlib. Read the named summary lines, inspect the figure, and answer the numbered activities before going on. Close each plot window to return to the terminal. All nine beats use the same four Python modules and the same command-line interface.

The fixed central body sits at the origin. Its gravitational parameter is \(\mu=GM\), supplied by --k in m3/s2. The particle's mass cancels out. The default state starts at 4.6×1010 m on the x-axis with a +y velocity of 58,980 m/s, a Mercury-like teaching case rather than an ephemeris.

Our thread is simple: the inverse-square acceleration determines the orbit; an exact orbit conserves specific energy and angular momentum; the numerical solution's drift and closure tell us how well this integration follows that exact motion.

Beat 0 · A planet in a fixed field

A test particle starts on the positive x-axis 4.6×1010 m from a fixed central mass with the Sun's gravitational parameter. It moves at 58 980 m s−1 in the +y direction, which is roughly Mercury at perihelion. Nothing acts on it but the gravity of the central mass. Ask the program what happens.

Run

python main.py --xInit 46000000000 --vxInit 0 --vyInit 58980 --k 1.3271244e+20 --dt0 10000 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001 --maxSteps 20000 --maxOrbits 1

Trace the oval from its right-hand starting point. In the summary find periapsis radius, apoapsis radius, elapsed simulated time and Keplerian period. The default ends after 761 accepted steps and 1.0000 azimuthal revolution. A stop at one revolution means the direction from the centre has turned through \(2\pi\), not that the position is mathematically exact.

1. Predict. If the particle's distance from the central body doubled, by what factor would the magnitude of its acceleration change?

The force law used by the code is \(\mathbf a=-\mu\,\mathbf r/r^3\). That is an adopted law of this model. The integrator advances x, y, \(v_x\), and \(v_y\), with \(d\mathbf r/dt=\mathbf v\).

2. Record. Compare 7.6025×106 s of simulated time with the 7.6019×106 s Keplerian period derived from the initial state. What could account for the difference within this model?

Beat 1 · Energy decides bound or unbound

The particle can be given any starting speed at the same place. Below a certain speed it stays bound to the central mass, and above it the particle leaves for ever. The dividing speed follows from one number, the energy per unit mass. Give the particle three different speeds and read what the program says about each.

For comparison, the default settings are --vyInit 58980, --maxSteps 20000.

python main.py --vyInit 70000 --maxSteps 20000
python main.py --vyInit 75961 --maxSteps 600
python main.py --vyInit 85000 --maxSteps 600

Compare specific energy, conic classification, eccentricity and termination. The first is elliptic; the last is hyperbolic and stops at the accepted-step limit before completing a revolution. Near escape, 75961 m/s is still slightly bound but its initial energy is so close to zero that a large fractional energy drift is a poor diagnostic on its own.

3. Estimate. Calculate \(\sqrt{2\mu/r_0}\) with the printed initial radius and \(\mu\). Is 75961 m/s below or above it?

Only after comparing the runs, use the specific-energy definition: \(\mathcal E=v^2/2-\mu/r\). Its sign sets elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic ideal motion, subject to the program's small near-zero classification tolerance. The printed elements describe the initial state, while the last accepted state can drift.

4. Check. For the 85000 m/s run, find the printed final radius and final speed. Why is closure radius residual reported n/a?

Beat 2 · Speed sets size, direction sets shape

Two particles at the same place with the same speed, aimed differently, have the same energy. What can differ between them? Keep the speed of the default run and turn its direction through 45 degrees.

For comparison, the default settings are --vxInit 0, --vyInit 58980.

python main.py --vxInit 41705.15 --vyInit 41705.15

Compare this with Beat 0. The speed is approximately the same, so the printed initial specific energy, semimajor axis and Keplerian period agree at displayed precision; eccentricity changes from 0.20575 to about 0.72192. The change is more than a rotation of the original ellipse because its specific angular momentum changed.

5. Compare. Record the two periapsis and apoapsis radii. Which start has the more elongated orbit? Does the numerical closure residual also change?

The relevant conserved quantity in the exact problem is \(h=xv_y-yv_x\). For a bound ellipse \(a=-\mu/(2\mathcal E)\), \(p=h^2/\mu\), and \(e=\sqrt{1+2\mathcal E h^2/\mu^2}\). Same energy fixes \(a\), while a different \(h\) changes \(e\).

6. Explain. Use \(y_0=0\) to show why reducing \(v_y\) while adding \(v_x\) reduces \(h\), even though the total speed stays fixed.

Beat 4 · Position and velocity both repeat

An exact bound, nonradial Kepler orbit around a fixed centre closes on itself: after one revolution the particle is back at its starting position with its starting velocity, and the next revolution retraces the first. The program counts revolutions by the angle the particle has turned through, and then compares the end state of the run with the start state.

It is surprisingly complex to decide when the orbit has gone around once. Since the initial position and velocity can be freely chosen by the user, we must cope with orbits that go clockwise or counter-clockwise, and with orbits that start on either axis or within any quadrant. Orbit monitors the angular position at successive accepted steps and accumulates the angle through which the particle has turned. One revolution means a total angular change of \(2\pi\) in either direction; maxOrbits can also request a fraction of a revolution or several revolutions. Comparing the current angle with the starting angle alone would not distinguish these cases.

Computing an angle requires care because angles are not uniquely defined. The angles \(a\), \(a+2\pi\) and \(a-2\pi\) describe the same direction. Python's math.atan2(y, x) returns the signed polar angle of the point \((x,y)\), measured from the positive x-axis, with counter-clockwise angles positive. It uses both coordinates to distinguish the quadrants, unlike taking an arctangent of \(y/x\), which also fails on the y-axis. Be careful about the argument order: the y-coordinate comes first! The returned angle lies between \(-\pi\) and \(+\pi\), so it jumps at the negative x-axis even though the physical motion is continuous.

At the beginning, the program initializes the previous angle and the accumulated angular change:

angle_previous = math.atan2(y, x)
accumulated_angle = 0.0

For each converged trial endpoint, it computes angle_new = math.atan2(y1, x1) and subtracts angle_previous. This is a difference from the last accepted position, not always from the initial position. The raw difference needs wrapping when a step crosses the angle discontinuity. For example, counter-clockwise motion from \(+179^\circ\) to \(-179^\circ\) produces a raw difference of \(-358^\circ\). Adding \(360^\circ\), or \(2\pi\) radians, gives the actual change of \(+2^\circ\). Clockwise motion across the same boundary, from \(-179^\circ\) to \(+179^\circ\), gives \(+358^\circ\); subtracting \(360^\circ\) gives \(-2^\circ\).

The program's _unwrap_delta function therefore adds \(2\pi\) while the difference is at most \(-\pi\), and subtracts \(2\pi\) while it is greater than \(+\pi\). The resulting signed increment lies in \((-\pi,\pi]\). This interpretation requires steps short enough to distinguish the direction of travel; the angular-step safeguard in Beat 6 shortens trials whose estimated angular travel exceeds a quarter-turn.

Each accepted signed increment is added to accumulated_angle, and angle_previous is replaced by that endpoint's angle_new. Counter-clockwise increments are positive and clockwise increments are negative. The number of revolutions completed is \(|\texttt{accumulated\_angle}|/(2\pi)\), so the same stopping rule works in both directions and from any starting quadrant. The target is \(2\pi\,\texttt{maxOrbits}\). When a trial reaches or crosses that target in magnitude, the program refines the final timestep, reintegrating as needed, until its angular change reaches the target within the endpoint tolerance. It then accepts the endpoint and stops. Other limits, such as maxSteps or the central singularity guard, can stop the calculation first.

The accumulated count starts at zero, so the first small step is not mistaken for a completed revolution. There is no need for a separate half-orbit flag or a test that looks for a sign change after returning to the starting angle. In the standard central-force model, the sign of the specific angular momentum \(h=xv_y-yv_x\) identifies the initial direction: positive for counter-clockwise motion, negative for clockwise motion. The stopping code obtains that direction directly from the signed angular increments. A radial trajectory has \(h=0\) and does not complete a revolution.

Run the default orbit for one, two and one-and-a-half revolutions, and look at the velocity-space plot of the first.

python main.py --output velocity --maxOrbits 1 --vyInit 58980
python main.py --maxOrbits 2 --output orbit --vyInit 58980
python main.py --maxOrbits 1.5 --output orbit --vyInit 58980
python main.py --vyInit -58980 --output velocity --maxOrbits 1

The velocity plot is a hodograph: its horizontal and vertical axes are \((v_x,v_y)\), and its near-circle is not the path in space. The red marker shows the initial velocity and the cross marks zero velocity. Reversing the tangential velocity reverses the direction of travel and flips the hodograph's offset.

9. Measure. Compare the default one-revolution closure radius residual, 5.2282e-09, with the two-revolution value, 2.0868e-08. Where do numerical errors show up when an exact Kepler orbit would retrace itself?

The program accumulates wrapped changes of the position angle. For whole-number revolution targets it reports normalized end-to-start radius and velocity differences. For 1.5 revolutions both closure lines are n/a, since that endpoint is not meant to coincide with the start. The hodograph circle's radius is \(\mu/|h|\) for a nonradial inverse-square orbit.

Beat 5 · The corrector

Newton's laws of orbital motion have an exact solution for a single fixed centre: the conic sections of Beat 2, from which the printed elements come.

Orbit deliberately integrates them step by step instead, so that you can see a method that also works for forces that have no analytic solution, and the elements then serve as an outside check. In EarthOrbit, the integration algorithm evaluates the acceleration only at the beginning of each step. It advances the velocity with a forward Euler update, then advances the position using the average of the beginning and end velocities. For the x-component, these updates are:

\[v_{x,n+1}=v_{x,n}+a_{x,n}\Delta t,\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac{v_{x,n}+v_{x,n+1}}{2}\Delta t.\]

Averaging the velocities improves the position update, but the forward Euler velocity update still makes the coupled EarthOrbit calculation first order for a position-dependent acceleration.

We could rely on smaller and smaller time-steps to get better accuracy, but there are other ways to improve the accuracy of a given time-step, and they are often more efficient, in that they give an accurate result in a shorter time. One such technique is the predictor-corrector method. It is a powerful concept, which can be used in a wide variety of situations. We introduce it here because the problem of planetary motion allows us to illustrate the method in a simple way.

The predictor-corrector method improves on the method described above by using the acceleration at the end of a time-step so that we can accurately calculate the change in velocity and, from that, the change in position. However, the acceleration can't be known until we know what the change in the position is, since the acceleration depends on position. This is a kind of chicken-and-egg problem, and the predictor-corrector method is perfect for solving it. The idea is to find a series of successively more accurate approximations to the final position. The method performs an iteration, which is the mathematical word to describe doing the same thing over and over again.

Start with the orbital equations written as a first-order system. Here \(\mathbf r=(x,y)\), \(\mathbf v=(v_x,v_y)\), and \(\mathbf a_n=\mathbf a(\mathbf r_n)\). Write the timestep as \(\Delta t\), so it is not confused with the specific angular momentum \(h\) used in the other beats:

\[\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf v,\qquad \dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf a(\mathbf r).\]

The constant-acceleration predictor initializes both position and velocity:

\[\mathbf r_{n+1}^{(0)}=\mathbf r_n+\mathbf v_n\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf a_n(\Delta t)^2,\qquad \mathbf v_{n+1}^{(0)}=\mathbf v_n+\mathbf a_n\Delta t.\]

Evaluate the acceleration at the predicted position. Each corrector iteration then computes:

\[\mathbf v_{n+1}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf v_n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\left[\mathbf a_n+\mathbf a\!\left(\mathbf r_{n+1}^{(k)}\right)\right],\]

\[\mathbf r_{n+1}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf r_n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\left[\mathbf v_n+\mathbf v_{n+1}^{(k+1)}\right].\]

The superscripts are not powers, but iteration numbers. In numerical analysis, subscripts usually indicate the time step, while superscripts in parentheses indicate the correction iteration within that time step.

The option eps2 is a convergence criterion that decides when to stop repeating. Define the velocity increment by \(\Delta\mathbf v^{(k)}=\mathbf v_{n+1}^{(k)}-\mathbf v_n\). After each correction, the code compares the change in this increment with the magnitude of the initial predicted increment:

\[\frac{\left\|\Delta\mathbf v^{(k+1)}-\Delta\mathbf v^{(k)}\right\|}{\left\|\mathbf a_n\Delta t\right\|}\leq\mathrm{eps2}.\]

If the test passes, the corrected position and velocity become the accepted endpoint, provided the remaining step safeguards also pass. Otherwise, the code evaluates the acceleration at the corrected position and repeats; failure to converge within the pass limit causes a shorter-step retry. Although the individual steps are much improved over the method used in EarthOrbit, this is still globally a second-order method when the corrector is sufficiently converged.

Rather than using fixed timesteps, as the EarthOrbit integrator does, Orbit adapts its timesteps using a scheme designed for this calculation. The code rejects or shortens steps based on:

  1. the relative difference between the starting and predicted endpoint accelerations;
  2. failure of the corrector iteration to meet eps2 within the pass limit;
  3. proximity to the central singularity;
  4. excessive estimated angular travel;
  5. non-finite trial states;
  6. final-event endpoint refinement.

Run the default orbit with a very strict eps2 and then with one so loose that only one corrector pass is needed.

For comparison, the default settings are --dt0 10000, --eps1 0.05, --eps2 0.0001.

python main.py --eps2 1e-8 --eps1 0.05 --dt0 10000
python main.py --eps2 10 --eps1 0.05 --dt0 10000
python main.py --dt0 1e6 --eps1 10 --eps2 1e-12

Compare with Beat 0. Tight and loose corrector settings both use 761 accepted steps in the first two runs. Their acceleration-evaluation counts are 3062 and 1534, versus 2301 at default tolerance. The loose run has a worse closure radius residual, although its reported maximum energy drift is slightly smaller; a single diagnostic is not a general measure of accuracy.

11. Compare. Record accepted steps, acceleration evaluations, energy drift and closure for all three commands. Why is the strict long-step example a different test from merely tightening eps2 at default dt0?

A predictor estimates the end of a step; an iterated corrector evaluates the acceleration at its estimated endpoint and updates the velocity. eps2 controls convergence of the velocity increment, not the orbit's error. If it fails to converge within the pass limit, that trial step is rejected and retried shorter.

Beat 6 · The step control

The program never takes a step longer than dt0, and it takes a shorter one when the acceleration changes too much during a step for the predictor to be trusted. The option eps1 sets how much is too much. With the default dt0 of 104 s this never happens: the 761 steps of Beat 0 are 760 steps of exactly 104 s and one last, shortened step that lands the run on the requested revolution (Beat 4, about 2.5×103 s). To watch the controller work, make dt0 ten times longer, then loosen eps1 so far that it cannot object, and then make dt0 a hundred times longer while it still cannot object.

For comparison, the default settings are --dt0 10000 --eps1 0.05.

python main.py --dt0 1e5 --eps1 0.05
python main.py --dt0 1e5 --eps1 10
python main.py --dt0 1e6 --eps1 10

The default 10,000 s maximum step is conservative for this initial orbit. Raising it to 100,000 s with the usual eps1 gives 181 accepted steps; loosening eps1 to 10 gives 77. The third run has only 14 accepted steps and appreciably worse energy drift. dt0 is a ceiling, while eps1 controls trial-step rejection when acceleration changes too much.

12. Inspect. Compare the printed shortest accepted step, longest accepted step and angular-step rejections. Which run is controlled mainly by dt0, and which is repeatedly shortened?

An additional angular safeguard rejects a trial step when its estimated angular travel exceeds a quarter revolution, or \(\pi/2\). A coarse trial segment that intersects the singularity guard is rejected and retried with a shorter step; it is not itself a physical singularity report.

Beat 7 · How far to trust it

Every number printed so far came from an approximation, and Beats 5 and 6 showed settings that change it. The way to decide when a result is good enough is to change the numerical settings and see whether the numbers you care about change. Compare the default dt0=10000 with 5000 s and 1250 s; add a 2500 s run to complete three successive halvings.

For comparison, the default settings are --dt0 10000.

python main.py --dt0 5000 --output energy
python main.py --dt0 1250 --output energy

As the step ceiling shrinks, the energy drift and one-revolution closure normally improve; the finer run shows about 8.3504e-07 maximum fractional energy drift and 1.2675e-12 closure radius residual. Read the final specific energy separately from the Keplerian block's specific energy for the starting state. The energy plot shows specific kinetic, potential and total energy; the total should be nearly flat.

13. Quantify. Compare the default, 5000 s, and 1250 s runs. How does the energy-drift ratio change as the maximum step is halved? Do not infer an order from a single pair without checking the actual accepted steps.

Exact energy and angular momentum are conserved under the adopted force law. A small fractional drift is useful when the initial invariant is safely nonzero; near escape or radial infall, inspect the absolute drift and the scale of the initial quantity as well.

Beat 8 · The hard cases

Start the particle at the default distance but with a much smaller speed, 10 km s−1, so that it falls a long way in toward the central mass before swinging back. Run it with the default integration controls, then with a tighter eps1. Finally take the limit in which the particle has no sideways motion at all.

For comparison, the default settings are --vyInit 58980 --dt0 10000 --eps1 0.05 --vxInit 0 --maxSteps 20000.

python main.py --vyInit 10000 --dt0 10000 --eps1 0.05 --vxInit 0 --maxSteps 20000
python main.py --vyInit 10000 --eps1 0.005 --dt0 10000 --vxInit 0 --maxSteps 20000
python main.py --vxInit -1000 --vyInit 0 --maxSteps 20000 --dt0 10000 --eps1 0.05

A slower tangential start makes a very elongated ellipse with a near-centre passage. The first run's maximum fractional energy drift is about 0.011176; tightening eps1 in the second run reduces it considerably. With zero tangential velocity the last run approaches the point-mass singularity guard: the program stops there instead of claiming to simulate passage through \(r=0\).

14. Test. Compare accepted steps and the energy drift of the first two runs. Why can more steps be worthwhile near periapsis?
15. Diagnose. In the radial run, read termination, shortest accepted step and maximum absolute angular-momentum drift. Explain why fractional angular-momentum drift is unavailable when initial \(h=0\), and why this teaching model does not determine what a real particle does at the central body's surface.

3 · Introductory to Intermediate — Position and Velocity Both Repeat

python main.py --maxOrbits 1 --output velocity

A closed orbit must return not only to its starting position but also to its starting velocity. Run one complete bound orbit and select --output velocity. The velocity-space curve should close just as the position-space orbit does. The console closure residuals give a numerical check: after an integral number of revolutions, both the final radius and velocity should be close to their initial values. Check the numbers of Beat 4: the circle has a radius of 48916 m s−1 and its centre is 10064 m s−1 from the origin. Try --vyInit -58980 as well: the orbit runs clockwise and the centre moves to the other side of the origin.

4 · Intermediate — Energy Conservation

# Bound default
python main.py --output energy

# Hyperbolic comparison
python main.py --vyInit 85000 --maxSteps 600 --output energy

After reading the introduction to gravitational energy in Chapter 6, select --output energy for a bound orbit. The kinetic and potential specific energies change continually, but their sum should remain nearly constant. Then make an unbound orbit and compare its total specific energy with that of the bound orbit: bound Kepler orbits have negative specific energy, hyperbolic orbits positive, and the marginal parabolic case zero. From the specific-energy formula in Beat 1, the total is −1.1457×109 J kg−1 for the first command and +7.2745×108 J kg−1 for the second. The console's drift lines show the remaining numerical error and give a quantitative check on conservation. Use the absolute drift for the parabolic case, where a fractional change from zero is undefined (Beat 1).

5 · Intermediate — Bound, Parabolic, and Hyperbolic Motion

python main.py --vyInit 70000
python main.py --vyInit 75961 --maxSteps 600
python main.py --vyInit 75961.2143594 --maxSteps 600
python main.py --vyInit 85000 --maxSteps 600

At a fixed starting radius \(r\), vary the initial speed through the escape value \(v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2\mu/r}\) (Beat 1). Below it the orbit is bound, at it the orbit is parabolic, and above it the orbit is hyperbolic. For unbound cases, increase --maxSteps if necessary, because the requested --maxOrbits may never be reached. Compare the trajectory class with the sign of the initial specific energy. A decimal value typed for \(v_{\rm esc}\) is rounded to floating-point precision, so its computed energy may be slightly positive or negative rather than exactly zero. The program therefore calls a start parabolic when its energy is within one part in 1012 of zero on the energy scale of the problem, which the third command satisfies and 75961 does not (Beat 1). Try speeds just below and above the threshold, and inspect the absolute drift, comparing it with the size of the initial specific energy, since the fractional drift is unreliable there and a trajectory whose drift exceeds that energy cannot be trusted (Beat 1).

6 · Intermediate — Direction Independence of Binding

# These two velocities have approximately the same speed (rounded components)
python main.py --vxInit 0 --vyInit 58980
python main.py --vxInit 41705.15 --vyInit 41705.15

Keep \(v_x^2+v_y^2\) fixed while rotating the initial velocity vector at a fixed starting position. The orbit's shape and angular momentum change, but its bound or unbound classification, its semimajor axis and its period do not, because the specific energy depends on the speed and not on the direction of the velocity. Check numbers from Beat 2: the eccentricity moves from 0.20575 to 0.72192 and the periapsis from 4.6000e+10 m to 1.6105e+10 m, while the semimajor axis stays at 5.7916e+10 m. Try other angles at the same speed. What is the largest eccentricity you can reach?

8 · Intermediate — Repeat Several Revolutions

Set --maxOrbits 2 or 5 and examine whether successive numerical ellipses lie on top of one another. 9 · Intermediate to Advanced — Numerical Convergence

# Baseline
python main.py

# Smaller maximum step and tighter tolerances
python main.py --dt0 5000 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-6

Reduce --dt0 and tighten --eps1 and --eps2. Compare the orbital period, the energy drift, the angular-momentum drift and the closure residuals rather than judging accuracy from the orbit plot alone. Change one numerical control at a time so that you can tell which effect it has. In the second command above, the extra tightening changes nothing: the run is identical to --dt0 5000 alone, 1521 steps and a drift of 1.3361e-05, because at that step size neither the eps1 test nor the eps2 test is the limit. You can also make either tolerance deliberately large to see what happens when that accuracy control is effectively relaxed (Beats 5 and 6).

10 · Advanced — Verify Kepler's Third Law

# Repeat with the Table 4.3 perihelion position and speed for other planets.

Run several bound planetary orbits with --maxOrbits 1. For each, use the integrated elapsed simulated time for one revolution as \(T\), and the printed semimajor axis \(a\), then compare \(T^2/a^3\). 11 · Advanced Programming — Verify Kepler's Second Law

Use the returned \(x,y,t\) arrays to check that equal areas are swept in equal times. Approximate the area swept during each step by the triangle formed by the origin and the two successive radius vectors, and divide by the length of the step, because the steps are not all equal. The rates should be nearly equal, and equal to \(h/2\) for the signed area rate (Beat 3); the unsigned swept-area rate is \(|h|/2\). Insert your analysis in main.py immediately after run_orbit() returns; the arrays are available there as result.xs, result.ys and result.ts. For the default orbit the rate should be close to 1.3565×1015 m2 s−1 and stay within a few parts in 105 of it. One way to start: 

import numpy as np
areas = 0.5 * (result.xs[:-1] * result.ys[1:] - result.xs[1:] * result.ys[:-1])
rate = areas / np.diff(result.ts)          # area swept per second
print(rate.min(), rate.max(), 0.5 * result.Hs[0])

For a non-radial Kepler orbit the velocity vectors lie on a circle: a bound ellipse traces the full circle, whereas parabolic and hyperbolic trajectories trace only part of it. Compare several eccentricities and then an unbound orbit. 13 · Advanced — Point-Mass Radial Infall

python main.py --vxInit -1000 --vyInit 0 --maxSteps 20000
python main.py --vxInit -1000 --vyInit 0 --maxSteps 20000 --eps1 0.001

Give the particle zero or nearly zero angular momentum and an inward velocity. Observe what happens as the trajectory approaches the mathematical point-mass singularity. The program contains a numerical guard to prevent evaluation at \(r=0\); the guard radius is not the physical surface of a star or planet (Beat 8). The first command stops at the singularity with an energy drift of 1.8426e+05. The second, with --eps1 0.001, uses all 20000 steps before it gets there, and its drift is only 9.9444e-05 up to that point. Try increasing --maxSteps for it, and compare how the energy drift behaves as the particle approaches the centre.

14 · Advanced Programming Extension — Try a Different Central Force Law

As an optional programming exercise, make a copy of the program and replace the Newtonian inverse-square acceleration with other central-force laws. A particularly instructive comparison is a Hooke-law acceleration proportional to \(-\mathbf r\), the other force for which Bertrand's theorem gives closed orbits (Beat 4). You can also try small non-Newtonian corrections and look for orbital precession. Historically, modified Newtonian force laws were proposed as explanations for Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession, while modern alternatives such as MOND have been proposed as alternatives to dark matter in some astrophysical settings. After experimenting with the code, research these topics and investigate why modified force laws have not replaced general relativity as the accepted explanation of Mercury's perihelion precession. Keep this as a separate experimental copy so the standard Orbit program continues to represent Newtonian gravity.

15 · Advanced Programming Extension — Compare Error Measures

Make a separate experimental copy and replace the Euclidean-norm comparisons in _relative_vector_change() and _relative_increment_change() with sums of absolute component differences. Run the same eccentric orbit with each version, then compare accepted-step counts, period error, conservation drift and closure residuals. Rotate the initial position and velocity together and repeat: which error measure is unchanged by a rotation of the coordinate axes, and does the difference matter at the tolerances you tested? Beats 5 and 6 describe what the two tests do.

Scope and checks

Orbit computes planar Newtonian motion about a fixed point mass. It omits relativity, drag, finite sizes, tides and a moving primary. An orbit near the numerical singularity guard is a failure of this model's assumptions, not a planetary collision calculation. The exact Keplerian elements derived from the starting state and the energy/angular-momentum drift give complementary checks; none alone certifies an entire computed trajectory.

The companion Sample Outputs Guide has one representative image for each Beat. The detailed tutorial supplies derivations and longer investigations.

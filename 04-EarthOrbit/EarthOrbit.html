Description

EarthOrbit is a numerical realisation of Newton's Cannon — the thought experiment Isaac Newton used to illustrate orbital motion in the Principia (1687). A projectile is launched horizontally from a tower high above Earth's surface. In the physical inverse-square version of the thought experiment, low launch speeds produce impact arcs, the circular speed gives a circular orbit, speeds between circular and escape give ellipses, and speeds at or above escape produce open trajectories. The default EarthOrbit calculation instead follows the near-surface approximation used for this exercise: gravity has constant magnitude \(g\) while its direction continually points toward Earth's centre.

The program integrates the projectile's two-dimensional trajectory using a fixed-timestep, averaged-velocity algorithm, plotting the result against a reference circle showing Earth's surface. An optional "inverse_square" extension is provided for realistic higher-altitude, Keplerian, and escape experiments. The default model is best used within a few kilometres of Earth's surface. All the usual launch parameters are set in main.py.

Physical Background

Newton's Cannon Thought Experiment

Newton imagined a cannon mounted on a mountain so high it clears the atmosphere, firing horizontally with increasing powder charges. For the physical inverse-square law, the sequence of outcomes illustrates a continuous family of trajectories:

  • Sub-orbital arc: below the circular speed the trajectory curves more steeply than Earth's surface and the projectile impacts the ground.
  • Circular orbit: at the circular speed \(v_c\) the curvature of the trajectory exactly matches Earth's curvature. The projectile never lands.
  • Elliptical orbit: between \(v_c\) and the escape speed \(v_e\) the orbit is a closed ellipse with the launch point as one of the apsides.
  • Escape trajectory: for inverse-square gravity, at or above the escape speed \(v_e = \sqrt{2}\,v_c\), the projectile leaves Earth and does not return.
Constant magnitude central force versus inverse-square: The default force model used by this program holds the magnitude of the acceleration fixed at \(g = 9.8\) m s−2 while continually redirecting it toward Earth's centre. Near the surface this is a useful pedagogical approximation, but it is not the inverse-square law and becomes increasingly unrealistic as the orbit ranges far from Earth's surface.

A broader result from classical mechanics, Bertrand's theorem, states that the inverse-square and isotropic harmonic-oscillator central forces are the only such force laws for which all bounded orbits are closed. Under constant-magnitude central force, non-circular bounded trajectories generally precess and form rosette patterns rather than Keplerian ellipses. The circular orbit remains a special closed case.

Use "inverse_square" for realistic higher-altitude, Kepler-law, and escape-orbit experiments.

The Inverse-Square Force Law

At any distance \(r\) from Earth's centre the gravitational acceleration has magnitude

Gravitational acceleration (inverse-square law) — Eq. 1
\[ |\mathbf{a}| \;=\; \frac{\mu_\oplus}{r^2} \]

where \(g = 9.8\) m s−2 and \(R_\oplus = 6\,378\,200\) m is a reference radius close to Earth's equatorial radius, not the modern mean radius. The textbook values imply \(gR_\oplus^2 \approx 3.9868\times10^{14}\) m³ s−2. The optional inverse-square extension instead uses \(\mu_\oplus = 3.98600435507\times10^{14}\) m³ s−2, the modern Earth gravitational parameter from JPL's DE440 astrodynamic parameters. The acceleration direction is always toward the centre.

Circular and Escape Speeds for the Inverse-Square Law

At orbital radius \(r = R_\oplus + h\):

Circular speed and escape speed — Eq. 2
\[ v_c \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\mu_\oplus}{r}}, \qquad v_e \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2\mu_\oplus}{r}} \;=\; \sqrt{2}\,v_c \]
Altitude h Orbital radius r (m) vc (m s−1) ve (m s−1)

Governing Equations

Equations of Motion

The state vector is \((x,\,y,\,u,\,v)\), where \((x, y)\) is the projectile position in the equatorial plane (with the origin at Earth's centre) and \((u, v)\) is the velocity. Newton's second law gives four coupled ODEs:

Equations of motion — Eq. 3
\[ \dot{x} = u, \quad \dot{y} = v, \qquad \dot{u} = a_x, \quad \dot{v} = a_y, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \]

where \((a_x, a_y)\) depend on the chosen force_law (see Physical Background):

"simplified" (default)
\[ a_x = -\frac{g\,x}{r}, \qquad a_y = -\frac{g\,y}{r} \qquad (|\mathbf{a}| = g \text{ always}) \]
"inverse_square"
\[ a_x = -\frac{\mu_\oplus x}{r^3}, \qquad a_y = -\frac{\mu_\oplus y}{r^3} \]

Initial Conditions

By convention the projectile starts on the positive \(y\)-axis (the "top" of the orbit):

Initial conditions — Eq. 4
\[ x_0 = 0, \quad y_0 = R_\oplus + h_0, \quad u_0 = u_\text{init}, \quad v_0 = v_\text{init} \]

Because the launch point sits on the \(y\)-axis, \(u\) (the \(x\)-velocity) is the tangential component and \(v\) (the \(y\)-velocity) is the radial component at launch. A purely horizontal (tangential) launch is achieved by setting \(v_\text{init} = 0\) and \(u_\text{init} \neq 0\) — this is what main.py uses, and places the launch point at an apsis (periapsis or apoapsis, depending on whether \(u_\text{init}\) is below or above \(v_c\)). Setting \(v_\text{init} \neq 0\) adds a radial velocity component and rotates the orbit's orientation.

Integration Algorithm

The Averaged-Velocity Fixed-Step Method

In the integration algorithm employed by this program, the acceleration is evaluated at the beginning of each interval, the velocity is advanced using that one acceleration value, and the position is then advanced using the average of the old and new velocities:

Integration step — Eq. 5
\[ u_1 = u_0 + a_x(x_0,y_0)\,\Delta t, \quad v_1 = v_0 + a_y(x_0,y_0)\,\Delta t \] \[ x \;\leftarrow\; x_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(u_0 + u_1)\,\Delta t, \quad y \;\leftarrow\; y_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v_1)\,\Delta t \]

This simple algorithm has limitations. For constant acceleration it gives the exact kinematic position update; for position-dependent gravity it accumulates numerical error. Quantitative orbital tests should therefore be repeated with smaller dt to check convergence.

Termination Conditions

After each step the driver evaluates \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) and stops when either:

  • \(r < R_\oplus\) — the projectile has hit Earth's surface, or
  • the step counter reaches maxSteps — the budget is exhausted.

The trajectory arrays are trimmed to the number of stored points actually produced before being returned. If the projectile hits Earth, the final plotted point may lie slightly below the surface circle because the driver stops after detecting \(r < R_\oplus\); it does not interpolate the exact surface-crossing point between timesteps.

Choosing dt and maxSteps: At the default dt = 0.4 s and launch speed ≈ 7 900 m s−1, one near-surface revolution takes roughly 5 070 s, requiring about 12 673 integration updates. maxSteps counts stored trajectory points, including the initial point, so the loop can perform at most maxSteps - 1 updates. The default maxSteps = 15 000 therefore covers slightly more than one revolution if the projectile does not hit Earth first. For longer-period orbits increase maxSteps; for better numerical accuracy decrease dt and increase maxSteps so the desired simulated duration is retained.

Module Overview

Physics Layer
physics_earthorbit.py

Defines Earth's physical constants and two selectable gravitational force laws via the force_law argument to compute_acceleration(): the constant-magnitude "simplified" approximation (the default) and the physically accurate "inverse_square" law. The textbook model retains rounded values for \(g\) and \(R_\oplus\), while the inverse-square extension uses the modern JPL DE440 value of \(\mu_\oplus=GM_\oplus\). K_APPROX remains available for comparing the textbook-derived value \(gR_\oplus^2\) with the modern parameter.

G_SURFACE = 9.8 R_EARTH = 6_378_200.0 # m; reference radius MU_EARTH = 3.986_004_355_07e14 # m³/s²; JPL DE440 Earth GM K_APPROX = G_SURFACE * R_EARTH**2 # textbook-derived comparison ForceLaw = Literal["simplified", "inverse_square"] def compute_acceleration(x, y, force_law="simplified") -> (ax, ay) # "simplified": ax = -G_SURFACE * x / r, ay = -G_SURFACE * y / r # "inverse_square": ax = -MU_EARTH * x / r³, ay = -MU_EARTH * y / r³
Driver Layer
driver_earthorbit.py

Implements the fixed-step averaged-velocity integration loop. Imports compute_acceleration, R_EARTH, and ForceLaw from the physics module, and forwards force_law to compute_acceleration() at every step. Builds the 401-point Earth-surface reference circle and advances the projectile until surface impact or maxSteps is reached. With return_diagnostics=True, it also returns time and velocity arrays for quantitative experiments.

from physics_earthorbit import compute_acceleration, R_EARTH, ForceLaw def run_earth_orbit( h0 = 300.0, uInit = 7900.0, # initial x/tangential velocity (m/s) vInit = 0.0, # initial y/radial velocity (m/s) dt = 0.4, maxSteps = 15000, force_law: ForceLaw = "simplified", return_diagnostics = False )
Plot Layer
plot_earthorbit.py

Renders the trajectory and Earth's surface circle on a single equal-aspect matplotlib figure. Contains no physics or integration logic. Imports NumPy for input validation and matplotlib for rendering.

figure, axes = plot_earth_orbit(xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth) # Blue dashed: Earth surface (xEarth, yEarth) # Red solid: Projectile trajectory (xs, ys) # Equal aspect, grid, legend # Title: "EarthOrbit — Attempting to Achieve Orbit"
Entry Point
main.py

The only file most users need to edit. Set the launch parameters and force_law here, then run python main.py. The plot appears immediately. uInit is the x/tangential (horizontal-at-launch) component, and vInit is the y/radial (vertical-at-launch) component.

from driver_earthorbit import run_earth_orbit from plot_earthorbit import plot_earth_orbit force_law = "simplified" xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth = run_earth_orbit( h0=300.0, uInit=7900.0, # tangential -- not quite enough to complete an orbit at 300 m vInit=0.0, dt=0.4, maxSteps=15000, force_law=force_law, ) plot_earth_orbit(xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth)

Parameters

All ordinary launch parameters are set as arguments to run_earth_orbit() in main.py. The defaults reproduce the Newton's Cannon setup: a tangential launch at 7 900 m s−1 from 300 m above the reference Earth radius, using the constant-magnitude-\(g\) force law.

Parameter Default Units Description
h0 300.0 m Initial altitude above Earth's surface. The starting position is \((0,\;R_\oplus + h_0)\). The default is 300 metres, not kilometres — just above the height of the Eiffel Tower, representing Newton's idealised mountain-top. For a realistic low-Earth orbit use h0 = 300_000.0.
uInit 7900.0 m s−1 Initial x-velocity (tangential/horizontal at launch, since the launch point sits on the \(y\)-axis). At 300 m altitude the simplified-law circular speed is about 7 906.3 m s−1, while the modern inverse-square value is about 7 905.1 m s−1. The default setup uses 7 900 m s−1, which is slightly sub-orbital. Try different values to see their effects. Because the numerical method is approximate, a trajectory that looks circular on the whole-Earth plot should not be interpreted as a high-precision orbit.
vInit 0.0 m s−1 Initial y-velocity (radial/vertical at launch). Zero gives a purely tangential launch and places the start point at an apsis when the tangential speed is not exactly circular. A non-zero value adds a radial component and changes the orbit's orientation and eccentricity.
dt 0.4 s Fixed integration timestep. Smaller values generally improve numerical accuracy; excessively large timesteps can distort the trajectory. Halving dt while approximately doubling maxSteps preserves the simulated duration while increasing temporal resolution.
maxSteps 15000 — Maximum number of stored trajectory points, including the initial position. The loop can therefore perform at most maxSteps - 1 integration updates. At dt = 0.4 s the default covers about 6 000 s of simulated time — just over one orbital period at 300 m altitude (\(T \approx 5\,070\) s). Increase for longer simulations or higher orbits with longer periods.
return_diagnostics False — Optional analysis output. When True, the driver returns ts, us, and vs after the usual four arrays. This is useful for period and energy experiments and leaves the normal plotting interface unchanged when False.
force_law "simplified" — "simplified" (default) runs the simulation using the constant-magnitude approximation; "inverse_square" switches to the inverse-square force law. Several Suggested Experiments below (Keplerian ellipses, escape, and Kepler's third law) require "inverse_square" explicitly. Under the default constant-magnitude force, non-circular bounded trajectories generally precess rather than tracing a fixed Kepler ellipse.
Altitude units — common mistake: h0 is in metres. Setting h0 = 300 gives an altitude of 300 m (just above the surface), not 300 km. To simulate a realistic low-Earth orbit at the altitude of the International Space Station, use h0 = 408_000 (408 km = 408 000 m) with uInit ≈ 7\,664 m s−1 (tangential) when using force_law="inverse_square".
Accepted parameter values: h0 must be non-negative, dt must be positive, maxSteps must be an integer of at least 2, and the altitude and velocity values must be finite numbers. return_diagnostics must be True or False. An unrecognised force_law also produces a clear ValueError rather than a misleading plot.

Output

The program produces a single matplotlib figure with two overlaid curves on shared equal-aspect axes:

  • Blue dashed circle — Earth's surface, drawn as 401 evenly spaced points on a circle of radius \(R_\oplus\). This is pre-computed by the driver and passed to the plotter as xEarth, yEarth.
  • Red solid line — the projectile trajectory \((x_j, y_j)\) for all accepted steps, from the launch point until surface impact or step budget exhaustion.

The axis labels are "x (meters)" and "y (meters)". The aspect ratio is forced equal so that the Earth appears as a true circle and orbital ellipses are not distorted. A legend and grid are included; the title reads EarthOrbit — Attempting to Achieve Orbit. The plotter returns its matplotlib Figure and Axes objects after displaying the figure, which is convenient for further customisation.

By default run_earth_orbit() returns the same four arrays used by the plotter. For quantitative work, call it with return_diagnostics=True; the function then additionally returns ts, us, and vs, aligned with the position arrays. These make it straightforward to estimate orbital periods, plot speed, or examine numerical energy error.

Before the plot opens, main.py also prints the model version, executable-code build identifier, and number of trajectory samples to the console. Use python main.py --version to print only the version and build.

Suggested Experiments

Suggested order: The experiments below are arranged from introductory parameter exploration to more demanding numerical-analysis work. Experiments 1–3 require only edits to the values passed from main.py; later experiments progressively ask for additional calculations or small code changes.
EXP-1 · INTRODUCTORY

Find the Circular Speed

Begin with the default near-surface setup: h0=300.0, vInit=0.0, and force_law="simplified". Start at 7 900 m s−1 and increase the horizontal launch speed toward 7 906 m s−1. Find the value for which the trajectory visually follows Earth's surface for a full revolution. Then compare your result with the theoretical circular-speed condition \(v_c=\sqrt{gr}\).

Repeat with force_law="inverse_square". At only 300 m altitude the two theoretical circular speeds differ by about 1.1 m s−1, so the same scan works for both models. Reduce dt if you want to distinguish numerical error from the physical difference between the two force laws.

h0=300.0, vInit=0.0 uInit=7906.0 # scan upward from 7900 force_law="simplified"
EXP-2 · INTRODUCTORY

How Sensitive Is the Orbit to Launch Speed?

Keep the 300 m starting altitude and vary only uInit through values such as 7 800, 7 850, 7 900, and the circular value found in Experiment 1. Compare how far each projectile travels before striking Earth. This is the central qualitative lesson of Newton's cannon: a change of only a few metres per second near orbital speed can make a large difference to the trajectory.

Very low speeds such as 5 000 m s−1 strike the surface so quickly that the trajectory is nearly indistinguishable from a dot on a whole-Earth plot.

h0=300.0, vInit=0.0 uInit=7800, 7850, 7900, 7906 force_law="simplified"
EXP-3 · INTRODUCTORY–INTERMEDIATE

Compare the Two Force Laws

First repeat a near-surface orbit with both force_law="simplified" and force_law="inverse_square". The trajectories should be very similar because the altitude is tiny compared with Earth's radius. Then raise the starting altitude to 300 km and use the appropriate circular speed for each law. Compare the resulting orbital speeds and periods.

This experiment shows directly where the constant-magnitude-\(g\) approximation is useful and where it ceases to represent terrestrial gravity well. At 300 km the two circular speeds already differ by several percent.

# Near surface: h0=300.0 # Higher altitude: h0=300_000.0 # Run each case with both force laws.
EXP-4 · INTERMEDIATE · SCHUTZ SUGGESTION

Interpolate the Impact Point

For a sub-orbital launch, the integration stops after the first stored point has moved inside Earth. The actual impact therefore occurred between the final two samples. Use the last two radii, \(r_{n-1}\ge R_\oplus\) and \(r_n<R_\oplus\), to estimate where the trajectory crossed Earth's surface rather than treating the final inside-Earth point as the impact location.

A simple first attempt is linear interpolation in the fraction \(f=(r_{n-1}-R_\oplus)/(r_{n-1}-r_n)\), followed by interpolation of both \(x\) and \(y\) between the two samples. Compare the interpolated point with the raw final point, and repeat with smaller dt. As an optional programming exercise, modify the driver so that the interpolated surface crossing replaces the below-surface endpoint.

import numpy as np from driver_earthorbit import run_earth_orbit from physics_earthorbit import R_EARTH xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth = run_earth_orbit(uInit=7800.0) r = np.hypot(xs, ys) f = (r[-2] - R_EARTH) / (r[-2] - r[-1]) x_hit = xs[-2] + f * (xs[-1] - xs[-2]) y_hit = ys[-2] + f * (ys[-1] - ys[-2])
EXP-5 · INTERMEDIATE

Radial Launch Component

Use the inverse-square extension at a higher starting altitude so an eccentric orbit has room to develop without immediately intersecting Earth. Keep the total speed close to the local circular speed but give the projectile a radial component. Compare the resulting orbit with the purely tangential circular case.

The orbit is no longer circular even though the total launch speed is almost unchanged. Requires force_law="inverse_square". This gives the intended Keplerian interpretation.

h0=1_000_000.0 uInit=7307.0, vInit=800.0 # |v|≈7351 m/s ≈ local v_c dt=1.0, maxSteps=10000 force_law="inverse_square"
EXP-6 · INTERMEDIATE

Elliptical Orbit at LEO Altitude

Start at 300 km altitude with no radial velocity and set the tangential speed above the local circular value. The launch point is then the perigee of an ellipse with a higher apogee. Try several launch speeds and observe how the eccentricity changes. Increase maxSteps as needed to capture a complete orbit.

Requires force_law="inverse_square". Under the default constant-magnitude force, non-circular bounded trajectories generally precess into rosettes instead of remaining fixed Keplerian ellipses.

h0=300_000.0, vInit=0.0 uInit=9000.0, maxSteps=50000 force_law="inverse_square"
EXP-7 · INTERMEDIATE

Geostationary-Altitude Orbit

Use the inverse-square law at an altitude of about 35 786 km with a circular speed near 3 075 m s−1. Estimate the period from the trajectory and compare it with one sidereal day (about 23 h 56 min). A timestep of 10 s is small compared with the orbital period while keeping the number of integration steps manageable.

This is a useful real-world check on the inverse-square extension. The simulation is two-dimensional and does not model Earth's rotation, so it reproduces the orbital period associated with geostationary altitude rather than geographic station-keeping itself. Enable return_diagnostics=True and use the returned time array to measure the period.

Requires force_law="inverse_square".

h0=35_786_000.0, vInit=0.0 uInit=3075.0 dt=10.0, maxSteps=10000 force_law="inverse_square" return_diagnostics=True
EXP-8 · INTERMEDIATE–ADVANCED

Approach the Escape Speed

At 300 km altitude the inverse-square escape speed is about 10 926 m s−1. Start somewhat below this value and increase the launch speed. Watch a bound ellipse become more elongated as the speed approaches escape. At or above the escape speed the trajectory does not return, so maxSteps determines how much of the open path is calculated.

Requires force_law="inverse_square". The default constant-magnitude central force has a potential that increases without bound with distance, so it has no finite escape speed.

h0=300_000.0, vInit=0.0 uInit=10900.0, maxSteps=100000 force_law="inverse_square"
EXP-9 · ADVANCED

Verify Kepler's Third Law

Run nearly circular inverse-square orbits at several altitudes with return_diagnostics=True. In this mode, run_earth_orbit() returns seven arrays: xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth, ts, us, and vs. For this experiment you need only xs, ys, and ts.

Compute the angular position with np.unwrap(np.arctan2(ys, xs)). Estimate the period T when the accumulated angular change first reaches 2π; interpolation between the surrounding samples gives a better estimate. Compare several orbital radii and confirm that \(T^2/r^3\) is approximately constant. Repeat with smaller dt to check numerical convergence. As a contrast, circular orbits under the constant-magnitude force obey \(T^2\propto r\), not Kepler's third law.

Runnable starter code: The block below generates one circular-orbit dataset and computes its accumulated angular travel. Your task is to locate and interpolate the \(2\pi\) crossing, calculate the period and \(T^2/r^3\), and then repeat the calculation at additional orbital radii.

import numpy as np from driver_earthorbit import run_earth_orbit from physics_earthorbit import MU_EARTH, R_EARTH h0 = 300_000.0 uInit = np.sqrt(MU_EARTH / (R_EARTH + h0)) result = run_earth_orbit( h0=h0, uInit=uInit, dt=1.0, maxSteps=7000, force_law="inverse_square", return_diagnostics=True) xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth, ts, us, vs = result theta = np.unwrap(np.arctan2(ys, xs)) angle_travelled = np.abs(theta - theta[0]) # Your task: find and interpolate the first 2*pi crossing.
EXP-10 · ADVANCED

Energy Conservation and Timestep Error

Use return_diagnostics=True so that the position and velocity histories are available. At each stored point calculate \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and the speed squared \(u^2+v^2\). For "inverse_square", calculate the specific mechanical energy \(\mathcal{E}=\tfrac12(u^2+v^2)-\mu_\oplus/r\); for "simplified", use \(\mathcal{E}=\tfrac12(u^2+v^2)+gr\).

Plot \(\mathcal{E}(t)-\mathcal{E}(0)\). The continuous equations conserve energy, but this fixed-timestep numerical algorithm does not conserve it exactly. Halve dt while increasing maxSteps enough to keep the same simulated duration, and verify that the energy error decreases. This experiment is the most direct quantitative test here of the limitations of the integration method itself.

Runnable starter code: This block produces the diagnostic arrays, radius, and speed squared for a concrete inverse-square run. Complete the exercise by calculating the matching energy array from the formula above and plotting its change from the initial value. Then repeat at finer timesteps.

import numpy as np from driver_earthorbit import run_earth_orbit from physics_earthorbit import MU_EARTH xs, ys, xEarth, yEarth, ts, us, vs = run_earth_orbit( h0=300_000.0, uInit=7725.7, dt=1.0, maxSteps=2001, force_law="inverse_square", return_diagnostics=True) r = np.hypot(xs, ys) speed_squared = us**2 + vs**2 # Your task: compute E = 0.5*speed_squared - MU_EARTH/r # and plot E - E[0] against ts.

Related Programs

  • CannonTrajectory — the simpler near-surface projectile calculation on which EarthOrbit builds. EarthOrbit adds the essential new feature that the direction of gravitational acceleration changes with position so that it always points toward Earth's centre; the underlying mathematics is developed in Investigations 4.1 and 4.2.
  • Orbit — the general planetary orbit program (Chapter 4). Uses a predictor-corrector scheme with adaptive timestep rather than EarthOrbit's simple fixed-step algorithm; supports multiple output modes including energy and velocity hodographs. EarthOrbit is the pedagogically simpler version focused specifically on Newton's Cannon.
  • RelativisticOrbit — explores strong-field orbital behaviour with an effective relativistic correction to the orbital acceleration (Chapter 21). It shares the predictor-corrector integration core used by Orbit.
  • Binary — extends the Orbit integrator to a two-body system with mutual gravitational interaction (Chapter 13). Both bodies move under Newton's law; the predictor-corrector loop handles the coupled equations simultaneously.
  • Atmosphere — companion program (Chapter 7) covering the related problem of vertical hydrostatic equilibrium in a planetary atmosphere, where the same constant-g vs. inverse-square distinction arises.

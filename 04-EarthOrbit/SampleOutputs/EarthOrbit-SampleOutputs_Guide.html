Representative Sample Outputs

Version 1.1.1  |  Build 77b768d3a136  |  Generated 5 September 2026

Purpose. This compact, self-contained guide presents four plots generated from the supplied current source. Together they show Newton’s cannon in the textbook constant-g model and the circular, elliptical, and unbound trajectories available through the inverse-square extension. No external image files are required.

In every figure, the dashed blue curve is the reference Earth surface and the red curve is the projectile trajectory. Coordinates are geocentric metres and both axes use the same scale. The launch point is at the top of Earth; uInit is initially horizontal and vInit is initially radial.

Figure 1 — Default near-surface shot

run_earth_orbit()
# h0=300 m, uInit=7900 m/s, vInit=0, dt=0.4 s
# maxSteps=15000, force_law="simplified"

The unchanged defaults use a constant 9.8 m/s² acceleration directed toward Earth’s centre. At 7.900 km/s the projectile travels a substantial arc, but its radius eventually crosses the model Earth surface.

What it shows

  • The red arc stays close to the surface because the launch speed is only about 6.3 m/s below the simplified model’s local circular speed.
  • The driver stops after storing the first point below the surface; it does not interpolate the exact surface crossing.

Flight time: 200.8 s  |  Angular travel: 14.25°  |  Stored points: 503  |  Final altitude: -0.99 m

Figure 2 — Circular low-Earth-orbit experiment

h0 = 300_000.0
u_circular = sqrt(MU_EARTH / (R_EARTH + h0))
run_earth_orbit(h0=h0, uInit=u_circular, vInit=0.0,
                dt=0.4, maxSteps=13_650,
                force_law="inverse_square")

Using the inverse-square force law and the analytical circular speed at 300 km altitude produces one nearly circular revolution.

What it shows

  • The calculated launch speed is 7.725724 km/s and the displayed run covers 1.003 revolutions in 90.99 minutes.
  • The fixed-step update is not symplectic. Over this run the computed altitude ranges from 300.0 to 319.4 km, making timestep error visible rather than concealing it.

Force law: inverse square  |  Launch speed: 7.725724 km/s  |  Elapsed time: 5459.6 s  |  Energy-span / |initial energy|: 0.291%

Figure 3 — Elliptical inverse-square orbit

run_earth_orbit(h0=300_000.0, uInit=9_000.0, vInit=0.0,
                dt=0.4, maxSteps=26_400,
                force_law="inverse_square")

A horizontal launch at 9.000 km/s from 300 km altitude remains bound but moves well above low Earth orbit, producing a clear Keplerian ellipse with Earth near one focus.

What it shows

  • The initial point is the perigee. Two-body theory predicts an eccentricity of 0.357 and an apogee altitude of about 7,718 km.
  • The numerical run reaches 7,736.7 km and traces 1.001 revolutions, close to one complete ellipse.

Perigee altitude: 300 km  |  Maximum computed altitude: 7,736.7 km  |  Elapsed time: 10559.6 s  |  Stored points: 26,400

Figure 4 — Above-escape-speed trajectory

run_earth_orbit(h0=300_000.0, uInit=11_500.0, vInit=0.0,
                dt=0.4, maxSteps=15_001,
                force_law="inverse_square")

At 300 km altitude the inverse-square escape speed is 10.926 km/s. The 11.500 km/s launch is above that threshold, so the trajectory is open rather than an ellipse.

What it shows

  • The specific orbital energy begins positive (6.438 MJ/kg), the signature of an unbound two-body trajectory.
  • After 100 minutes the projectile is 33,649 km above the model surface and still moving at 5.728 km/s.

Launch / escape speed: 11.500 / 10.926 km/s  |  Final altitude: 33,649 km  |  Elapsed time: 6000 s

Notes for evaluation

  • Two models: simplified keeps the acceleration magnitude at 9.8 m/s²; inverse_square uses MU_EARTH = 3.98600435507×10¹⁴ m³/s² and is the appropriate choice away from the surface.
  • Integrator: acceleration is evaluated at the beginning of each fixed step, velocity is advanced, and position uses the average of the old and new velocities. The method is not velocity Verlet/leapfrog and is not generally symplectic.
  • Interface: experiment settings are arguments to run_earth_orbit (or edits to main.py); the command line exposes only --version.
  • Verification: all 66 supplied tests passed in a headless run before these figures were generated.

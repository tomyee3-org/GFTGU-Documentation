Description

MercPert follows a small body — called "Mercury" here, though it can stand for any planet, asteroid, or dust grain — as it moves under the gravity of two stars that orbit each other. The two massive bodies are a Sun-like primary and a companion whose mass you choose; by default the companion has 0.1 solar mass, about 100 Jupiter masses, which puts it just above the hydrogen-burning limit: a small red dwarf, not a planet. The two stars move on circles about their common centre of mass. Mercury feels both of them but is too light to pull on either, so this is the planar circular restricted three-body problem.

The question MercPert lets you ask is how much a companion disturbs a planet's orbit, and when the disturbance stops being a small correction and becomes the whole story. A light, distant companion barely changes a Kepler orbit. A heavy or nearby one makes the orbit precess, drives the planet into close encounters with either star, throws it out of the system, or lets it survive only far outside the binary. The phenomena explored here are the gravitational part of the "cleaning out" of a young planetary system's inner regions.

Try, while doing your own simulations, to determine what regions of the orbit plane are so disturbed by "Jupiter" that stable orbits are not possible. How far outside "Jupiter's" orbit is it safe for "Mercury" to orbit without being seriously disturbed? What happens if "Mercury" starts out on the opposite side of the "Sun" from "Jupiter"? Whenever you see a report in the scientific press of the discovery of another solar system around a distant star, with a Jupiter-like planet, come back to this program and experiment to see if there is room for other planets to orbit inside this Jupiter-like planet's orbit, allowing for the finite size of the central star.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: a companion's pull is a perturbation whose size grows with its mass and with how close it comes, and although Mercury's ordinary energy is not conserved, one combination of energy and angular momentum measured in the frame rotating with the binary — the Jacobi constant — is, which both limits where Mercury can go and tells you how far to trust the calculation. Beats 2 and 3 measure the perturbation, Beat 4 introduces the Jacobi constant, Beats 5 through 7 show what strong perturbations do, and Beat 8 uses the Jacobi constant to judge the numbers.

What this program is, and what it does not model. MercPert is a teaching model. It is planar and uses Newtonian point masses. The two stars are made to move on an exact circle, and Mercury has no effect on them. There is no gas, disk, migration, tides, relativity, radiation, or physics of impacts. The collision radii are stopping surfaces you choose, not models of stars' surfaces, and the program never decides by itself whether an orbit is "stable" or "ejected"; you have to choose a criterion and a time span. Its adaptive predictor-corrector integrator is second-order and very accurate on smooth orbits, but close encounters demand small steps and tight tolerances; Beat 8 says how to tell when they are needed.

The equations on this page are rendered by MathJax, loaded from a public CDN, so an internet connection is needed to see them typeset; with no connection, the raw \(\LaTeX\) source (e.g. \(a(t)\)) will show as plain text instead of a formula. Reading the output

Every run prints a summary at the console and then opens a plot.

The first console line gives version, build, accepted-step count and stopping reason. Next come the binary's circular period, Mercury's integration time, its minimum and maximum distance from the Sun, maximum absolute fractional Jacobi drift, and the smallest/largest accepted working timestep. A run at max_steps has that many accepted steps plus the initial state. A collision can stop sooner.

The orbit plot uses dashed red for Sun, dashed green for companion and solid blue for Mercury. A black × appears only for a configured collision. With --show_jacobi_diagnostic, the second plot shows signed \((C-C_0)/|C_0|\), whereas the console shows its maximum magnitude. If \(C_0=0\), the printed fractional drift is undefined. For a collision, the interpolated endpoint contributes to the printed drift. The Sample Outputs Guide provides representative figures and quoted values for Beats 0–8.

Start here

Put a small test particle near a Sun–companion binary, run a command, then compare the printed diagnostics with the blue trajectory. Follow Beats 0–9 in order on a first pass. Every command starts a fresh run from the MercPert program folder; close the plot window before the next one.

Mercury's input position and velocity are relative to the Sun, while the plotted positions use the system's centre of mass. The dashed red and green circles show the prescribed stellar paths; the solid blue curve is Mercury's calculated path. A crossing of coloured paths on the screen does not mean the bodies met at the same time.

Beat 0 · A test particle in a binary

Begin with the default: a 1-solar-mass Sun, a 0.1-solar-mass companion 0.7 astronomical units (AU) away, and Mercury 0.3 AU from the Sun with a 59,220 m/s sideways launch.

python main.py --m_sun_solar 1 --m_planet_solar 0.1 --binary_separation_au 0.7 --x_init_au 0.3 ^
  --vy_init 59220 --dt 2000 --max_steps 10000 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
Read these lines

The stellar period is 203.96 days. The run covers 231.48 days and 10,000 accepted steps; Mercury ranges from 0.30000 to 0.40762 AU from the Sun. Find the compact red circle, wider green circle, and blue loops. The stars move on set circles; Mercury responds to both of them.

The program solves Newton's equation for a massless third body: \(\ddot{\mathbf r}=-GM_\odot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_\odot)/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_\odot|^3-GM_c(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_c)/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_c|^3\). Do not call the blue loops closed or stable from this finite run alone.

Beat 1 · The binary is prescribed

Make the companion half as massive as the Sun. Watch what moves in the plot.

For comparison, the default settings are --m_planet_solar 0.1, --vy_init 59220.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.5 --vy_init 54400 --m_sun_solar 1
Check the geometry

The printed binary period is 174.66 days. The Sun's red circle has radius 0.23333 AU about the centre of mass; the companion's green one has radius 0.46667 AU. Their 0.7 AU separation remains fixed. Read both radii from the axes, not from Mercury's printed Sun-distance range (0.14420–0.31804 AU).

The stellar angular speed is \(\omega=\sqrt{G(M_\odot+M_c)/d^3}\). At \(t=0\) the Sun moves in the negative \(y\) direction. The 54,400 m/s input is relative to that moving Sun, so it is not Mercury's barycentric speed.

Beat 2 · Switch the companion off

The companion mass must remain positive. Make it negligible and try a circular-speed launch, then the default faster launch.

For comparison, the default settings are --m_planet_solar 0.1, --vy_init 59220.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 1e-12 --vy_init 54379.16
python main.py --m_planet_solar 1e-12 --vy_init 59220
Compare

The first Sun-distance range prints 0.30000–0.30000 AU. The second prints 0.30000–0.43707 AU. The default companion in Beat 0 kept the same 59,220 m/s launch within 0.40762 AU over the same run.

For one Sun, \(v_c=\sqrt{GM_\odot/r}\). Starting at perihelion \(r_p\) with tangential speed \(v>v_c\), energy and angular momentum give \(r_a/r_p=[2-(v/v_c)^2]^{-1}(v/v_c)^2\), provided the launch remains bound. At \(r_p=0.3\) AU, this predicts 0.43707 AU for the second run.

Try Task 3 in Practice Tasks after this beat.

Beat 3 · Turn the companion back up

Keep the near-circular speed and multiply companion mass by ten on each run.

For comparison, the default settings are --m_planet_solar 0.1.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.001 --vy_init 54379.16
python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.01 --vy_init 54379.16
python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.1 --vy_init 54379.16
Subtract each range

The Sun-distance spreads are 0.00049, 0.00491 and 0.05020 AU. For these settings they grow by about a factor of ten each time the companion mass does. The relation is empirical over these three finite runs, not a promise for every mass or encounter.

The companion's direct acceleration relative to the Sun's pull scales as \((M_c/M_\odot)(r/\Delta)^2\), where \(\Delta\) is Mercury's distance from the companion. That ratio changes throughout the orbit; close passages can break the simple proportional pattern.

Beat 4 · The one number that is conserved

Ask for the second plot: signed fractional change of the Jacobi constant in the rotating binary frame.

python main.py --show_jacobi_diagnostic --m_planet_solar 0.1 --vy_init 59220
Read and look

The console gives a maximum absolute fractional Jacobi drift of 1.2305e-06. The second plot shows its sign, ranging about −1.23e-06 to +6.6e-08. Close the orbit window if it obscures the diagnostic window.

Ordinary inertial energy of Mercury can change because the moving stars exchange energy with it. In a frame turning with the binary, the stellar field is stationary and \(C=2(\mathbf r\times\mathbf v)_z\omega-v^2+2GM_\odot/r_\odot+2GM_c/r_c\) is conserved by the ideal equations. A small computed drift diagnoses numerical error; it does not prove the entire path is accurate.

Beat 5 · Close encounters and the step controller

Run longer, then tighten the acceleration-change and corrector thresholds.

python main.py --max_steps 40000 --eps1 0.05 --eps2 0.0001
python main.py --max_steps 40000 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-5
Compare the runs

The first reaches 0.016704 AU from the Sun over 919.20 days; its accepted working timesteps range from 199.63 to 2000.0 s and its Jacobi drift reaches 0.0051623. The tighter run needs steps down to 39.907 s and reports drift 0.00022738, but covers only 852.41 days. The runs have unequal durations, so their drift maxima alone do not measure the benefit of the tighter tolerance. The blue path is much less regular than in Beat 0.

The controller rejects a proposed step when acceleration changes too much and iterates a predictor–corrector until its change is below eps2. dt is the maximum working step, not a guarantee that each accepted step is that long. Drift this large calls for convergence checks before quoting a close-encounter trajectory.

Beat 6 · Collision and escape

Make the companion as massive as the Sun for a collision, then try a launch that heads far outward.

For comparison, the default settings are --m_planet_solar 0.1, --x_init_au 0.3, --max_steps 10000.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 1 --x_init_au 0.3 --max_steps 10000
python main.py --x_init_au 0.45 --max_steps 30000 --m_planet_solar 0.1
Read the stopping reason

The first stops at collision with Sun after 1920 steps and 41.543 days, at one nominal solar radius (0.0046505 AU); the black × marks that boundary crossing. The second reaches 11.531 AU from the Sun within 694.44 days, but its stopping reason is max_steps reached, not “escaped.”

A point mass has no physical surface in these equations. The positive Sun collision radius is a numerical stop; the companion's default collision radius is zero (disabled). A long outward track in a finite run is evidence of outward motion over that interval. The program does not certify permanent escape, and a positive instantaneous energy near moving stars would not by itself do so.

Try Task 7 in Practice Tasks after this beat.

Finite dimension masses experience tidal effects, not modeled by this program, which can have major astrophysical consequences. Try Task 9 in Practice Tasks after this beat.

Beat 7 · Outside the binary

Place two equal Suns 1 AU apart and launch 2.5 AU from the Sun. Try a seemingly plausible relative speed, then include the Sun's motion.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 1 --binary_separation_au 1 --x_init_au 2.5 --vy_init 30000 --max_steps 40000
python main.py --m_planet_solar 1 --binary_separation_au 1 --x_init_au 2.5 --vy_init 50846 --max_steps 40000
Compare the blue paths

Mercury starts 2.0 AU from the barycentre. About 29,785 m/s would circle a point mass of two solar masses there. But the Sun moves initially at about −21,061 m/s in \(y\); the first relative speed makes only about 8,939 m/s barycentric, so Mercury falls into the binary. With 50,846 m/s relative to the Sun, it circles outside. Its Sun-distance range is 1.5463–2.6554 AU.

For a nearby binary, a point-mass circular estimate is only a starting guess. The pair's changing geometry still perturbs the outside orbit.

Beat 8 · How far to trust a run

Halve the maximum step on a smooth orbit; keep the simulated duration at 20 million seconds by changing max_steps.

For comparison, the default settings are --dt 2000, --max_steps 10000.

python main.py --dt 2000 --max_steps 10000
python main.py --dt 1000 --max_steps 20000
python main.py --dt 500 --max_steps 40000
Record drift

The three maximum fractional Jacobi drifts are 1.2305e-06, 3.0312e-07 and 7.5216e-08. The ratio is about four when dt halves, consistent with second-order convergence on this smooth run.

Now halve dt for the Sun-grazing run of Beat 5:

python main.py --max_steps 80000 --dt 1000

Its drift is 0.0051786, much like 0.0051623 at the larger cap. Close to the Sun, eps1 rather than the cap already controls the working step. Beat 5's tighter-eps1 run reported a smaller drift but covered less time. Match physical duration and inspect actual accepted steps when judging convergence.

Beat 9 · Where the model and the run stop

Try three invalid settings and one singular starting position.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 0
python main.py --eps2 0.1
python main.py --binary_separation_au 0.001
python main.py --x_init 0

The first three are rejected by the argument parser: masses must be positive, eps2 must be smaller than eps1, and the two collision radii must fit within the binary separation. The fourth passes parsing but starts exactly at the Sun's point-mass centre, where gravity is singular; it reports a MercPert error: message with no plot. An ordinary max_steps reached is not an error.

MercPert is planar, prescribes a circular binary, and neglects Mercury's mass, atmosphere, disks, tides, relativity and impact evolution. A finite-radius collision is interpolated along an accepted segment. Printed five-significant-digit numbers still need a timestep study before they can be trusted quantitatively.

Practice Tasks

Task 1
From Beat 0, compare the 231.48-day run with the binary's 203.96-day period. State what the blue loops do and what a single run cannot establish.
Task 2
Use Beat 1's mass ratio and 0.7 AU separation to calculate the two stellar orbit radii, then check them against the axes.
Task 3
Predict the isolated-Sun circular speed at 0.3 AU and the aphelion of the 59,220 m/s launch before running both Beat 2 commands.
Task 4
Extend Beat 3's companion-mass sequence to 0.3 and 0.5 solar masses. Record when the nearly proportional spread no longer holds.
Task 5
Explain why Beat 4's console drift is nonnegative while the plotted drift takes both signs.
Task 6
Repeat Beat 5 with a tighter eps1. Record both the smallest working step and the Jacobi drift; compare the actual simulated durations.
Task 7
For Beat 6's outward run, propose a finite observation interval and distance threshold for an operational “ejected during this run” label. Explain why that would not certify permanent escape.
Task 8
For Beat 7, add the Sun's initial barycentric y velocity to each Sun-relative input velocity and predict which launch can circle outside the binary.
Task 9
The Roche limit. Set the two masses equal to 10 and 1 and set the separation to about 0.3 AU. This represents a typical binary containing a black hole (10 solar masses) and an ordinary solar-mass star, with a moderate separation. Now experiment with orbits for the small body that might tell us about the way the black hole strips gas from the normal star. This gas starts out at the surface of the solar-mass star, which might be a good fraction of the distance between the hole and the star if the star has become a giant. The gas starts out at rest with respect to the binary system, which means it moves with the same initial angular velocity as the star and black hole. Compute this angular velocity and use it to set up initial data as follows. Take the initial x-position to be anywhere you like between the two bodies. Take the initial y-position to be zero. Take the initial x-velocity to be zero and the initial y-velocity to be the angular velocity times the x-position. By changing the initial position, find out what possible behaviors there are. For what range of starting positions does the small body fall toward the black hole? For what positions does it remain near the star? The dividing place is called the Roche limit: if a giant star in a binary expands to larger than this size, it will lose its outer layers, which will fall onto the black hole. Note that what happens to gas and to our small body when they fall toward the black hole will be very different: the small body will just follow a gravitational trajectory, but the gas will get hot, develop strong pressure gradients, and radiate away much of its energy. The gas will then be trapped into an accretion disk around the black hole. See the discussion of accretion disks in the textbook. But even though the subsequent motion of gas and small particles will be different, the Roche limit will be the same, because at the outer edge of a star the pressure is zero so the gas particles behave like freely orbiting bodies.

Example Gallery

These examples use the Sun-relative initial-condition convention described above. The dashed red and green curves show the prescribed reference orbits of the two massive bodies, while Mercury's calculated trajectory is solid blue. Unless a collision is explicitly marked, an intersection of two plotted curves does not imply that the corresponding bodies occupied that point at the same time.

Perturbed bound orbit. With Mercury starting 0.3 AU from the Sun at 56 km/s, the companion drives conspicuous apsidal precession. The orbit remains bounded, but it no longer closes as a simple Kepler ellipse.
Large regular rosette. A faster start at 1 AU produces an extended, orderly multi-lobed trajectory. Strong perturbation does not necessarily imply immediate instability or chaotic-looking motion.
Collision with the Sun. After several close passages, Mercury reaches the nominal solar radius. The black × marks the first detected crossing of the Sun's moving finite-radius boundary, where the integration terminates.
Ejection. After several strongly perturbed revolutions, a gravitational encounter transfers Mercury onto a much larger outward trajectory. This provides a clear contrast with continued bound motion and collision.
Retrograde orbit: This uses the same orbital parameters as the preceding "Ejection" figure, but with Mercury in a retrograde orbit. This remains stable, because Mercury never spends enough time near Jupiter to be seriously disturbed.
Near the outer Lagrange point. With an Earth-sized companion at 1 AU, a test particle initialized near \(L_2\) approximately co-rotates with the companion over the plotted interval. \(L_2\) is an unstable equilibrium, so the figure should not be interpreted as evidence of permanent stability.

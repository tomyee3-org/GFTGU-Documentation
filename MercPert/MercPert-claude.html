Description

MercPert follows a small body — called "Mercury" here, though it can stand for any planet, asteroid, or dust grain — as it moves under the gravity of two stars that orbit each other. The two massive bodies are a Sun-like primary and a companion whose mass you choose; by default the companion has 0.1 solar mass, about 100 Jupiter masses, which puts it just above the hydrogen-burning limit: a small red dwarf, not a planet. The two stars move on circles about their common centre of mass. Mercury feels both of them but is too light to pull on either, so this is the planar circular restricted three-body problem.

The question MercPert lets you ask is how much a companion disturbs a planet's orbit, and when the disturbance stops being a small correction and becomes the whole story. A light, distant companion barely changes a Kepler orbit. A heavy or nearby one makes the orbit precess, drives the planet into close encounters with either star, throws it out of the system, or lets it survive only far outside the binary. The same test-particle calculation is the gravitational part of the "cleaning out" of a young planetary system's inner regions discussed in Chapter 13 of the 2003 edition; the program deliberately leaves out the gas of a protoplanetary disk.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: a companion's pull is a perturbation whose size grows with its mass and with how close it comes, and although Mercury's ordinary energy is not conserved, one combination of energy and angular momentum measured in the frame rotating with the binary — the Jacobi constant — is, which both limits where Mercury can go and tells you how far to trust the calculation. Beats 2 and 3 measure the perturbation, Beat 4 introduces the Jacobi constant, Beats 5 through 7 show what strong perturbations do, and Beat 8 uses the Jacobi constant to judge the numbers.

Textbook reference: Bernard Schutz, Gravity from the Ground Up, Cambridge University Press (2003), Chapter 13.
What this program is, and what it does not model. MercPert is a teaching model. It is planar and uses Newtonian point masses. The two stars are made to move on an exact circle, and Mercury has no effect on them. There is no gas, disk, migration, tides, relativity, radiation, or physics of impacts. The collision radii are stopping surfaces you choose, not models of stars' surfaces, and the program never decides by itself whether an orbit is "stable" or "ejected"; you have to choose a criterion and a time span. Its adaptive predictor-corrector integrator is second-order and very accurate on smooth orbits, but close encounters demand small steps and tight tolerances; Beat 8 says how to tell when they are needed.
Reading this page. The equations on this page are rendered by MathJax, loaded from a public CDN, so an internet connection is needed to see them typeset; with no connection, the raw \(\LaTeX\) source (e.g. \(a(t)\)) will show as plain text instead of a formula. How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equations at work, not independent evidence that they are right. The outside checks this page uses are Kepler's laws (Beats 1 and 2), which the runs can be compared with by hand, and the Jacobi constant (Beats 4 and 8), which the exact equations conserve and a correct calculation must very nearly conserve too.

Every beat uses the same program. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Beat 0 runs the default system, Beat 1 looks at the two stars, Beats 2 and 3 switch the companion off and turn it back up, Beat 4 introduces the Jacobi constant, Beat 5 follows a close encounter and the adaptive step control, Beat 6 shows a collision and an ejection, Beat 7 moves Mercury outside the binary, Beat 8 asks how far a run can be trusted, and Beat 9 collects the limits of the model and of a run.

Run every command from the program's folder. Each run prints a six-line summary and then opens an orbit plot; close the window to end the program. The plot is drawn in the barycentric frame, centred on the two stars' centre of mass: the dashed red curve is the Sun's circle, the dashed green curve is the companion's, and the solid blue curve is Mercury. Distances are printed in AU (and metres), times in days (and seconds), speeds are in metres per second, and printed numbers have five significant figures.

Mercury's starting values are measured from the Sun. --x_init, --y_init, --vx_init and --vy_init give Mercury's position and velocity relative to the Sun, while the calculation and the plot use the barycentric frame. At the start the Sun is on the negative \(x\)-axis and moving in the \(-y\) direction, so a Mercury placed on the \(+x\) side of the Sun with positive vy_init is launched in the same, counter-clockwise, sense as the binary. The program does the conversion for you (Beat 7 shows when it matters).
What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag saying what kind of statement it is. A green ODE tag marks the differential equation the program integrates: Eq. 1, Newton's law of gravity applied to Mercury. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows from equations already stated, such as Kepler's third law for the binary or the Jacobi constant. A grey PRESCRIPTION tag marks a rule the program adopts rather than derives; the clearest example is Eq. 3, the stars' circular motion, which is imposed rather than integrated. The predictor, corrector and collision-crossing formulas (Eqs. 7 to 9) are numerical method, not physics, and are not tagged. Ten equations are numbered.

Beat 0 · A test particle in a binary

Put a Sun and a 0.1-solar-mass companion 0.7 AU apart, and start Mercury 0.3 AU from the Sun, on the side facing the companion, moving sideways at 59,220 m/s. Run it.

python main.py

Look at the six printed lines and at the orbit plot.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the numbers: the run took its full 10,000 steps (termination: max_steps reached), covering 231.48 days, while the two stars go round each other once every 203.96 days, so the run is 1.13 binary periods long. Mercury's distance from the Sun stayed between 0.30000 and 0.40762 AU. Then compare the launch speed with the speed of a circular orbit at 0.3 AU around the Sun alone, \(v_c=\sqrt{GM_\odot/r}\) (Beat 2 derives this): 54,379 m/s. The launch is 8.90% faster than that, so the Sun alone would put Mercury at the perihelion of an ellipse, and 0.3 AU is the closest it gets. Write down how far out you expect such an ellipse to reach before reading Beat 2, which works it out; then compare with the 0.40762 AU the run actually reached. Then look at the plot: a small dashed red circle for the Sun, a large dashed green circle for the companion, and Mercury's blue path looping around the Sun while the whole pattern turns. The loops do not close on themselves.

Then the equation behind the run. Let \(\mathbf r=(x,y)\) be Mercury's position in the barycentric frame, \(\mathbf r_\odot(t)\) and \(\mathbf r_c(t)\) the positions of the Sun and the companion, and \(M_\odot\), \(M_c\) their masses. Newton's law of gravity from both bodies at once gives

Eq. 1 — Mercury's equation of motion ODE
\[ \ddot{\mathbf r}=-\frac{GM_\odot\,(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_\odot(t))}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_\odot(t)|^3} -\frac{GM_c\,(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_c(t))}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_c(t)|^3}. \]

The positions of the two stars depend on time, so Mercury's acceleration depends on time as well as on where Mercury is: this is the feature that makes the problem harder than a single-star orbit. mercury_acceleration(t, x, y, binary_params) in physics_mercpert.py evaluates Eq. 1. The program uses the IAU 2015 nominal value \(GM_\odot=1.3271244\times10^{20}\) m3 s−2 and gives both masses in solar-mass units.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 1 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 1 · The binary is prescribed

The two stars are not integrated at all. The program puts them on circles about their centre of mass and moves them at a fixed rate. Make the companion heavier, so the Sun's own circle is big enough to see.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.5 --vy_init 54400

Look at the Circular period line and at the two dashed circles on the plot.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the printed period, 174.66 days, and check it by hand from Kepler's third law for two bodies of total mass \(1.5\,M_\odot\) a distance 0.7 AU apart (Eq. 2): it agrees. Then work out the radii of the two circles about the centre of mass. The Sun is twice as heavy as the companion here, so the centre of mass lies one third of the way from the Sun to the companion: the Sun circles at \(0.7\times0.5/1.5=0.23333\) AU from it and the companion at 0.46667 AU. Read the dashed red and green circles off the plot's axes and check both. Then work out how fast the Sun is moving: \(r_\odot\omega=14{,}533\) m/s. Mercury's starting velocity of 54,400 m/s is measured from the Sun, so Mercury's speed in the frame of the plot is quite different, and its blue path, swinging between 0.14420 and 0.31804 AU from the Sun, makes loops around a Sun that is itself going round in a circle.

Then the equations. For two bodies of masses \(M_\odot\) and \(M_c\) a distance \(d\) apart moving on circles, Newton's laws give one common angular velocity and two radii:

Eq. 2 — Binary angular velocity and radii DERIVED
\[ \omega=\sqrt{\frac{G(M_\odot+M_c)}{d^{3}}},\qquad P=\frac{2\pi}{\omega},\qquad r_\odot=\frac{M_c}{M_\odot+M_c}\,d,\qquad r_c=\frac{M_\odot}{M_\odot+M_c}\,d. \]
Eq. 3 — Prescribed motion of the two stars PRESCRIPTION
\[ \mathbf r_\odot(t)=-r_\odot\,(\cos\omega t,\ \sin\omega t),\qquad \mathbf r_c(t)=+r_c\,(\cos\omega t,\ \sin\omega t). \]

Eq. 3 is tagged as a prescription because the program imposes it: the circle is an exact solution of the two-body problem, but it is only the right motion because Mercury is assumed to have no mass. A real planet pulls back on both stars a little; the restricted problem drops that pull. At \(t=0\) the Sun is on the negative \(x\)-axis and the companion on the positive \(x\)-axis, and the pair turns counter-clockwise. binary_positions() and binary_velocities() in physics_mercpert.py evaluate Eq. 3 and its time derivative. The option names --m_sun_solar and --m_planet_solar are historical; the second is simply the companion's mass.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 7 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 2 · Switch the companion off

Before measuring what the companion does, take it away and check that the program reproduces a plain Kepler orbit. The companion mass must be positive, so make it tiny instead of zero.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 1e-12 --vy_init 54379.16
python main.py --m_planet_solar 1e-12

Look at the Sun-distance range line of each run.

Three tasks, in order. First, the first run launches Mercury at exactly the circular speed 54,379.16 m/s, and the Sun-distance range prints as 0.30000 to 0.30000 AU: a circle, to the precision printed in AU (the metre values differ only in their fifth figure), over more than 230 days. Then take the second run, which keeps the default 59,220 m/s. Use Eq. 4 to predict how far out the resulting ellipse should reach, starting from \(v/v_c=1.0890\): 0.43707 AU. The run prints 0.30000 to 0.43707 AU, in agreement to five figures. Then go back to Beat 0, which was the same launch with the default companion: there Mercury never got beyond 0.40762 AU. The companion, not the numerical method, is what kept it 0.03 AU closer in.

Then the equations. For one star alone, a circular orbit of radius \(r\) needs gravity to supply exactly the centripetal acceleration, \(GM_\odot/r^2=v_c^2/r\). A launch at a perihelion \(r_p\) with speed \(v\) larger than \(v_c\) reaches out to the aphelion \(r_a\) given by energy and angular-momentum conservation:

Eq. 4 — Circular speed and the aphelion of an unperturbed orbit DERIVED
\[ v_c=\sqrt{\frac{GM_\odot}{r}},\qquad r_a=r_p\,\frac{(v/v_c)^2}{2-(v/v_c)^2}\quad\text{for a launch at perihelion, } v_c<v<\sqrt2\,v_c. \]

At \(r_p=0.3\) AU, \(v_c=54{,}379\) m/s, and with \(v/v_c=1.0890\) Eq. 4 gives \(r_a=0.43707\) AU. The Circular period line still prints a value, 213.92 days, because a companion of \(10^{-12}\) solar mass still goes round on its circle; it simply has no measurable effect.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 1 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 3 · Turn the companion back up

Now keep Mercury on the circular-speed launch of Beat 2 and bring the companion back in steps of a factor of ten in mass: roughly one Jupiter mass, ten, and then the default 0.1 solar mass.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.001 --vy_init 54379.16
python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.01 --vy_init 54379.16
python main.py --m_planet_solar 0.1 --vy_init 54379.16

Look at the Sun-distance range line of each run.

Three tasks, in order. First, for each run subtract the smallest Sun distance from the largest: 0.30014 − 0.29965 = 0.00049 AU, then 0.30140 − 0.29649 = 0.00491 AU, then 0.31291 − 0.26271 = 0.05020 AU. Then divide each spread by the one before it: 10.0 and 10.2. Ten times the companion mass gives, to within a few per cent, ten times the disturbance. That is the signature of a perturbation: a small extra force whose effect is proportional to its size. Then look at the last run's plot: even at 0.1 solar mass the orbit is still recognisably a slightly off-centre, slowly turning ellipse around the Sun, not yet the tangle of Beat 5.

Then the size of the extra force. At Mercury's distance \(r\) from the Sun and a distance \(\Delta\) from the companion, the companion's direct pull compared with the Sun's is

Eq. 5 — The companion's pull as a fraction of the Sun's DERIVED
\[ \frac{GM_c/\Delta^{2}}{GM_\odot/r^{2}}=\frac{M_c}{M_\odot}\left(\frac{r}{\Delta}\right)^{2}. \]

With the default system at the start, \(r=0.3\) AU and \(\Delta=0.4\) AU, this ratio is \(0.1\times(0.75)^2=0.056\): a few-per-cent force, proportional to \(M_c\), which is why the spreads above scale in step with the mass. Two things make the real effect smaller and more complicated than this one number. The Sun is pulled by the companion too, so what disturbs Mercury's orbit around the Sun is only the difference between the two pulls; and \(\Delta\) changes as both Mercury and the companion move. Both effects are in Eq. 1, which is why the program integrates it rather than using Eq. 5.

Beat 4 · The one number that is conserved

With a single star, Mercury's energy would stay fixed. With a moving companion, the gravity Mercury feels changes with time, so its energy does not stay fixed. Measure that, and then measure what does stay fixed.

python main.py --show_jacobi_diagnostic
python -c "import math, physics_mercpert as p, driver_mercpert as d; b=p.BinarySystemParams(1.0, 0.1, 0.7*p.AU); o=d.run_mercpert(b, p.MercuryInitialConditions(0.3*p.AU, 0.0, 0.0, 59220.0), d.MercPertRunParams(2000.0, 10000, 0.05, 1e-4, p.R_SUN)); E=[0.5*(u*u+v*v) - p.GM_SUN*(1.0/math.hypot(x-sx, y-sy) + 0.1/math.hypot(x-cx, y-cy)) for x, y, u, v, sx, sy, cx, cy in zip(o.merc_x, o.merc_y, o.merc_vx, o.merc_vy, o.sun_x, o.sun_y, o.planet_x, o.planet_y)]; print(f'{(max(E)-min(E))/abs(E[0]):.4f}')"

The first command is the default run with a second plot, of the Jacobi constant's fractional change against time. The second repeats the same default run inside Python and prints the largest change of Mercury's ordinary energy per unit mass, kinetic plus potential in the barycentric frame, as a fraction of its starting value.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the energy result: 0.3110. Mercury's energy wanders by 31% over the run, so it is not even approximately conserved. Then read the printed Maximum fractional Jacobi drift: 1.2305e-06, about one part in a million, and look at the second plot, where the drift wanders between about +0.1 and −1.2 × 10−6 without a steady trend. Then compare the two: the same run, the same calculated path, and one quantity changes 250,000 times more than the other. The Jacobi constant is the conserved one, and its tiny drift is entirely numerical error.

Then why. Mercury's energy is not conserved because the stars move, so the gravity it feels at a given point changes with time. But in a frame that turns with the binary at angular velocity \(\omega\), the two stars stand still, the gravitational field no longer changes with time, and there is again a conserved quantity. In the barycentric frame it reads

Eq. 6 — The Jacobi constant DERIVED
\[ C=\omega^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})+2\left(\frac{GM_\odot}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_\odot|} +\frac{GM_c}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_c|}\right)-|\mathbf v_{\rm rot}|^{2},\qquad \mathbf v_{\rm rot}=\mathbf v-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r=(v_x+\omega y,\ v_y-\omega x). \]

The first term comes from the centrifugal effect of the turning frame, the second from the two stars' gravity, and the third is Mercury's speed measured in the turning frame. The exact equations keep \(C\) fixed, so \(C\) can do two jobs. Physically, because \(|\mathbf v_{\rm rot}|^2\) cannot be negative, a given \(C\) forbids Mercury from some regions of space altogether (Experiment 8). Numerically, any change in the calculated \(C\) is error, which is how Beat 8 uses it. jacobi_constant() in physics_mercpert.py evaluates Eq. 6 at every stored step, and the printed drift is the largest \(|C-C_0|/|C_0|\) over the run.

Beat 5 · Close encounters and the step controller

Beat 0's run was short. Let the default system run four times longer and see what the companion does to Mercury over two and a half years. Then run it again with a stricter accuracy setting.

python main.py --max_steps 40000
python main.py --max_steps 40000 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-5

Look at the Sun-distance range and Adaptive controller working timestep range lines of each run.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the first run's closest approach to the Sun: 0.016704 AU, which is 3.6 solar radii from the Sun's centre. In Beat 0 Mercury never came closer than 0.3 AU; over 919.20 days the companion has pumped its orbit into a Sun-grazing one. The plot shows the change: the neat loops of Beat 0 have become a tangle. Then read the timestep range: 199.63 to 2000.0 s. The default run never needed anything shorter than its 2000 s maximum; this one needed a step ten times shorter near the Sun, which is also why 40,000 steps covered 919.20 days rather than the 925.93 days that 40,000 full steps would. Then compare the second run, which makes eps1 five times smaller and eps2 tighter: its smallest step is 39.907 s, and 199.63 / 39.907 = 5.00. The shortest step the controller allows has shrunk in direct proportion to eps1. The second run also meets the Sun at almost the same distance, 0.016877 AU, and its Jacobi drift is 23 times smaller; Beat 8 returns to that.

Then the rules the controller follows. Each step starts with a cheap Euler guess at the end of the step, and checks how much the acceleration would change over it:

Eq. 7 — Euler predictor and acceleration-change gate (numerical method, untagged)
\[ \mathbf r_{\rm pred}=\mathbf r_n+\mathbf v_n\,\Delta t,\qquad \mathbf v_{\rm pred}=\mathbf v_n+\mathbf a_n\,\Delta t,\qquad \frac{|\mathbf a_{\rm pred}-\mathbf a_n|}{\max(|\mathbf a_{\rm pred}|,|\mathbf a_n|)}\le\varepsilon_1, \]

where \(\mathbf a_{\rm pred}\) is Eq. 1 evaluated at \(\mathbf r_{\rm pred}\) and at the later time \(t_n+\Delta t\), with the stars moved on. If the gate fails, \(\Delta t\) is halved and the step tried again. A step that passes the gate is then refined by averaging the start and end of the step, repeatedly, until the end-of-step velocity stops changing:

Eq. 8 — Iterated trapezoidal corrector (numerical method, untagged)
\[ \mathbf v_{n+1}=\mathbf v_n+\tfrac12(\mathbf a_n+\mathbf a_{n+1})\,\Delta t,\qquad \mathbf r_{n+1}=\mathbf r_n+\tfrac12(\mathbf v_n+\mathbf v_{n+1})\,\Delta t, \] \[ \text{repeat until }\ \frac{|\mathbf v_{n+1}^{\rm new}-\mathbf v_{n+1}^{\rm old}|} {\max(|\mathbf v_{n+1}^{\rm new}|,|\mathbf v_{n+1}^{\rm old}|)}<\varepsilon_2 . \]

If ten passes of Eq. 8 do not meet eps2, \(\Delta t\) is halved and the step is tried again. After each accepted step the working step grows by 10%, back toward the --dt maximum, so one close encounter does not slow down the whole rest of the run. Near the Sun the direction of the Sun's pull swings round at a rate of about Mercury's speed divided by its distance, so the fractional change in Eq. 7 is roughly that rate times \(\Delta t\): the gate therefore lets through a step roughly proportional to \(\varepsilon_1\), which is what the ratio of 5.00 above shows. Both tests compare whole vectors, not separate \(x\) and \(y\) components, so they do not misbehave when one component passes through zero.

Beat 6 · Collision and escape

Strong perturbations end in one of two dramatic ways: Mercury hits a star, or it leaves. Here is one of each.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 1
python main.py --x_init_au 0.45 --max_steps 30000

Look at the first printed line of the first run, and at the Sun-distance range line of both.

Three tasks, in order. First, the first run makes the companion as heavy as the Sun and changes nothing else. It stops after 1920 steps and 41.543 days with termination: collision with Sun, and its closest Sun distance, 0.0046505 AU (6.9570e+08 m), is exactly one nominal solar radius: the run ends where Mercury first reaches the Sun's collision surface, marked on the plot with a black ×. The Sun's dashed circle is only partly drawn, because the run lasted less than one binary period. Then the second run keeps the default companion but starts Mercury at 0.45 AU instead of 0.3 AU. There the default 59,220 m/s is 33% faster than circular, so even the Sun alone would send Mercury out to 3.6 AU on a long ellipse (Eq. 4), right across the companion's circle. After 694.44 days its Sun distance has reached 11.531 AU, more than sixteen times the binary separation, and the plot shows the blue path heading away to the upper left, far outside both stars' circles: the companion has thrown it out. Then notice what the program does not say. The first line reads max_steps reached, not "escaped". The program has no rule for deciding that Mercury has left for good. You have to supply one, for example that Mercury's energy measured from the centre of mass is positive (Experiment 6).

Then how a collision is found. Point masses have no surface, so the program stops Mercury at a collision radius that you choose: by default the Sun's is its nominal radius, \(R_\odot=6.957\times10^{8}\) m, and the companion's is zero, meaning "never stop". After each accepted step, before keeping it, the program checks whether Mercury's straight path relative to the moving star crossed that radius at any point during the step, not only at its end:

Eq. 9 — Crossing a moving collision radius (numerical method, untagged)
\[ \bigl|\,\boldsymbol\rho_n+s\,(\boldsymbol\rho_{n+1}-\boldsymbol\rho_n)\,\bigr|=R_{\rm coll}, \qquad \boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf r-\mathbf r_\star,\qquad 0\le s\le1 , \]

where \(\mathbf r_\star\) is the star's position at the start and at the end of the step. The smallest root \(s\) gives the collision time \(t_n+s\,\Delta t\), and Mercury's position and velocity are interpolated linearly to it. A collision radius is a stopping rule, not a model of what happens on impact, and R_SUN suits only a Sun-like primary. Leaving the companion's radius at zero lets Mercury pass through the companion's position as if it were a point: the default system run for 100,000 steps comes within about 11,500 km of it, well inside where a real red dwarf's surface would be (Experiment 4).

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 4 and Experiment 6 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 7 · Outside the binary

Planets exist around binary stars, orbiting both at once. Try one: two equal suns 1 AU apart, and Mercury started 2.5 AU from the Sun, on the same side as the companion but well beyond it. First with a plausible-looking speed, then with the right one.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 1 --binary_separation_au 1 --x_init_au 2.5 --vy_init 30000 --max_steps 40000
python main.py --m_planet_solar 1 --binary_separation_au 1 --x_init_au 2.5 --vy_init 50846 --max_steps 40000

Look at the Sun-distance range line of each run and at the two plots.

Three tasks, in order. First, find where Mercury really starts. With equal masses the Sun circles 0.5 AU from the centre of mass, on the \(-x\) side, so a Mercury 2.5 AU from the Sun is 2.0 AU from the centre of mass. A circular orbit around the whole \(2\,M_\odot\) binary at that distance needs about \(\sqrt{G(2M_\odot)/2\ \text{AU}}=29{,}785\) m/s. Then remember that vy_init is measured from the Sun, which is itself moving at \(r_\odot\omega=21{,}061\) m/s in the \(-y\) direction at the start. The first run's 30,000 m/s is therefore only 8,939 m/s in the frame of the centre of mass, far too slow for a circular orbit: Mercury falls into the binary, reaches 0.27651 AU from the Sun, and wanders through and around the binary. The second run adds the Sun's speed to the circular speed, \(29{,}785+21{,}061=50{,}846\) m/s, and Mercury goes round the outside of the binary, as the plot shows. Then check the second run's Sun-distance range, 1.5463 to 2.6554 AU, against what an exact 2 AU circle would give, 1.5 to 2.5 AU, because the Sun swings 0.5 AU either way. It is close but not exact: a binary is not a point mass, and its pull on a nearby orbit is not quite that of \(2\,M_\odot\) at the centre. Its Jacobi drift is 1.4107e-09, the smallest on this page, because this orbit never comes near either star.

Then the conversion the program makes before it starts. With the Sun at \(\mathbf r_\odot(0)=(-r_\odot,0)\) moving with \(\mathbf v_\odot(0)=(0,-r_\odot\omega)\), from Eq. 3,

Eq. 10 — From Sun-relative input to the barycentric start DERIVED
\[ \mathbf r(0)=\mathbf r_\odot(0)+(x_{\rm init},\,y_{\rm init}),\qquad \mathbf v(0)=\mathbf v_\odot(0)+(v_{x,\rm init},\,v_{y,\rm init}). \]

For an orbit around the Sun alone, as in Beats 0 to 5, measuring from the Sun is what you want. For an orbit around the whole binary it means adding the Sun's own velocity by hand, as above. mercury_initial_barycentric_state() in physics_mercpert.py performs Eq. 10. The circumbinary orbit takes about 730 days to go round once, so the 925.93-day run covers about 1.3 orbits; Experiment 7 looks for the smallest orbit that survives.

Beat 8 · How far to trust a run

Every printed number comes from a calculation with a finite step. The Jacobi constant tells you how large the resulting error is, because the exact equations keep it fixed. Use it twice: once on the smooth default orbit, once on the close encounter of Beat 5.

python main.py
python main.py --dt 1000 --max_steps 20000
python main.py --dt 500 --max_steps 40000
python main.py --max_steps 80000 --dt 1000

Look at the Maximum fractional Jacobi drift line of each run.

Three tasks, in order. First, the first three runs cover the same 231.48 days with maximum steps of 2000, 1000 and 500 s. Their drifts are 1.2305e-06, 3.0312e-07 and 7.5216e-08. Divide each by the next: 4.06 and 4.03. Halving the step divides the error by four, so the error goes as \(\Delta t^{2}\): the method is second order. Then look at their Sun-distance ranges: all three print 0.30000 to 0.40762 AU. On a smooth orbit the default settings are already accurate to five figures, and apart from the timestep line itself, the drift is the only line that changes. Then compare the fourth run with the first command of Beat 5. It halves the maximum step for the Sun-grazing run, and its drift, 0.0051786, is no better than Beat 5's 0.0051623. Near the Sun the step was already being set by eps1, not by --dt, so shrinking --dt changes nothing there; tightening eps1, as in Beat 5's second run, cut the drift 23-fold, to 0.00022738.

Then the rule this suggests. The corrector of Eq. 8 converges to the trapezoidal rule, whose error over a fixed time span shrinks as the square of the step:

Second-order global convergence (unnumbered)
\[ \text{error}\propto\Delta t^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\text{drift}(\Delta t)}{\text{drift}(\Delta t/2)}\approx4 . \]

So: read the Jacobi drift for every run you intend to quote. On smooth orbits reduce --dt; when the run includes a close encounter, reduce --eps1 and --eps2. All three Sun-grazing runs agree that the closest approach is about 0.017 AU (0.016704, 0.016877 and 0.017065 AU), so that result is robust. What is not robust is the exact path after a close encounter: small differences grow quickly there, so two accurate runs can end in different places. Compare such runs by what they have in common, such as whether Mercury stays bound or how close it comes, not point by point.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 5 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 9 · Where the model and the run stop

Everything so far used sensible settings. Try a few that are not.

python main.py --m_planet_solar 0
python main.py --eps2 0.1
python main.py --binary_separation_au 0.001
python main.py --x_init 0

Look at what each command prints.

Three tasks, in order. First, the input checks. After a short usage reminder, the first three print MercPert: error: argument --m_planet_solar: enter a number greater than zero, MercPert: error: --eps2 must be smaller than --eps1, and MercPert: error: collision radii must sum to less than binary separation. The last one arises because the two stars, 0.001 AU apart, would be closer than the Sun's 6.957e8 m collision radius. None of them starts a run. Then the fourth, which passes the input checks but places Mercury exactly at the Sun's centre. The run cannot begin, because the point-mass force is infinite there, and the program prints MercPert error: Mercury has reached the Sun's point-mass position; the Newtonian point-mass force is singular there. instead of a Python traceback. Then notice what MercPert does not treat as an error: running out of steps. Every run on this page that did not hit a collision surface ended with max_steps reached, a normal outcome, so a longer look always means a larger --max_steps.

Then the list of what the model leaves out, which no setting can put back. The problem is two-dimensional, so inclined orbits are impossible. The two stars are points on an exact circle, with no eccentric binaries, tides, or stellar sizes except the stopping radii. Mercury has no mass, so it never disturbs the stars, and there is no gas, disk, migration, radiation pressure or relativity. The integrator is accurate but not exact: its error grows during close encounters (Beat 8), and a long run through several encounters can follow a quite different path from the exact one while remaining physically typical. A collision is located by linear interpolation within one step, and the printed Sun-distance range uses only the stored steps, so a very brief closest approach can fall between them. Five-figure printing supports comparisons between runs; it is not a claim that the model is accurate to five figures.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 6 in Suggested Experiments.

Algorithm

This section collects the program's numerical rules in one place. Eqs. 7 and 8 are introduced in Beat 5, Eq. 9 in Beat 6, and Eq. 10 in Beat 7.

One run, in order

  1. Check the settings. Masses, separation and dt must be positive and finite, max_steps a positive integer, eps1 and eps2 strictly between 0 and 1 with eps2 smaller than eps1, and the collision radii non-negative with a sum smaller than the separation.
  2. Convert Mercury's Sun-relative start to the barycentric frame (Eq. 10) and store the starting state and its Jacobi constant. If Mercury already starts inside a collision radius, stop at once with zero accepted steps.
  3. Repeat until max_steps steps have been accepted:
    1. Evaluate Mercury's acceleration at the start of the step (Eq. 1).
    2. Try the step with the current working \(\Delta t\): make the Euler prediction and apply the acceleration-change gate (Eq. 7), then iterate the corrector (Eq. 8) up to ten times. If the gate fails, if the corrector has not converged after ten passes, or if a trial state is not finite, halve \(\Delta t\) and try again, up to 80 times. If all 80 fail, or if \(\Delta t\) becomes too small to advance the clock, stop with a clear error.
    3. Before keeping the accepted step, test the whole step against each star's moving collision radius (Eq. 9). If it crosses one, interpolate to the crossing, store that state, and stop.
    4. Otherwise store the new state and its Jacobi constant, and let \(\Delta t\) grow by 10%, up to the --dt maximum.
  4. Print the summary and draw the plot or plots.

Only accepted steps are stored; rejected trial steps leave no trace in the output. The printed working-timestep range covers the accepted steps before any collision shortened the final one, so its minimum can be larger than the last stored time increment. Reaching max_steps is a normal outcome, not an error.

Collision interpolation

Over one accepted step, both Mercury and the star are taken to move in straight lines, so their separation changes linearly in the fraction \(s\) of the step, and Eq. 9 is a quadratic in \(s\). The smaller root in \([0,1]\) is the first crossing. The time, position and velocity at the collision are all interpolated linearly with that \(s\). Because the adaptive step is already short near a star, this estimate improves automatically in a close approach, and it improves further with smaller eps1.

Version and Build ID. python main.py --version prints the model version and the Build ID without running a simulation. The Build ID is a short hash of the four program modules listed under Code Modules and of nothing else — this Help file, the Release Notes, the sample outputs, and the test suite are not part of it. It appears at the top of this page and on the first line of every run's printed summary.

Code Modules

physics_mercpert.py
Physics Layer

Defines the IAU 2015 nominal constants \(GM_\odot=1.3271244\times10^{20}\) m3 s−2 and \(R_\odot=6.957\times10^{8}\) m, the astronomical unit \(1.495978707\times10^{11}\) m, and the two input records. Simulation Driver

Runs the adaptive predictor-corrector (Eqs. 7 and 8), the timestep control and recovery, and the moving-collision test (Eq. 9), and returns every accepted state. In the Python interface both collision radii default to zero; the command line sets the Sun's to \(R_\odot\).

plot_mercpert.py
Visualisation

Draws the barycentric orbit plot, with the two stars' circles limited to one binary period, Mercury's full path, start markers, a collision marker, and a label of Mercury's Sun-relative start, and, on request, the Jacobi-drift plot.

plot_orbits(output, title, merc_ic, binary_params, corner, position_unit) plot_jacobi_drift(output)
main.py
Command-Line Entry Point

Defines every command-line option, accepts distances in metres or AU, prints the six-line summary, and turns invalid settings or failed runs into one-line MercPert: error: ... or MercPert error: ... messages instead of Python tracebacks.

Quick Start

Requirements

Running the Program

Clone or download the repository, then run the program from its folder:

cd GFTGU-Programs/MercPert

Each run prints a summary, described under Printed Summary, and then opens the plot window. The options and their defaults are listed under Parameters.

Output

The orbit plot, titled MercPert orbits, is drawn in the barycentric frame with equal scales on both axes, labelled "barycentric x" and "barycentric y", and shows:

  • the Sun's circle as a dashed red curve and the companion's as a dashed green curve, each drawn for at most one binary period so that repeated circles do not overlap,
  • Mercury's path for the whole run as a solid blue curve,
  • small filled circles at the three starting positions,
  • a black × at the end of the path only when a collision stopped the run,
  • a label giving Mercury's starting position and velocity relative to the Sun, in the corner chosen by --annotation_corner, with the legend in the opposite corner.

Where two plotted curves cross, the bodies were not necessarily there at the same time; only the black × marks a real meeting. With --show_jacobi_diagnostic a second window, MercPert Jacobi-constant drift, plots \((C-C_0)/|C_0|\) against time in days.

run_mercpert() returns a MercPertOutput record with lists of the times, both stars' positions, Mercury's position and velocity, the Jacobi constant, and the time increment of each stored step, together with the number of accepted steps, the reason the run stopped, the body hit (if any), the working-timestep range, and the version and Build ID. The starting state is stored first, so a run that reaches max_steps returns max_steps + 1 states.

Printed Summary

Every run prints six lines and then opens the plot. This is the full summary of the default run, python main.py:

MercPert 1.1.0 (build 9852baf113f7) — accepted steps: 10000; termination: max_steps reached
Circular period of Sun–companion pair: 203.96 days
Test particle integration time: 231.48 days (2.0000e+07 s)
Test particle Sun-distance range: 0.30000 to 0.40762 AU (4.4879e+10 to 6.0979e+10 m)
Maximum fractional Jacobi drift: 1.2305e-06
Adaptive controller working timestep range: 2000.0 to 2000.0 s (accepted steps before collision truncation)
  • First line: version, Build ID, accepted steps, and why the run stopped: max_steps reached, collision with Sun or collision with companion.
  • Circular period of the two stars, from Eq. 2.
  • Integration time of the run, in days and seconds.
  • Sun-distance range: Mercury's smallest and largest distance from the Sun among the stored states.
  • Maximum fractional Jacobi drift: the largest \(|C-C_0|/|C_0|\) over the run (Eq. 6); it is reported as undefined if \(C_0\) happens to be exactly zero.
  • Working timestep range: the smallest and largest accepted controller step, or none (no accepted steps) if the run stopped at once.

All numbers are printed to five significant figures. Use python main.py --version to print only the version and Build ID, with no simulation.

Suggested Experiments

Suggested order: the experiments run from introductory changes of one setting to open-ended investigations. Keep a note of the exact command for every run you compare, and read the Jacobi drift of any run whose numbers you intend to quote (Beat 8).
Experiment 1 · Introductory
A quieter orbit

Goes with Beat 0 and Beat 2.

The default launch is 8.9% faster than circular, so Mercury starts on an ellipse. Launch it at close to the circular speed instead, with python main.py --vy_init 54400, and compare the Sun-distance range and the plot with the default run. Then try a few speeds between 50,000 and 60,000 m/s and find the one that makes the range narrowest.

Check: --vy_init 54400 gives 0.26300 to 0.31302 AU, a spread of 0.050 AU, against 0.30000 to 0.40762 AU (0.108 AU) for the default. Even a launch at the isolated-star circular speed is not circular here: the default companion alone produces a spread of about 0.05 AU (Beat 3).

Experiment 2 · Introductory
Where does the perturbation stop being small?

Goes with Beat 3.

Extend Beat 3's sequence to heavier companions, keeping --vy_init 54379.16: try --m_planet_solar 0.3 and 0.5 as well as 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1. Plot the spread of the Sun-distance range against companion mass on logarithmic axes. Where does the straight line of slope one, the mark of a small perturbation, stop fitting?

Check: the spreads are 0.00049, 0.00491 and 0.05020 AU for 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1 M☉, then 0.12144 AU at 0.3 M☉ (0.20721 to 0.32865 AU) and 0.17373 AU at 0.5 M☉ (0.14417 to 0.31790 AU). Three and five times the mass of 0.1 M☉ give only 2.4 and 3.5 times the spread: the simple proportion has broken down by a few tenths of a solar mass.

Experiment 3 · Introductory–Intermediate
Prograde and retrograde

Goes with Beat 3.

Reverse the direction of Mercury's orbit by changing the sign of its starting velocity, so that it goes round the Sun against the binary's motion: compare python main.py --vy_init 54400 with python main.py --vy_init -54400, and then the same pair at other starting distances. Use the same number of steps and the same tolerances for each pair.

Check: the prograde run ranges over 0.26300 to 0.31302 AU and the retrograde run over 0.28457 to 0.30606 AU: the retrograde orbit is disturbed less than half as much. A retrograde orbit passes the companion faster, so each encounter lasts a shorter time.

Experiment 4 · Intermediate
Longer runs, and giving the companion a size

Goes with Beat 5 and Beat 6.

Continue Beat 5's run further, with --max_steps 60000 and then 100,000, and watch the closest Sun distance, the smallest working step and the Jacobi drift. At 100,000 steps Mercury also passes extremely close to the companion, which by default has no collision radius. Give it one about the size of a real 0.1 M☉ red dwarf, roughly 0.12 solar radii: python main.py --max_steps 100000 --companion_collision_radius 8.4e7.

Check: at 60,000 steps the Sun-distance range is 0.013970 to 0.82179 AU, the smallest step 3.6155 s and the drift 0.014880. With the companion's collision radius set, the run stops after 59365 steps and 1344.2 days with collision with companion. A result that depended on Mercury passing through a star is not a result, so choose collision radii before trusting a long run.

Experiment 5 · Intermediate
Which setting controls the error?

Goes with Beat 8.

On the smooth default orbit, compare python main.py --dt 1000 --max_steps 20000 with python main.py --dt 1000 --max_steps 20000 --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-5. On the Sun-grazing run of Beat 5, compare changing --dt with changing --eps1. Write a one-sentence rule for which setting to change, and when.

Check: both smooth runs print a drift of 3.0312e-07: with no close encounter the tolerances are never the limiting factor and only --dt matters. On the Sun-grazing run it is the other way round (Beat 8): halving --dt leaves the drift near 0.0052, while --eps1 0.01 --eps2 1e-5 reduces it to 0.00022738.

Experiment 6 · Advanced
Map a finite-time stability boundary

Goes with Beat 6 and Beat 9.

Hold the default binary fixed and vary Mercury's starting distance from the Sun, for example python main.py --x_init_au 0.25 --max_steps 30000, then 0.35 and 0.45, and more values in between. Decide in advance how you will classify each run after 30,000 steps: still close to the Sun, strongly disturbed, collided, or escaped. For "escaped", write a few lines of Python that call run_mercpert() and compute Mercury's energy per unit mass, \(\tfrac12v^2-GM_\odot/r_\odot-GM_c/r_c\), at the last stored state; a positive value means it is no longer bound to the binary.

Check: at 0.25 AU Mercury stays between 0.23209 and 0.25319 AU. At 0.35 AU it ranges from 0.032654 to 0.67442 AU but its energy stays negative. At 0.45 AU it passes about 0.045 AU from the companion on day 56, its energy turns positive, and it ends 11.531 AU from the Sun. The boundary you find depends on the run length and on your criteria; it is not an absolute stability limit.

Experiment 7 · Advanced
The smallest orbit around both stars

Goes with Beat 1 and Beat 7.

Using Beat 7's equal-mass binary, launch Mercury on circular orbits around the centre of mass at different distances \(R\) from it. For each, --x_init_au is \(R+0.5\) and --vy_init is \(\sqrt{G(2M_\odot)/R}\) plus the Sun's 21,061 m/s. Run each for 60,000 steps and find the smallest \(R\) at which Mercury stays near its starting orbit. Express it as a multiple of the binary separation.

Check: at \(R=2.0\) AU (--x_init_au 2.5 --vy_init 50846) Mercury's Sun distance stays between 1.5095 and 2.6554 AU for 1388.9 days. At \(R=1.5\) AU (--x_init_au 2.0 --vy_init 55453) it is thrown out, becoming unbound after about 128 days and reaching 17.893 AU. The boundary lies between 1.5 and 2 times the separation for runs of this length.

Experiment 8 · Capstone
Balancing at the inner Lagrange point

Goes with Beat 4.

Between the two stars there is a point, \(L_1\), where a body turning with the binary feels no net force in the rotating frame. With the default binary, find it: put Mercury on the line between the stars (--y_init 0 --vx_init 0), give it the speed that turns with the binary, vy_init = \(\omega\times\) x_init (measured from the Sun, as in Eq. 10), and adjust x_init until it lingers longest. Then explain, using the Jacobi constant, why Mercury at \(L_1\) marks the narrowest opening between the region around the Sun and the region around the companion.

Check: \(\omega=3.5655\times10^{-7}\) s−1 and \(L_1\) lies 0.50226 AU from the Sun. python main.py --x_init_au 0.50226 --vy_init 26789.83 --max_steps 5000 stays within 0.01 AU of its start for about 93 days and then drifts toward the companion (Sun distance 0.50226 to 0.63380 AU), while python main.py --x_init_au 0.5 --vy_init 26669.35 --max_steps 5000, just 0.0023 AU nearer the Sun, falls toward the Sun within about 22 days (0.096849 to 0.50000 AU). \(L_1\) is an equilibrium, but an unstable one.

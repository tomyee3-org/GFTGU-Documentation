Overview

Star builds a star from the centre outwards. It starts from a central pressure and a central temperature, and at each small step in radius it lets the weight of the gas above push the pressure down, and the gas it passes add to the mass inside. It stops where the pressure reaches zero: that radius is the surface of the star, and the mass inside it is the mass of the star.

Every run prints the radius and the mass of the star; the radial step it used; the radius and mass of the Lane–Emden solution of the same equations, which the program computes separately and far more accurately than its steps, with the relative difference of the numbers the program integrated; and the pressure, density, temperature and mass at five points inside the star. Then it plots one of pressure, density, temperature or enclosed mass against radius.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: a star is gas in balance between its own weight and its own pressure; with one power law linking pressure to density, the balance fixes the whole star — its radius, its mass and every profile inside it — from the central conditions alone, and the program's step-by-step answer can be compared with the Lane–Emden solution of the same equations, computed far more accurately, which shows how large the error of the steps is. Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument, a command whose printed summary or plot illustrates that argument, and then the equation that the printed numbers follow from.

Scope: Star is Newtonian. SphereGravity shows why only the mass inside a radius pulls on the gas at that radius; Atmosphere balances pressure against a gravity that does not change with height; Neutron repeats this calculation in general relativity.
What this program is, and what it does not model. Star is a teaching model of a spherical star in Newtonian gravity, held up by gas pressure. The link between pressure and density is one power law, the polytrope, imposed everywhere in the star; nothing in the program generates energy, carries it outwards by radiation or convection, or changes the composition from centre to surface. The temperature is inferred from the pressure and density, not computed from how heat flows. The default inputs are chosen so that the radius and mass come out close to the Sun's, but the interior is not the Sun's interior. The numerical method is the simplest one, forward Euler steps, and it carries an error that the program measures against the Lane–Emden solution of the same equations, computed far more accurately. Beats 5 to 8 show how large that error is and where the model stops.

How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation that the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equations at work, not independent evidence that they are right. The outside check the program offers is the Lane–Emden solution of the same equations, computed separately and far more accurately, printed beside the numbers it integrated. Beats 5 to 8 compare the two.

The program has no random numbers, so every command prints the numbers quoted here. Beat 0 shows the default star, Beats 1 to 3 its density, temperature and mass, and Beat 4 how the star changes with its central conditions. Beat 5 gives the Lane–Emden solution, Beats 6 and 7 the error of the steps, and Beat 8 the limits of the model.

Run every command from the program's folder. Each run prints a summary and then opens a plot window; close the window to end the program. Each beat names the lines of the summary to look at. All quantities are in SI units: metres, kilograms, pascals, kilograms per cubic metre and kelvin.

What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag. A red LAW tag marks a law of nature that the model adopts as it stands; Eq. (4), the ideal gas law, is the only equation with this tag, since Newton's gravity enters through Eqs. (1) and (2). A green ODE tag marks a differential equation that the program integrates: Eqs. (2) and (3). An orange CLOSURE tag marks a relation chosen for teaching rather than derived: Eq. (5), the polytrope, is the only one, and it is the assumption that everything else in the model depends on. A grey DEFINITION tag marks a quantity defined by a formula. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows from the equations already stated. A violet ALGORITHM tag marks a rule of the calculation, a choice made by the program rather than physics: Eqs. (14) to (17). The tags are there so that a law, an assumption, a definition and a numerical rule are never mistaken for one another. Eighteen equations are numbered.

Beat 0 · A star that holds itself up

Run the program with all its defaults. The plot shows the pressure from the centre to the surface.

python main.py

Look at the plot, and at the two lines under Stellar structure summary, Radius (m) and Total mass (kg).

Three tasks, in order. First, read the plot. The pressure starts at the central pressure, 7.158e+15 Pa, falls slowly at the very centre and then steeply, fastest at about a seventh of the radius; on this linear axis it is close to zero over the whole outer half of the star, and it reaches zero at the surface. Then read the size. The star has a radius of 6.9674e+08 m and a mass of 1.9825e+30 kg; the Sun's radius is about 6.957e8 m and its mass about 1.989e30 kg, so the default star is within 0.2% of the Sun's radius and 0.4% of its mass. Then ask why the pressure falls. Say what pushes up on a thin layer of gas, and what pulls it down, if the layer does not move.

Then the balance. Inside a spherical star, the gas at radius \(r\) is pulled towards the centre only by the mass \(m(r)\) inside that radius: by Newton's shell theorem, the shells of gas outside \(r\) pull on it with no net force at all.

(1)
Gravity inside a spherical star DERIVED
\[ g(r)=\frac{G\,m(r)}{r^{2}} . \]

A thin layer of gas of area \(A\) and thickness \(dr\) has mass \(\rho A\,dr\), so gravity pulls it inwards with force \(g\rho A\,dr\). It stays where it is only if the pressure below it is larger than the pressure above it by exactly that force divided by \(A\).

(2)
Hydrostatic equilibrium ODE
\[ \frac{dp}{dr}=-\frac{G\,m(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^{2}} . \]

The mass inside \(r\) grows by the mass of each thin shell passed, of area \(4\pi r^2\).

(3)
Mass continuity ODE
\[ \frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^{2}\rho(r),\qquad m(0)=0 . \]

Here \(p\) is the pressure, \(\rho\) the density and \(G=6.672\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\), the value the program uses. At the centre the pressure falls slowly because there is little mass inside to pull; near the surface it falls slowly again because there is little gas left to weigh, but by then it is too small to see on a linear axis. Eqs. (2) and (3) are two equations for three unknowns, \(p\), \(\rho\) and \(m\): they cannot be integrated until the density is tied to the pressure. Beat 1 supplies that link.

Beat 1 · What the pressure needs: a density

Plot the density on a logarithmic axis, so that the thin outer layers can be seen.

python main.py --output_type density --log_y

Look at the plot, and at the columns pressure (Pa) and density (kg/m^3) of the Interior checkpoints table.

Three tasks, in order. First, the centre. The central density is 49187 kg/m3, the row 0%; compute it from the central pressure 7.158e+15 Pa, the central temperature 2.263e+07 K and \(\mu=1.285\) with Eq. (6) below. Then halfway out. At half the radius, the row 50%, the pressure has fallen to 1.12e+14 Pa and the density to 2313.2 kg/m3; check that \(\rho_c\,(p/p_c)^{1/\gamma}\) with \(\gamma=1.36\) gives the same density. Then the plot. On the logarithmic axis the density falls from 49187 kg/m3 by more than nine powers of ten before the last point, and faster and faster towards the surface, where it reaches zero; the zero itself cannot be drawn on a logarithmic axis, so the last point is left out. Say why a smaller \(\gamma\) makes the density fall faster as the pressure falls.

Then the link between pressure and density. The gas of a star is, to a good approximation, an ideal gas.

(4)
The ideal gas law LAW
\[ p=\frac{\rho\,k_{\mathrm B}T}{\mu\,m_p} . \]

Here \(T\) is the temperature, \(k_{\mathrm B}=1.38\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}\), \(m_p=1.67\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}\) and \(\mu\), --mu, is the mean mass of a gas particle in units of \(m_p\). The ideal gas law brings in the temperature, a fourth unknown, and the equation that would fix it, the flow of heat out of the star, is not in the model. The program instead ties the pressure to the density directly.

(5)
The polytrope CLOSURE
\[ p=K\rho^{\gamma},\qquad\text{or}\qquad \rho=D\,p^{1/\gamma},\quad D=K^{-1/\gamma} . \]

This is the one assumption the whole model rests on: a single power law, with one exponent \(\gamma\), --gamma, and one constant, from the centre to the surface. It holds for an ideal gas whose entropy is the same everywhere, as in a region stirred by convection, with \(\gamma\) its ratio of specific heats, and for fully degenerate electrons in the non-relativistic or ultra-relativistic limit; the layers of a real star it describes only roughly. The constant is fixed at the centre, where the ideal gas law gives the density.

(6)
Central density and the polytropic constant DERIVED
\[ q=\frac{m_p\,\mu}{k_{\mathrm B}},\qquad \rho_c=\frac{p_c\,q}{T_c},\qquad D=\frac{\rho_c}{p_c^{1/\gamma}} . \]

The shorthand \(q\) and the constant \(D\) are definitions; \(\rho_c\) is the ideal gas law, Eq. (4), at the centre. With the default inputs, \(\rho_c=7.158\times10^{15}\times1.67\times10^{-27}\times1.285/(1.38\times10^{-23}\times2.263\times10^{7})=49187\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), the 0% row. The ideal gas law is used only at the centre, to set \(\rho_c\) and \(D\), and afterwards only to read off a temperature. Beat 2 looks at that temperature.

Beat 2 · The temperature comes last

Plot the temperature.

python main.py --output_type temperature

Look at the plot, and at the columns pressure (Pa) and temperature (K) of the Interior checkpoints table.

Three tasks, in order. First, the centre and the surface. The temperature is the input 2.263e+07 K at the centre and falls to zero at the surface, because the model's surface is where the pressure is zero; the surface of a real star has a temperature of thousands of kelvin. Then halfway out. At the row 50% the temperature is 7.5289e+06 K, a third of the central value; check that \(T_c\,(p/p_c)^{1-1/\gamma}\), with the pressure 1.12e+14 Pa of that row, gives the same number to four figures. Then the shape. The temperature falls far more gently than the pressure: from the centre to half the radius the pressure falls by a factor of 64, the temperature only by a factor of 3. Say why a temperature that follows from the pressure and density alone cannot tell you how much energy the star radiates.

Then where the temperature comes from. Once the density is known from the pressure, the ideal gas law, Eq. (4), gives the temperature; with Eq. (5) it depends on the pressure alone.

(7)
The temperature DERIVED
\[ T=\frac{q\,p}{\rho}=\frac{q}{D}\,p^{\,1-1/\gamma},\qquad \frac{T}{T_c}=\left(\frac{p}{p_c}\right)^{1-1/\gamma} . \]

With \(1-1/\gamma=0.26471\), the pressure ratio 1.12e+14/7.158e+15 = 0.015647 gives a temperature ratio of 0.3327, and \(0.3327\times2.263\times10^7=7.529\times10^6\) K. In a real star the temperature is set by the flow of energy outwards from the core, and the pressure and density follow from it; here the order is reversed, and the temperature is the last thing computed. The central temperature and density of the default model differ substantially from those of detailed solar models, although its radius and mass are close to the Sun's. Beat 3 looks at the mass.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 2 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 3 · Where the mass is

Plot the mass inside each radius.

python main.py --output_type mass

Look at the plot, and at the column mass within r (kg) of the Interior checkpoints table and the line Total mass (kg).

Three tasks, in order. First, read the mass inside a quarter of the radius, 6.2826e+29 kg, and inside half the radius, 1.6732e+30 kg: that is 32% and 84% of the total mass 1.9825e+30 kg, although a quarter and a half of the radius enclose only 1.6% and 12.5% of the volume. Then compute the mean density of the star from its mass and radius with Eq. (8): about 1399 kg/m3. Then compare it with the central density of Beat 1, 49187 kg/m3: the centre is 35.2 times denser than the average. Say why the mass curve on the plot is almost flat over the outer quarter of the radius, which holds only 1% of the mass.

Then the mean density.

(8)
Mean density DEFINITION
\[ \bar\rho=\frac{3M}{4\pi R^{3}} . \]

With \(M=1.9825\times10^{30}\) kg and \(R=6.9674\times10^{8}\) m, \(\bar\rho=1399\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), close to the Sun's mean density. A polytrope is always centrally condensed, and the more so the smaller \(\gamma\) is; Beat 5 gives the Lane–Emden ratio \(\rho_c/\bar\rho\) for any \(\gamma\), and Beat 8 shows a star in which nearly all the mass is in the inner quarter of the radius. Beat 4 asks how the star changes when its central conditions change.

Beat 4 · One star, a family of stars

Make the centre 10% hotter, at the same central pressure.

python main.py --T_c 2.4893e7

Look at Radius (m) and Total mass (kg), at the three lines under Numerical grid, and at the two relative differences under Lane-Emden solution of the same polytrope (numerical reference). Compare each with the default run of Beat 0.

Three tasks, in order. First, the radius: 7.6641e+08 m, against 6.9674e+08 m in Beat 0, a ratio of 1.1000, exactly the ratio of the central temperatures. Then the mass: 2.3988e+30 kg, against 1.9825e+30 kg, a ratio of 1.2100, the square of 1.1. Then the grid: the radial step is 5.791e+05 m, 1.1 times the default 5.2645e+05 m, the number of grid points is 1325 in both runs, and the relative differences from the Lane–Emden solution, -0.014174 and -0.0018494, are the same in both runs. Say what that tells you about the shapes of the two stars.

Then why. The central values set one natural length, the radial scale, and the program's radial step is a fixed fraction of it, --steps_per_scale.

(9)
The radial scale DEFINITION
\[ \ell=\frac{\sqrt{p_c/G}}{\rho_c} . \]

For the default star \(\ell=2.1058\times10^{8}\) m, and the step \(\ell/400\) is the printed 5.2645e+05 m. If lengths are measured in units of \(\ell\), densities in units of \(\rho_c\), pressures in units of \(p_c\) and masses in units of \(\rho_c\ell^3\), Eqs. (2), (3) and (5) contain no constant at all except \(\gamma\). So every star with the same \(\gamma\) has the same shape; only the two scales change, the radius in proportion to \(\ell\) and the mass in proportion to \(\rho_c\ell^3\). With \(\rho_c=p_c m_p\mu/(k_{\mathrm B}T_c)\) from Eq. (6):

(10)
How the star scales with its central conditions DERIVED
\[ R\propto\frac{T_c}{\mu\,p_c^{1/2}},\qquad M\propto\frac{T_c^{2}}{\mu^{2}\,p_c^{1/2}}\qquad(\gamma\ \text{fixed}) . \]

Because the step scales with \(\ell\) too, the program's Euler steps land on the same points of the same dimensionless star, and the scaling holds to rounding for the printed numbers, not only for the Lane–Emden solution. Holding \(\gamma\) fixed while changing \(p_c\) or \(T_c\) also changes the constant \(K\) of Eq. (5): these stars follow the same law \(p\propto\rho^\gamma\) but not the same equation of state. Experiment 8 builds a family with one fixed \(K\). Beat 5 finds the shape that all these stars share.

Beat 5 · The Lane–Emden solution

Change the exponent to \(\gamma=2\), for which the exact solution is a simple formula.

python main.py --gamma 2

Look at the three lines under Lane-Emden solution of the same polytrope (numerical reference), and at Radius (m) and Total mass (kg).

Three tasks, in order. First, read the index: Polytropic index n = 1/(gamma - 1) is 1. Then compute the exact radius by hand from Eq. (13) below: \(R=\sqrt{\pi p_c/(2G)}/\rho_c\), with \(p_c=7.158\times10^{15}\) Pa and the central density 49187 kg/m3 of the 0% row, gives 2.6392e+08 m, the printed Lane-Emden radius (m). Then compare: the program's own radius, 2.6239e+08 m, is short by the printed -0.0058198, and its mass, 1.1565e+30 kg, is larger than the exact 1.1513e+30 kg by 0.004502. Say why a smaller radius and a larger mass can both be errors of the same calculation.

Then the Lane–Emden solution. Write the density as \(\rho=\rho_c\,\theta^n\) and the radius as \(r=a\,\xi\). Eliminating \(m\) between Eqs. (2) and (3), with Eq. (5), leaves one equation for \(\theta(\xi)\), the Lane–Emden equation.

(11)
The Lane–Emden equation DERIVED
\[ \frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\frac{d}{d\xi}\!\left(\xi^{2}\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right)=-\theta^{\,n},\qquad \theta(0)=1,\ \theta'(0)=0,\qquad n=\frac{1}{\gamma-1},\quad a=\ell\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{4\pi}} . \]

The pressure is \(p=p_c\,\theta^{n+1}\), so the surface is the first zero of \(\theta\), at \(\xi=\xi_1\). The radius and the mass follow from \(\xi_1\) and the slope of \(\theta\) there, which depend only on \(n\).

(12)
Radius and mass of a polytrope DERIVED
\[ R=\xi_1\,a,\qquad M=4\pi a^{3}\rho_c\,\omega_n,\quad \omega_n=-\xi_1^{2}\,\theta'(\xi_1),\qquad \frac{\rho_c}{\bar\rho}=\frac{\xi_1^{3}}{3\,\omega_n} . \]

For \(n=1\) the Lane–Emden equation is linear and has a formula for its solution, which serves as a check on the program's own integration.

(13)
The polytrope with \(n=1\) DERIVED
\[ \theta=\frac{\sin\xi}{\xi},\qquad \xi_1=\pi,\quad \omega_1=\pi,\qquad R=\pi a=\frac{1}{\rho_c}\sqrt{\frac{\pi p_c}{2G}} . \]

For every \(n\), including \(n=1\), the program integrates Eq. (11) itself, with fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps that are refined as the surface approaches, and then again with steps half as long, and prints the half-step values. The relative change between the two integrations estimates the error that remains, and in checks against an independent high-accuracy solver it has always been the larger of the two. It is below \(10^{-8}\) for every \(n\) below 4.99, so there the printed values are good to eight significant figures or more. It passes \(10^{-7}\) only when \(\gamma\) is within about \(1.3\times10^{-7}\) of 6/5, and then the program prints a warning line under the Lane–Emden mass, because the values may no longer be good to seven significant figures (Beat 8). For the default \(n=2.7778\) it finds \(\xi_1=6.1212\) and \(\omega_n=2.0876\), so the Lane–Emden centre-to-mean density ratio is 36.62, against 35.2 from the integrated radius and mass of Beat 3. The Lane–Emden values solve the same equations as the program's steps, far more accurately, so the printed differences measure the error of the steps in the radius and the mass, including the first point and the surface interpolation of Beats 6 and 7, and nothing else. They are not a check of the profiles inside the star, and they do not measure how well a polytrope describes a real star. Beat 6 shows how the error shrinks.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 7 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 6 · Euler steps and their error

Halve the radial step: 800 steps per radial scale instead of 400. The star then needs more grid points than the default 2000, so allow 4000.

python main.py --steps_per_scale 800 --max_points 4000

Look at the three lines under Numerical grid, and at the two relative differences under Lane-Emden solution of the same polytrope (numerical reference). Compare them with the default run of Beat 0.

Three tasks, in order. First, the grid: the step is 2.6323e+05 m, half the default 5.2645e+05 m, with 2666 grid points and no restart. Then the radius error: -0.0075071, against -0.014174 at the default step, a ratio of 1.888. Then the mass error: -0.00092707, against -0.0018494, a ratio of 1.995. Say what the next halving of the step should give, and check it with Experiment 6.

Then the steps. The program starts at the centre and moves outwards in equal steps \(\Delta r\), using at each step the values at the start of the step.

(14)
Forward Euler steps ALGORITHM
\[ p_j=p_{j-1}-\frac{G\rho_{j-1}m_{j-1}}{r_{j-1}^{2}}\,\Delta r,\qquad m_j=m_{j-1}+4\pi r_{j-1}^{2}\rho_{j-1}\,\Delta r,\qquad \rho_j=D\,p_j^{1/\gamma},\quad T_j=\frac{q\,p_j}{\rho_j} . \]

At the centre \(Gm/r^2\) is 0/0, so the program does not step from \(r=0\): it puts the first point one step out, gives it the central pressure, density and temperature, and the mass of a uniform sphere of radius \(\Delta r\).

(15)
The grid and the first point ALGORITHM
\[ \Delta r=\frac{\ell}{N_s},\qquad r_1=\Delta r,\quad p_1=p_c,\quad \rho_1=\rho_c,\quad T_1=T_c,\quad m_1=\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_c\,\Delta r^{3} . \]

Here \(N_s\) is --steps_per_scale. Each Euler step makes an error proportional to \(\Delta r^2\), and a star takes a number of steps proportional to \(1/\Delta r\), so the error is proportional to \(\Delta r\): halving the step halves the error. The mass follows this closely, as the ratio 1.995 shows. The radius follows it less cleanly, 1.888 here, because the density \(\rho=Dp^{1/\gamma}\) is not smooth where the pressure reaches zero, which affects the interpolated surface; for \(n<1\), such as \(\gamma=4\) in Experiment 7, the radius error does not fall in a regular way at all. The default star is 1.4% smaller than the exact polytrope of Eq. (12). Its closeness to the Sun's radius in Beat 0 is therefore partly an accident of the step size: the Lane–Emden solution of the same equations has a radius of 7.0676e+08 m, 1.6% larger than the Sun's. Beat 7 looks at the last step and at what happens when the grid is too short.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 6 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 7 · The surface and the restart

Allow only 1000 grid points, fewer than the default star needs at the default step.

python main.py --max_points 1000

Look at the three lines under Numerical grid, at Radius (m), and at the two relative differences.

Three tasks, in order. First, the restart: Restarts (radial step doubled) is 1, and the step is 1.0529e+06 m, twice the default 5.2645e+05 m. Then the cost: 654 grid points, a radius of 6.8749e+08 m, and a radius error of -0.02726, about twice the default -0.014174. Then run python main.py --steps_per_scale 200 and compare: every printed number is the same, except that it reports no restart. Say why giving the program fewer grid points made the answer worse, not just shorter.

Then the last step. The step at which the pressure first becomes zero or negative is not kept. Instead the program draws a straight line through the pressures at its two ends and puts the surface where that line reaches zero.

(16)
The surface ALGORITHM
\[ R=r_{j-1}+f\,\Delta r,\quad f=\frac{p_{j-1}}{p_{j-1}-p_j^{\rm trial}},\qquad p=\rho=T=0\ \text{at}\ R,\qquad M=m_{j-1}+4\pi r_{j-1}^{2}\rho_{j-1}\,f\,\Delta r . \]

The last shell uses the density at its inner edge, as every Euler step does; since the density falls to zero across it, its mass is slightly too large. If the pressure has not reached zero after --max_points points, the step was too small to reach the surface, and the program starts again from the centre with a larger one.

(17)
The restart ALGORITHM
\[ \Delta r\;\to\;2\,\Delta r\quad\text{until the surface lies within } N_{\max}\ \text{points (at most 64 times)} . \]

Here \(N_{\max}\) is --max_points. The restart buys range, not accuracy: after \(k\) restarts the run is the one with \(N_s/2^k\) steps per radial scale. Raising --max_points therefore refines a run only by removing restarts; once Restarts (radial step doubled) is 0, more points change nothing, and only --steps_per_scale refines the run further. Beat 8 shows a star that needs several restarts.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 6 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 8 · Where one polytrope stops

Take \(\gamma\) close to its lower limit of 1.2, then much closer, and then try the limit itself and a logarithmic mass plot. The last two commands are rejected; the messages say why.

python main.py --gamma 1.21
python main.py --gamma 1.2000000001
python main.py --gamma 1.2
python main.py --output_type mass --log_y

Look, in the first run, at the three lines under Numerical grid, at the Lane–Emden lines, and at the column mass within r (kg). Then read the two error messages.

Three tasks, in order. First, the grid: the program restarted 4 times, and its step, 8.4233e+06 m, is 16 times the default. Then the error: the index is 4.7619, and the integrated radius, 7.2406e+09 m, is short of the Lane–Emden 9.9626e+09 m by -0.27322, a quarter, with the mass short by -0.080272. Then the mass: 2.8287e+30 kg of the total 2.8415e+30 kg lies inside a quarter of the radius, almost all of it. Finally, --gamma 1.2 is refused because the polytrope then has no finite radius, and a logarithmic mass plot because the mass at the centre is zero. Say why a star whose outer three quarters hold almost no mass needs so many small steps to reach its surface.

Then the limit. The Lane–Emden function \(\theta\) reaches zero at a finite \(\xi_1\) only for \(n<5\); for \(n=5\) it falls off forever and the star has no surface.

(18)
A finite radius DERIVED
\[ \xi_1\ \text{finite}\iff n<5\iff\gamma>\tfrac{6}{5}\qquad(\gamma>1) . \]

As \(\gamma\) approaches 6/5, \(\xi_1\) grows without limit, the envelope becomes enormous and nearly empty, and the step needed to reach the surface grows: the restarts of the first run trade accuracy for range, which is why its radius is a quarter short. With enough points and a finer step the error comes back down (Experiment 6). Closer still to 6/5 the program keeps printing a finite radius, but it is an artefact of the steps: in the second run, with --gamma 1.2000000001, the Lane–Emden radius is tens of millions of times larger, and the printed relative difference, -1, is the warning. So near 6/5 the Lane–Emden values themselves become less certain, and the second run also prints Warning: gamma is so close to 6/5 that these Lane-Emden values are uncertain by about 0.0001 (relative). under the Lane–Emden mass, with the index given to more figures, 4.9999999975, since five figures would round it to 5. The program refuses \(\gamma\le1.2\), and does not compute the Lane–Emden solution if its surface lies beyond \(\xi=10^{12}\), which happens only for \(\gamma\) within about \(10^{-12}\) of 6/5. A second limit has nothing to do with the numbers: \(\gamma=4/3\) is the boundary of stability of a star whose gas responds to compression with that same exponent, but the program computes equilibrium only, and a real star cannot be classified by one constant \(\gamma\). The red giant and neutron star of Experiments 9 and 10 stretch one polytrope further than any real star allows.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 9, Experiment 10 and Experiment 11 in Suggested Experiments.

Algorithm

This section collects in one place the rules of the calculation that the beats introduced one at a time.

The integrated star

  1. Compute \(q\), \(\rho_c\) and \(D\) from Eq. (6), the radial scale \(\ell\) from Eq. (9), and the step \(\Delta r=\ell/N_s\), with \(N_s\) = --steps_per_scale.
  2. Store the centre, \(r_0=0\), with \(p=p_c\), \(\rho=\rho_c\), \(T=T_c\), \(m=0\), and the first point of Eq. (15).
  3. Step outwards with Eq. (14). When a trial pressure is zero or negative, place the surface by Eq. (16) and stop.
  4. If --max_points points are used without reaching the surface, double \(\Delta r\) and start again from step 2, Eq. (17), at most 64 times.

The printed summary

  1. Print the radius and mass of the surface point, the step \(\Delta r\) finally used, the number of grid points from the centre to the surface, and the number of restarts.
  2. Compute \(n=1/(\gamma-1)\) and integrate the Lane–Emden equation (11) from its series near \(\xi=0\) with fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps of 0.1% of \(\xi\) (0.001 near the centre), shorter where \(\theta\) is small if \(n<1\), until \(\theta\) first becomes zero or negative; then repeat that last step in three stages, with steps 10, 100 and 1000 times shorter. Locate the zero on a cubic through the last step, and add the mass of the last short interval to \(\omega_n\). Repeat the whole integration with steps half as long, keep the half-step \(\xi_1\) and \(\omega_n\), and take the larger of their relative changes between the two integrations as their uncertainty. It is below \(10^{-8}\) for \(n\) below 4.99; if it exceeds \(10^{-7}\), which happens only for \(\gamma\) within about \(1.3\times10^{-7}\) of 6/5, the printed summary says so.
  3. Print the radius and mass of Eq. (12) and the relative differences of the integrated radius and mass from them.
  4. Print the pressure, density, temperature and mass at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 90% of the radius, interpolated linearly between the two grid points on either side.

Program Files

main.py
Entry Point & Configuration

Defines every command-line option in parse_args(), calls the driver, prints the summary (see Printed Summary) with print_structure_summary(), and passes the result to the plotter. It can be imported without running anything.

physics_star.py
Physics Layer

Contains the constants; q_factor(), central_density(), polytropic_D() and radial_scale(); the single steps hydrostatic_step() and mass_step(); density_from_pressure() and temperature_from_prho(); polytropic_index(), lane_emden_surface() and lane_emden_solution(); the input checks; and the version and build identifier.

driver_star.py
Driver Layer

Provides integrate_star(p_c, T_c, mu, gamma, max_points=2000, steps_per_scale=400, output_type="pressure"), which returns a StarResult holding the radius, pressure, density, temperature and mass at every grid point, the step used, the number of restarts and \(\gamma\); and interpolate_profile_checkpoints() for the checkpoint table.

plot_star.py
Visualisation

Provides plot_star_structure(result, log_y=False), which draws the selected profile (see Plot), shows it, and returns the figure and axes so that a script can save them.

The build identifier printed by python main.py --version and shown at the top of this page is computed from these four files only, so it changes whenever the program changes and not when this page or the tests change.

Quick Start

Requirements

Python 3.10 or later with matplotlib:

pip install "matplotlib>=3.5"

Running the Program

Clone or download the repository, then open a terminal in the folder that contains main.py:

cd GFTGU-Programs/Star
python main.py
python main.py --output_type temperature
python main.py --help

To display the program version and build identifier without running a calculation, use python main.py --version. Options omitted from a command keep their defaults.

# A finer integration of the default star
python main.py --steps_per_scale 1600 --max_points 8000

Command-Line Parameters

All settings are command-line options described in main.py. --output_type and --log_y change only the plot; the printed summary is the same whichever profile is plotted.

ParameterDefaultDescription
--p_c7.158e15Central pressure \(p_c\) in Pa; any positive finite number. Eq. (10): at fixed \(T_c\), \(\mu\), \(\gamma\), the radius and mass scale as \(p_c^{-1/2}\).
--T_c2.263e7Central temperature \(T_c\) in K; any positive finite number. It sets \(\rho_c\) with Eq. (6); the radius scales as \(T_c\) and the mass as \(T_c^2\).
--mu1.285Mean particle mass \(\mu\) in units of the proton mass; any positive finite number. It is a parameter of the model, not the actual composition of the Sun's core; the radius scales as \(\mu^{-1}\) and the mass as \(\mu^{-2}\).
--gamma1.36Polytropic exponent \(\gamma\) of Eq. (5), not the index \(n=1/(\gamma-1)\); it must be greater than 1.2, Eq. (18).
--max_points2000Largest number of grid points in one attempt, from 3 to 1000000. A star that needs more points is restarted with a doubled step, Eq. (17); raising it refines a run only by removing restarts.
--steps_per_scale400Steps \(N_s\) per radial scale, Eq. (15); a positive whole number. Larger values give a finer run when no restart is needed.
--output_type"pressure"Profile to plot: pressure in Pa, density in kg/m3, temperature in K, or mass, the mass inside each radius, in kg.
--log_yFalseA switch: when present, the vertical axis is logarithmic. It is refused with --output_type mass, because the mass at the centre is zero; the surface point, where the other profiles are zero, is left out of a logarithmic plot.
Input checking: The command-line parser rejects a --p_c, --T_c or --mu that is not a positive finite number, a --gamma of 1.2 or less, a --max_points that is not a whole number from 3 to 1000000, a --steps_per_scale that is not a positive whole number, and an unknown --output_type; the program then refuses --log_y with --output_type mass before integrating. Accepted inputs so extreme that a quantity leaves the range of the computer's floating-point numbers end with a one-line message naming that quantity. The driver makes the same checks for callers from Python.

Plot

The plot draws one profile against the radius in metres, from the centre to the surface. --output_type chooses which: every run computes all four, so the printed summary does not depend on the choice.

--output_typeVertical axisWhat to look for
pressurePressure [Pa]Falls from \(p_c\) to zero, steeply in the inner part of the star; on a linear axis it looks like zero over the outer half (Beat 0).
densityDensity [kg/m³]Falls from \(\rho_c\) to zero as \(p^{1/\gamma}\); best seen with --log_y (Beat 1).
temperatureTemperature [K]Falls from \(T_c\) to zero as \(p^{1-1/\gamma}\), more gently than the pressure (Beat 2).
massEnclosed mass [kg]Rises from zero to the mass of the star, and is almost flat over the outer quarter of the radius (Beat 3).

With --log_y the vertical axis is logarithmic and the surface point is left out, because its value is zero. The plot function returns the figure and axes, so that a script can save the plot with figure.savefig().

Suggested Experiments

The experiments are ranked from introductory to advanced, and each is cited by the beat it extends. Each command is run from the program's folder.

1 · The Four Profiles — Introductory

Plot all four profiles of the default star. Find where the pressure, the density and the temperature have fallen to half their central values, and where the mass has reached half the total. Which of the four halves is reached first, and why?

python main.py --output_type pressure
python main.py --output_type density --log_y
python main.py --output_type temperature
python main.py --output_type mass

2 · A Hotter Centre, a Cooler Centre — Introductory to Intermediate

Change \(T_c\) by 10% either way at the same central pressure. Check that the radius changes in proportion to \(T_c\) and the mass in proportion to \(T_c^2\), Eq. (10), and that the checkpoint temperatures change in proportion to \(T_c\) while the checkpoint pressures do not change.

python main.py --T_c 2.0367e7
python main.py --T_c 2.263e7
python main.py --T_c 2.4893e7

3 · Four Times the Central Pressure — Intermediate

Multiply and divide the central pressure by four at the same central temperature. By Eq. (10) both the radius and the mass should change by a factor of two. Why does a higher central pressure at the same temperature make a smaller star?

python main.py --p_c 1.7895e15
python main.py --p_c 7.158e15
python main.py --p_c 2.8632e16

4 · The Mean Particle Mass — Intermediate

Change \(\mu\), the mean mass of a gas particle in units of the proton mass, at the same central pressure and temperature. Check that the radius scales as \(\mu^{-1}\) and the mass as \(\mu^{-2}\). A fully ionised gas of hydrogen has \(\mu=0.5\) and one of helium \(\mu=4/3\); why does a star with heavier particles come out smaller at the same central conditions?

python main.py --mu 0.6
python main.py --mu 1.0
python main.py --mu 1.285

5 · Restore the Mass — Intermediate

Make the centre 10% hotter, then find by trial the central pressure that brings the mass back to that of the default star, 1.9825e+30 kg. Use Eq. (10) to predict the pressure first. Is the radius then back to its default value too? What does this say about how much a measured mass alone tells us about a star?

python main.py --T_c 2.4893e7 --p_c 7.158e15
python main.py --T_c 2.4893e7 --p_c 1.048e16

6 · How Fine Is Fine Enough? — Intermediate to Advanced

Halve the step three times, allowing enough points each time to avoid a restart, and tabulate the relative differences from the Lane–Emden solution. Check that each halving roughly halves the error, Beat 6. Then take \(\gamma=1.21\), where the default run restarted four times and missed the radius by a quarter (Beat 8), and find how fine a run has to be to bring the error below 1%.

python main.py --steps_per_scale 200
python main.py --steps_per_scale 400
python main.py --steps_per_scale 800 --max_points 4000
python main.py --steps_per_scale 1600 --max_points 8000
python main.py --gamma 1.21 --steps_per_scale 1000 --max_points 100000

7 · The Shape of a Polytrope — Intermediate to Advanced

Run the default star with \(\gamma\) = 4, 2, 5/3, 1.36, 4/3 and 1.25, and read the index \(n\) and the radius and mass of the Lane–Emden solution from each. For each, compute the ratio of central to mean density, \(\rho_c/\bar\rho\), from the integrated radius and mass with Eq. (8) and from the Lane–Emden values with Eq. (8) again. Check that Restarts (radial step doubled) is 0 in each run; \(\gamma=1.25\) needs more than the default 2000 points. How does the concentration of the star change with \(n\), and how well does the default step reproduce it?

python main.py --gamma 4
python main.py --gamma 2
python main.py --gamma 1.6666667
python main.py --gamma 1.36
python main.py --gamma 1.3333333
python main.py --gamma 1.25 --max_points 4000

8 · Two Kinds of Family — Advanced

The families of Experiments 2 to 4 keep \(\gamma\) but not the constant \(K\) of Eq. (5). Build a family with one fixed equation of state instead: keep \(\mu\) and \(\gamma\), and set \(T_c=T_{c,0}(p_c/p_{c,0})^{1-1/\gamma}\) so that \(D\), and hence \(K\), does not change. For this family the Lane–Emden solution gives \(R\propto p_c^{(\gamma-2)/(2\gamma)}\) and \(M\propto p_c^{(3\gamma-4)/(2\gamma)}\). Check the exponents with the three commands below, and explain why the mass does not depend on the central pressure at all when \(\gamma=4/3\).

python main.py --p_c 1.7895e15 --T_c 1.5678905e7
python main.py --p_c 7.158e15 --T_c 2.263e7
python main.py --p_c 2.8632e16 --T_c 3.2662797e7

9 · A Schematic Red Giant — Advanced

Take \(\gamma=1.335\) and look for central values that give about the default mass with a radius about a hundred times larger; then try for ten times the mass. A real red giant has a dense core and a vast, thin envelope with different equations of state, so this is a sketch of the size of such a star, not a model of one.

python main.py --gamma 1.335 --p_c 7.158e7 --T_c 2.263e5
python main.py --gamma 1.335 --p_c 7.158e9 --T_c 2.263e6

10 · A Newtonian Neutron Star — Advanced

With \(\gamma=2\) and \(\mu=1\), look for central values that give a radius of about 10 km and a mass of about 1.4 solar masses, 2.8e+30 kg. The central pressure and the formal temperature come out enormous; here they only normalise the polytrope, since neutron-star matter is not an ideal gas and gravity this strong needs general relativity. Compare the result with the Neutron program.

python main.py --mu 1 --gamma 2 --p_c 1.9888e34 --T_c 1.1188e12 --output_type mass

11 · More Than One Polytrope — Advanced Programming

In a separate copy of the program, divide the star into two or three regions in radius with different values of \(\gamma\), for example a stiffer core and a softer envelope. At each boundary keep the pressure continuous, and choose the new constant \(D\) so that the density is continuous too, unless a jump is intended. Compare the radius, the mass and the profiles with those of one polytrope. Command-line options alone cannot do this; it needs changes to driver_star.py.

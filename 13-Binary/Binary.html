Overview

Binary simulates two point masses interacting only through Newtonian gravity. The masses, initial positions, and initial velocities are all editable, so the same program can represent bound binaries, extreme mass-ratio systems, and unbound gravitational encounters.

The program integrates the coupled equations of motion with an Euler predictor and an iterated trapezoidal corrector. It records both bodies' positions and velocities together with the system's kinetic, gravitational potential, and total mechanical energy.

By default the calculation stops after the relative position vector has completed one revolution. Set stop_after_one_orbit = False when you want a long integration for energy-conservation tests or when studying an unbound orbit. In all cases max_steps remains a safety ceiling.

Scope: Binary is a Newtonian two-body program. Multiple extends the Newtonian calculation to many gravitating bodies, while RelativisticOrbit treats relativistic orbital motion later in the book.

Quick Start

Requirements

Python 3.10 or later with matplotlib:

pip install matplotlib

Running the Program

Clone or download the repository, then:

cd GFTGU-Programs/Binary
python main.py

The default configuration uses two equal-mass bodies, \(M_A = M_B = 2\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}\) (approximately one solar mass each), placed symmetrically at \(x = \pm\,4.6\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m}\) with equal and opposite transverse velocities of \(\pm\,1.3\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). This produces a visibly eccentric elliptical orbit.

Choosing Output

Edit the output_type variable near the bottom of main.py to select which plot is displayed. See Output Types for the complete list of accepted string values.

Program Files

main.py
Entry Point & Configuration

Defines every initial condition and simulation parameter — masses, starting positions, starting velocities, the maximum timestep dt, the safety limit max_steps, the tolerance thresholds eps1 and eps2, and stop_after_one_orbit. After calling integrate_binary() to run the simulation it passes the result to plot_binary() for display. All user-adjustable settings live here; no other file needs editing for typical use.

physics_binary.py
Physics Engine

Defines the BinaryState dataclass, which stores the current time together with the positions \((x_A, y_A,\, x_B, y_B)\) and velocities \((v_A, u_A,\, v_B, u_B)\) of both bodies. Provides three functions: relative_displacement() returns the separation vector \((\Delta x,\,\Delta y)\) and scalar distance \(r\); accelerations() evaluates the Newtonian gravitational acceleration on each body; and energies() computes the instantaneous kinetic, gravitational potential, and total mechanical energy of the system.

driver_binary.py
Integration Driver

Defines the BinaryResult dataclass (time-series lists of positions, velocities, and energies for both bodies) and the function integrate_binary(). The driver advances the system step by step using the Euler predictor followed by the averaged-acceleration corrector, with vector-based convergence testing and adaptive timestep control. It can stop after one relative revolution or continue to the max_steps safety ceiling. All numerical-method logic lives in this module.

plot_binary.py
Visualisation

Provides the plot_binary() function and the OutputType string literal type. Accepts a BinaryResult and renders one of seven plots via matplotlib: orbital trajectories in the \(xy\)-plane (equal aspect ratio), velocity-space diagrams, position vs. time, or velocity vs. time for each body individually, and the full energy budget vs. time. All plots carry axis labels, titles, and legends.

Two-Body Problem

Two point masses \(M_A\) and \(M_B\) interact solely through Newtonian gravitational attraction:

(1) \[ F=\frac{GM_AM_B}{r^2}, \qquad r=\sqrt{(x_A-x_B)^2+(y_A-y_B)^2}. \]

The program uses \(G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\, \mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\). Following the notation used in the program, \(v=\dot x\) and \(u=\dot y\).

Centre of Mass

(2) \[ \mathbf R_{\rm CM}= \frac{M_A\mathbf r_A+M_B\mathbf r_B}{M_A+M_B}, \qquad \mathbf V_{\rm CM}= \frac{M_A\mathbf v_A+M_B\mathbf v_B}{M_A+M_B}. \]

Because the system is isolated, \(\mathbf V_{\rm CM}\) is constant. With the default equal masses and equal-and-opposite velocities it is zero, so the centre of mass remains at the origin. Equal masses alone do not require \(\mathbf v_A=-\mathbf v_B\); that relation additionally requires zero centre-of-mass velocity.

Only the relative separation controls the mutual force. Translating both positions by the same vector therefore leaves the relative orbit unchanged, and adding the same constant velocity to both bodies produces a Galilean boost without changing their motion relative to one another.

Equations of Motion

Applying Newton's second law component by component, with the force directed along the line joining the two bodies, yields four coupled second-order ODEs. Define the displacement of A relative to B: \(\Delta x = x_A - x_B\), \(\Delta y = y_A - y_B\).

Body A

(3) \[ \ddot x_A \;=\; -\frac{G\,M_B\,\Delta x}{r^3}, \qquad \ddot y_A \;=\; -\frac{G\,M_B\,\Delta y}{r^3} \]

Body B

(4) \[ \ddot x_B \;=\; +\frac{G\,M_A\,\Delta x}{r^3}, \qquad \ddot y_B \;=\; +\frac{G\,M_A\,\Delta y}{r^3} \]

The sign flip between bodies reflects Newton's third law: the force on B is equal and opposite to the force on A. These accelerations are computed by accelerations() in physics_binary.py and evaluated at every stage of the predictor–corrector loop.

The complete system state at any instant is the eight-component vector \((x_A,\,y_A,\,v_A,\,u_A,\,x_B,\,y_B,\,v_B,\,u_B)\), stored in the BinaryState dataclass and advanced one accepted timestep at a time by integrate_binary().

Energy Conservation

The total mechanical energy is the sum of the kinetic energies of both bodies and their mutual gravitational potential energy.

Kinetic Energy

(5) \[ K \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,M_A\!\left(v_A^2 + u_A^2\right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,M_B\!\left(v_B^2 + u_B^2\right) \]

Gravitational Potential Energy

(6) \[ U \;=\; -\frac{G\,M_A\,M_B}{r} \]

Total Mechanical Energy

(7) \[ E \;=\; K + U \]

In the centre-of-mass frame, a gravitationally bound orbit has \(E < 0\), and the total mechanical energy is conserved. Any systematic drift in \(E\) over the run is a direct measure of accumulated numerical error. The energy vs. time plot is therefore the primary diagnostic for assessing integration quality.

The plotted energy uses the velocities in the chosen inertial frame and therefore includes any kinetic energy of the centre of mass. When using the sign of \(E\) to classify the internal orbit as bound or unbound, keep the centre of mass at rest or subtract its translational kinetic energy.

Tip: If the total energy shows a noticeable drift, reducing dt is usually the most effective remedy. Tightening eps1 or eps2 can also make the step acceptance and corrector tests more demanding, but the improvement is problem-dependent. The safest test is to repeat the same physical run with stricter numerical controls and check that the result changes less and less.

Termination Condition

With stop_after_one_orbit = True, the program follows the angle swept by the relative vector \(\mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B\). Signed angle increments between successive accepted steps are accumulated, and the calculation stops when their magnitude reaches \(2\pi\), provided this occurs before max_steps. This is a geometric stopping rule for one relative revolution; it is independent of the coordinate origin and does not by itself prove that the numerical trajectory is exactly closed.

Set stop_after_one_orbit = False to continue for the full max_steps accepted steps. This is useful for unbound trajectories and long-term energy-conservation tests.

Safety limit: max_steps is always enforced. An unbound orbit will generally never satisfy the one-revolution condition.

Predictor–Corrector

Each accepted step begins with an explicit Euler predictor. The program then evaluates gravity at the predicted positions and, if the timestep passes the accuracy gate described below, refines the result using an iterated trapezoidal corrector.

Euler Predictor

(8) \[ \tilde{\mathbf r}_A=\mathbf r_A^n+\mathbf v_A^n\Delta t, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf v}_A=\mathbf v_A^n+\mathbf a_A^n\Delta t, \]

with equivalent equations for body B.

Iterated Trapezoidal Corrector

(9) \[ \mathbf v_A^{n+1} =\mathbf v_A^n+ \frac{\mathbf a_A^n+\mathbf a_A^{n+1}}{2}\Delta t, \] \[ \mathbf r_A^{n+1} =\mathbf r_A^n+ \frac{\mathbf v_A^n+\mathbf v_A^{n+1}}{2}\Delta t. \]

The end-of-step acceleration is recomputed from the refined positions and the corrector is iterated. Convergence is tested from the dimensionless relative change of each body's velocity vector, rather than from individual Cartesian components.

If ten corrector iterations are insufficient to meet eps2, the timestep is halved and the same step is retried instead of silently accepting a non-converged result. eps2 is a convergence test within one trial step; it is not a direct tolerance on the global position, period, or energy error.

Adaptive Timestep

Before the corrector is run, the program compares body A's acceleration at the beginning of the step with the acceleration at the Euler-predicted position. Since the two bodies' accelerations are proportional in an isolated two-body system, their fractional changes are identical and testing one body is sufficient.

(10) \[ \Delta_a= \frac{\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}_A-\mathbf a_A^n\right\rVert} {\max\!\left(\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf a}_A\right\rVert, \left\lVert\mathbf a_A^n\right\rVert\right)}. \]

If \(\Delta_a>\varepsilon_1\), the attempt is rejected and the working timestep is halved. After an accepted step, the working timestep is allowed to grow by 10% toward the user-specified ceiling dt. If repeated halving would drive the working timestep below a tiny numerical safety floor, the program stops with a clear error instead of continuing with effectively zero time advance.

Interpretation: For sufficiently eccentric or rapidly varying trajectories this mechanism normally uses smaller steps near periapsis. With the current default parameters, no timestep reduction is needed; increasing the eccentricity or choosing a larger dt makes the controller easier to observe.

Convergence Criteria

Two independent dimensionless tolerances control the integration.

ParameterDefaultRole
eps1\(0.05\) Rejects a predictor step when the relative change in the acceleration vector is too large.
eps2\(10^{-4}\) Requires successive corrected velocity vectors to agree to this relative tolerance. Failure to converge after ten iterations causes the timestep to be halved and retried.

Tighter tolerances make the local acceptance and convergence tests more demanding and can require more computation. Once the corrector is already converging reliably, reducing dt often has a much larger effect on the global error than tightening eps2. There is no universal factor relating a tolerance change to an energy-error change, so accuracy should be established by a convergence test.

Suggested Experiments

1 · Circular and Elliptical Equal-Mass Binaries

Run the defaults first. For equal masses \(M\) at \(x=\pm a\), each body in a circular orbit has radius \(a\) while the gravitational separation is \(2a\), giving \(v_{\rm circ}=\sqrt{GM/(4a)}\). With the default values this is about \(2.69\times10^4\) m/s. Try \(u_A=+2.69\times10^4\) m/s and \(u_B=-2.69\times10^4\) m/s, then try intermediate speeds and watch the eccentricity change.

2 · Translation Invariance

Add the same constant displacement to both bodies. Their coordinate trajectories move to a different part of the plane, but the relative orbit is unchanged.

3 · Galilean Relativity

Add the same velocity vector to both bodies. The binary now drifts across the plot, but its internal orbit is unchanged. Compare the new "velocity space" plot with the default one: both hodographs have the same shape and are simply translated by the added velocity. If you also compare energy plots, remember that the kinetic energy includes the motion of the centre of mass.

4 · Unequal Masses and the Centre of Mass

Make body B progressively less massive. To keep the centre of mass at rest, choose initial velocities satisfying \(M_A\mathbf v_A+M_B\mathbf v_B=0\). A convenient method is to choose the lighter body's velocity first and then set the heavier body's velocity to balance its momentum component by component. Observe that the more massive body makes the smaller orbit about the common centre of mass. In the extreme mass-ratio limit the result approaches the fixed-central-mass picture used in Orbit.

5 · Bound, Parabolic, and Unbound Motion

Keep the centre of mass at rest while increasing the relative speed from the same starting positions. Plot "energy vs time". Under this zero-centre-of-mass condition, verify that bound elliptical motion has \(E<0\), the marginal parabolic case has \(E\approx0\), and hyperbolic motion has \(E>0\). Set stop_after_one_orbit = False for unbound runs.

6 · Challenge: Stellar Reflex Motion — Sun and Planets

Use approximate circular-orbit data for the Sun and one planet, choosing opposite transverse velocities so that the centre of mass is at rest. Start with Jupiter and examine the Sun's small barycentric orbit; its radius should be slightly larger than the Sun's own radius. Repeat with Saturn and then Earth. Which planet produces the largest reflex motion of the Sun, and why?

7 · Numerical Convergence and Energy Conservation

Set stop_after_one_orbit = False and integrate for several periods. Repeat the same physical initial conditions with smaller dt and tighter eps1/eps2. Compare the total-energy curves, for example by estimating the largest fractional deviation \(\max|E(t)-E(0)|/|E(0)|\). A trustworthy result should change less and less as the numerical controls are tightened.

Output Types

Assign one of these strings to output_type in main.py:

  • "orbits"Both trajectories in the \(xy\)-plane, with equal axis scales.
  • "velocity space" The x-component of velocity is plotted horizontally and the y-component vertically, showing how each velocity vector changes during the motion. In inverse-square Kepler motion, the relative-motion hodograph is a circle for a complete bound orbit; the individual bodies have the same form, scaled according to the masses and shifted by any centre-of-mass velocity. With the default equal masses and zero centre-of-mass velocity, the two velocity vectors are negatives of one another at every instant.
  • "position vs. time, body A"\(x_A(t)\) and \(y_A(t)\).
  • "position vs. time, body B"\(x_B(t)\) and \(y_B(t)\).
  • "velocity vs. time, body A"\(v_A(t)\) and \(u_A(t)\).
  • "velocity vs. time, body B"\(v_B(t)\) and \(u_B(t)\).
  • "energy vs time"System kinetic \(K\), potential \(U\), and total \(E\); the principal numerical-accuracy diagnostic.

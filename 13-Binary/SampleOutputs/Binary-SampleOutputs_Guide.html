What the program calculates

Binary integrates the Newtonian motion of two mutually gravitating point masses. Its editable masses, positions, velocities, timestep controls, and termination rule support circular and elliptical binaries, unequal-mass systems, moving centre-of-mass frames, and unbound encounters.

The program records both bodies’ positions and velocities as well as their kinetic, gravitational-potential, and total mechanical energies. The supplied plotting module provides seven distinct designs through eight accepted selector strings.

Figure 1 — Default eccentric equal-mass binary

MA = MB = 2.0e30
xInitA, xInitB = +4.6e10, -4.6e10
uInitA, uInitB = +13000.0, -13000.0
dt = 2000
stop_after_one_orbit = True
output_type = "orbits"

The default run begins with the bodies on opposite sides of their common centre of mass and with equal-and-opposite transverse velocities. Their equal masses produce mirror-image trajectories.

What it shows

  • The relative separation changes from 92.000 to 12.127 billion metres, making the orbital eccentricity visually obvious.
  • Using the sampled apoapsis and periapsis separations gives an eccentricity estimate of 0.767.
  • The geometric one-revolution rule stopped the calculation after 2,284 accepted steps and 52.87 days.

# Same simulation as Figure 1
output_type = "velocity space"

Each point gives one body’s x- and y-velocity components. These curves are hodographs, not trajectories in physical space.

What it shows

  • The complete bound-orbit hodographs are circular even though Figure 1’s spatial paths are eccentric ellipses.
  • The equal masses and zero centre-of-mass velocity make the two velocity curves equal in size and opposite in direction.
  • Adding the same constant velocity to both bodies would translate both hodographs without changing their shapes or the relative orbit.

The positions and velocities were divided in inverse proportion to the masses, placing the centre of mass at rest at the origin and giving the bodies the circular relative speed.

What it shows

  • Body A is twice as massive as body B, so its orbital radius is half as large: approximately 30.667 versus 61.333 billion metres.
  • The relative circular speed is 46.652 km/s, divided between the bodies so that their total momentum is zero.
  • The calculation completed one relative revolution in 143.43 days with a maximum sampled fractional energy drift of 4.916e-09.

Notes for evaluation

  • Output range: selectors cover physical-space orbits, velocity space, position versus time for either body, velocity versus time for either body, and the system’s energy budget.
  • Numerical method: an Euler predictor is followed by an iterated trapezoidal corrector. A working timestep can be reduced when the predicted acceleration changes too much or the corrector does not converge.
  • Termination: the default stops after the relative position vector accumulates one revolution. Setting stop_after_one_orbit = False permits long-term tests and unbound encounters, with max_steps retained as a safety limit.
  • Frame dependence: spatial translation and a common velocity boost leave the relative orbit unchanged, while the plotted coordinate trajectories and inertial-frame kinetic energy can change.
  • Build verification: the executable and documentation both identify version 1.1.4, build 83cbf117fcdb. In accordance with the preparation instructions, no regression suite was run.

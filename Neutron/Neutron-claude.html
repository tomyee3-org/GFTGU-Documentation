Overview

Neutron computes static, spherically symmetric stellar equilibria in general relativity. It integrates the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) pressure equation and the enclosed-mass equation outward from the center until the pressure reaches zero, using one simple polytropic equation of state, \(p=K\rho^\gamma\). It is introduced in Investigation 20.1 of Schutz's text, where it serves as the reader's first relativistic stellar-structure calculation.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: a star is a battle between gravity, which pulls inward, and pressure, which pushes outward — and in general relativity pressure itself adds to the gravity it is resisting, so the same battle needs a different equation than it does in Newton's theory. Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument, a command whose printed summary illustrates it, and then the equation that the printed numbers follow from.

By varying the central pressure and the equation-of-state parameters, students can construct equilibrium sequences and locate a maximum gravitational mass. The program does not evolve a star in time, so it does not simulate collapse; it computes static equilibrium configurations only.

What this program is, and what it does not model. Neutron is a teaching model of a static, non-rotating, spherically symmetric star built from one pedagogical polytropic equation of state. It has no crust-plus-core or tabulated nuclear-matter equation of state, no rotation, no magnetic field, no finite temperature, no baryonic-mass or binding-energy bookkeeping, and no time evolution: it cannot simulate collapse, a merger, or oscillation modes. Use it to see how the TOV equation differs from Newtonian hydrostatics and how a relativistic stellar-structure solver locates a surface it is not allowed to overshoot. Beats 6 and 7 collect the limits.

The equations on this page are rendered by MathJax, loaded from a public CDN, so an internet connection is needed to see them typeset; with no connection, the raw \(\LaTeX\) source (e.g. \(a(t)\)) will show as plain text instead of a formula. How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation that the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equations at work, not independent evidence that they are right. The outside checks this page offers are an independent fine-grid reference integration (Beat 5) and the weak-field limit in which the relativistic and Newtonian equations must agree (Beat 1).

Every beat uses the same program and, unless a run stops with an error (Beat 7), the same two-part printed summary: nine global properties, then a five-row table of radial checkpoints at 0, ¼, ½, ¾ and 0.90 of the surface radius. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Run every command from the program's folder. Radii are printed in kilometres, masses in solar masses, pressures in pascals and densities in kg/m³, and the global summary lines use five to seven significant figures (see Printed Summary).

What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag. A green ODE tag marks a differential equation the program integrates; the program adopts it and does not derive it. A grey DEFINITION tag marks a quantity defined by a formula, such as the numerical scale height. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows from equations already stated. A violet ALGORITHM tag marks a rule of the numerical method — a choice made by the program, not a law of physics. An orange CLOSURE tag marks a relation that is physically motivated but chosen for teaching purposes rather than derived from the TOV equations themselves — the polytropic equation of state is this page's only example. The tags are there so that a physical assumption, a definition, a numerical rule and a closure choice are never mistaken for one another. Eight equations are numbered and carry one of these five tags; one unnumbered comparison equation appears in Beat 1 and carries none of them, since the program never integrates it and it is not offered as a definition, a derivation or an algorithm — it exists only so the weak-field check in Beat 1 has something to compare against.

A star is nothing but a stand-off between gravity, pulling inward, and pressure, pushing outward. Ask the program to referee that stand-off for one central pressure and one simple equation of state, and read what balance it finds.

Three tasks, in order. First, put the global numbers in perspective. The star is 7.804 km in radius and 0.9626 solar masses — a solar mass of matter packed into a sphere about the size of a city. Its central density, 6.633451×1018 kg/m³, is about 6.63×1015 times the density of water. Its compactness, \(2GM/(Rc^2)=0.364\), is roughly 86,000 times the Sun's own compactness of about \(4.25\times10^{-6}\): the Sun is a diffuse gas cloud by comparison, and this star is a genuinely relativistic object. Both the causality and Buchdahl checks (Beat 6) say satisfied for this default model, meaning its central sound speed stays below \(c\) and its compactness stays below Buchdahl's geometric bound — the two specific limits this program tests. Passing them says nothing about whether the polytropic equation of state itself is realistic, whether the star would be dynamically stable, or what it is made of; Beats 2 and 7 return to those limits. Then look at how the checkpoint table's mass column grows: 0.0908 solar masses inside a quarter of the radius, then 0.3947 more between a quarter and a half, 0.3792 more between a half and three quarters, and only 0.0878 more in the belt from three quarters out to nine tenths. Most of the star's mass lives in its middle shells, not squeezed into the very center the way the pressure and density columns are. Then look at the plot: with no --output_type given, the default is "pressure", so the window shows pressure falling smoothly and monotonically from 1.26×1035 Pa at the center to zero, by construction, at the program's estimated surface radius of 7.804 km — the stored endpoint pressure is set to exactly zero there, but Beat 5 shows this estimated radius is close to, and not identical with, the star's true zero-pressure radius.

Then the equation this run is not using. In ordinary Newtonian gravity, a static spherical fluid in hydrostatic equilibrium obeys

Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium (for later comparison, unnumbered, untagged — the program never integrates this equation)
\[ \frac{dp}{dr} = -\frac{G\,m(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^2}. \]

This is not the equation structure_derivatives() evaluates; it is the equation a purely Newtonian code would integrate, and Beat 1 uses it as the baseline against which the program's real, relativistic pressure equation is judged. The mass side of the balance, by contrast, is exactly what the program does integrate, unchanged by relativity:

Eq. 1 — Enclosed-mass equation ODE
\[ \frac{dm}{dr} = 4\pi r^2 \rho(r). \]

Here \(m(r)\) is the mass already enclosed within radius \(r\) — more precisely, the TOV gravitational mass function, not a separately tracked baryonic or rest mass — and \(\rho\) is mass density, so the energy density is \(\rho c^2\). This equation says only that a thin shell of radius \(r\), thickness \(dr\) and density \(\rho(r)\) contributes its own volume times its own density to the running total; nothing about it depends on whether gravity is Newtonian or relativistic. structure_derivatives() in physics_neutron.py returns this alongside the true pressure derivative. What changes going from Newton to Einstein is entirely on the pressure side, which Beat 1 takes up.

Beat 1 · Why relativity: pressure gravitates too

The Newtonian equation of Beat 0 says gravity is sourced by mass density alone, and pressure only pushes back against it. General relativity says pressure does both: it still resists gravity, but it also adds to the very gravity it is resisting. Check whether that correction matters by comparing the two equations directly, first somewhere gravity is weak.

import math
import physics_neutron as phys

r, p, m, K, gamma = 1.0e6, 1.0, 1.0e20, 1.0e-12, 2.0
rho = phys.eos_density(p, K, gamma)
newtonian = -phys.G * rho * m / r**2
relativistic, _ = phys.structure_derivatives(r, p, m, K, gamma)

compactness = 2 * phys.G * m / (r * phys.C2)
pressure_energy_ratio = p / (rho * phys.C2)
pressure_source_ratio = 4 * math.pi * r**3 * p / (m * phys.C2)

print('Newtonian dp/dr:', newtonian)
print('TOV dp/dr      :', relativistic)
print('relative diff  :', abs(relativistic / newtonian - 1))
print('compactness            2Gm/(rc^2)        :', compactness)
print('pressure/energy ratio  p/(rho c^2)       :', pressure_energy_ratio)
print('pressure source ratio  4pi r^3 p/(m c^2) :', pressure_source_ratio)

Save this as weak_field_check.py beside the program's four modules and run it with python weak_field_check.py. (A single-line command is easier to reuse across shells than a multi-line python -c string, which some shells — including Windows' cmd.exe — do not accept pasted as several physical lines.)

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the two derivatives: Newtonian dp/dr is −6674.3, and TOV dp/dr is −6674.300000000989 — the same to nine significant figures, with a relative difference of 1.4855×10−13. Then look at the three printed factors that make up that difference, computed separately: the compactness 2Gm/(rc²)=1.4852×10−13 accounts for nearly all of it by itself, while the pressure/energy-density ratio p/(ρc²)=1.1127×10−23 and the pressure-source ratio 4πr³p/(mc²)=1.3982×10−18 are ten and five orders of magnitude smaller still — but that ranking belongs only to this one hand-picked, deliberately low-pressure state, not to this program's stars in general. Then check the same three factors inside the actual default toy-star model of Beat 0, where the ranking reverses close to the center: at its first stored point off-center (r=16.375 m), compactness is a mere 1.107×10−5, while the pressure/energy ratio is already 0.2113 and the pressure-source ratio 0.6340 — both far larger. A hundred samples further out (r≈1637.5 m), compactness has grown to 0.10076, but the pressure-source ratio, 0.53470, is still the larger of the two. Close to the center, then, it is routinely the pressure corrections, not the compactness factor, that dominate; that reversal, not the toy state's own numbers, is the actual demonstration behind this beat's title, "pressure gravitates too". It does not last: compactness overtakes the pressure/energy ratio near r/R≈0.287 and the pressure-source ratio near r/R≈0.420 (at r/R≈0.504 the three read 0.3700, 0.1091 and 0.2092), and from there outward compactness is the largest of the three, staying near its surface value of 0.3643 while both pressure corrections fall toward zero along with the pressure itself. The correction that matters most is therefore a matter of where in the star you look, not a single fixed ranking. It might seem that the classical equation becomes exact right at the center, since compactness itself goes to zero there — but it does not. Near \(r=0\), \(m(r)\) and \(p(r)\) both follow the central expansion of Eq. 4 below, and the ratio of the TOV to the Newtonian pressure gradient tends to a fixed limit, \((1+\eta)(1+3\eta)\), where \(\eta=p_C/(\rho_Cc^2)\) is the central pressure-to-rest-energy ratio (about 0.2113 for the default model) — not to 1. Evaluating the central-state series directly at \(r_0\) from 16.375 m down to 10−6 m confirms the ratio holds fixed at 1.979374, matching \((1.2113)(1.6340)\approx1.979\) to six figures, while compactness there falls from 1.107×10−5 to 4.13×10−20. So the classical equation is off by nearly a factor of two arbitrarily close to the center of this particular model, not just out in its middle shells: Beat 0's weak-field, low-pressure limit needs compactness and \(\eta\) both small together, and this star's high central pressure keeps \(\eta\) of order one everywhere, however small its compactness gets.

Then the real equation, in full. With \(\rho\) again denoting mass density so that the energy density is \(\rho c^2\),

Eq. 2 — Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff pressure equation ODE
\[ \frac{dp}{dr} = -\frac{G\left(\rho+\dfrac{p}{c^2}\right) \left(m+\dfrac{4\pi r^3p}{c^2}\right)} {r\left(r-\dfrac{2Gm}{c^2}\right)}. \]

Set every explicit \(p/c^2\) term to zero and the numerator becomes exactly the Newtonian source \(-G\rho m\); the denominator, though, still carries \(r(r-2Gm/c^2)\) rather than plain \(r^2\), so fully recovering the Newtonian equation of Beat 0 also needs the compactness \(2Gm/(rc^2)\) to vanish. The weak-field check above tested both limits at once, not only the explicit pressure terms, which is why its one printed relative difference tracked the compactness factor rather than the pressure factors. Three relativistic corrections are visible in Eq. 2, and none of them is optional once compactness is not tiny. The factor \(\rho+p/c^2\) is the inertial mass-energy density, larger than \(\rho\) alone because pressure itself carries energy. The factor \(m+4\pi r^3p/c^2\) is the active gravitational source enclosed within \(r\): pressure inside the star adds to the mass that gravitates, which is the "pressure gravitates too" of this beat's title. The denominator's compactness factor \(1-2Gm/(rc^2)\) is what a purely Newtonian equation has no room for at all. Which of the three corrections is largest is not fixed: the weak-field check above chose a state where compactness dominates by design, but the same check repeated inside the default toy-star model's own interior (Beat 1) shows the two pressure corrections running larger close to the center, with compactness overtaking each of them in turn (near r/R≈0.287 and r/R≈0.420) and staying the largest of the three from there out to the surface, where the pressure corrections have fallen toward zero along with the pressure itself while compactness holds near its surface value of 0.3643. structure_derivatives() raises a RuntimeError if the compactness factor is ever driven to zero or below, because the coordinates the program uses cannot describe a horizon. \(K\) and \(\gamma\) enter only through \(\rho\), which Beat 2 defines.

Beat 2 · The equation of state

Eqs. 1 and 2 relate \(p\), \(m\) and \(\rho\), but they are two equations for three unknown functions of \(r\). Closing the system takes one more relation between pressure and density — the equation of state — and Neutron uses the simplest one with the right qualitative behavior. See what happens when that relation is allowed to degenerate.

python main.py --gamma 1

Look at what the program prints instead of a model summary.

Three tasks, in order. First, the rejection itself: the program exits immediately with Neutron: error: --gamma must be greater than 1 and never reaches compute_neutron_star(). Then call the underlying solver directly from Python with the same exponent, compute_neutron_star(1.0, 1.26e35, 5.3802e3), and the message changes to gamma must be finite and greater than 1. — the same restriction enforced a second time, independently, inside the physics layer rather than only in the command-line parser. Then ask why: with the pure choice \(\gamma=1\), pressure and density in Eq. 3 below are proportional, so a star built from it has no finite radius at all — pressure only approaches zero asymptotically as \(r\to\infty\) — and the surface-locating algorithm of Beat 5 divides by \(\gamma-1\), which would be a division by zero. The restriction \(\gamma>1\) protects the model's physical and numerical definition; it is not merely limiting how far a student may push the exponent.

Then the equation of state itself, and its inverse for recovering density from pressure:

Eq. 3 — Polytropic equation of state CLOSURE
\[ p = K\rho^\gamma, \qquad \rho = \left(\frac{p}{K}\right)^{1/\gamma}. \]

eos_density() in physics_neutron.py evaluates the right-hand inverse, in logarithmic form to avoid an avoidable overflow when \(p\) and \(K\) have very different magnitudes. The default \(\gamma\approx5/3\) has the same exponent as an ideal non-relativistic degenerate Fermi gas, and \(K=5.3802\times10^3\) (SI units that themselves depend on \(\gamma\)) is chosen to give a plausible central density; at the extreme densities reached inside a real neutron star, actual matter needs relativistic, strong-interaction nuclear physics that this one-parameter polytrope does not attempt. Because the dimensions of \(K\) depend on \(\gamma\), the two cannot be varied independently in a controlled comparison: to compare two exponents at the same central state, compute \(K=p_C/\rho_C^{\gamma}\) separately for each one, which is exactly what Experiment 6 does.

Beat 3 · Starting from the center

Eq. 2 has \(r\) in its denominator twice, so it cannot be evaluated exactly at \(r=0\), even though the physical star is perfectly regular there. The solver starts a small distance out instead, using a hand-derived series that is accurate near the center. See how much that starting point matters.

from physics_neutron import central_state

for r0 in (16.375, 1.0):
    p0, rho0, m0 = central_state(1.26e35, 5.3802e3, 1.666667, r0)
    print(f'r0={r0:8.3f} m   p0={p0:.10e} Pa   rho0={rho0:.10e} kg/m^3   m0={m0:.10e} kg')

Save this as central_state_check.py beside the program's four modules and run it with python central_state_check.py. Compare the two printed rows, and compare both with the central pressure and density printed by Beat 0.

Three tasks, in order. First, the default run's own starting radius: with steps_per_scale=400, the nominal step for the default model is 16.375 m (Beat 0's last printed line), so that is the first row's \(r_0\). Its pressure, 1.2599673537×1035 Pa, is already extremely close to the true central pressure of 1.26×1035 Pa — different in only the fifth significant figure. Then the second row, computed at \(r_0=1\) m, which is 16.375 times closer to the center (16.375 m / 1 m = 16.375): its pressure, 1.2599998782×1035 Pa, differs from the true center by less than one part in 106, confirming that the series is a genuine local approximation that improves as \(r_0\) shrinks, not an arbitrary fudge. Then the enclosed mass column: \(m_0\) is 1.22×1023 kg at 16.375 m but only 2.78×1019 kg at 1 m — four orders of magnitude smaller, because \(m_0\propto r_0^3\) and \(16.375^3\approx4390\). A smaller starting radius is a better pressure approximation but an even smaller, and therefore even safer to neglect, starting mass.

Then the series itself. Near \(r=0\), the enclosed mass grows as the density times the volume of a small sphere, and substituting that leading mass into Eq. 2 gives a pressure correction quadratic in \(r\):

Eq. 4 — Regular central expansion DERIVED
\[ m(r_0) \simeq \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_C r_0^{3}, \qquad p(r_0) \simeq p_C - 2\pi G\left(\rho_C+\frac{p_C}{c^2}\right) \left(\frac{\rho_C}{3}+\frac{p_C}{c^2}\right) r_0^{2}. \]

central_state() evaluates exactly these two formulas, then converts the resulting \(p(r_0)\) back to a density with Eq. 3, giving the triple \((p_0,\rho_0,m_0)\) that seeds the outward march of Beat 4. If \(r_0\) is chosen so large that \(p(r_0)\) would already be zero or negative, central_state() raises ValueError: The initial radial step is too large rather than silently starting the integration below the surface; this is what a very coarse steps_per_scale risks.

Beat 4 · Marching outward: RK4 and the scale height

From the central state of Beat 3, the solver takes one numerical step at a time, each one calling structure_derivatives() (Eq. 2) at several trial points and combining them. Refine the step and see the model settle down.

python main.py --steps_per_scale 200 --output_type mass
python main.py --steps_per_scale 400 --output_type mass
python main.py --steps_per_scale 800 --output_type mass
python main.py --steps_per_scale 1600 --output_type mass

Compare the printed surface radius and total mass across the four runs: at this console precision they read 7.804, 7.804, 7.804 and 7.803 km, and 0.962591 solar masses all four times. That rounding (the summary prints surface_radius_km to three decimals and total_mass_solar to six) hides exactly the refinement this beat is about, so see it with the full-precision values the program actually computed:

from driver_neutron import compute_neutron_star

for n in (200, 400, 800, 1600):
    d = compute_neutron_star(1.666667, 1.26e35, 5.3802e3, steps_per_scale=n)
    print(f'steps_per_scale={n:5d}  R={d["surface_radius_km"]:.6f} km  '
          f'M={d["total_mass_solar"]:.8f} Msun  samples={len(d["radius"])}')

Save this as convergence_check.py beside the program's four modules and run it with python convergence_check.py.

Three tasks, in order. First, the raw numbers: surface radius is 7.803970, 7.803563, 7.803543 and 7.803139 km, and total mass is 0.96259066, 0.96259065, 0.96259064 and 0.96259064 solar masses, at steps_per_scale 200, 400, 800 and 1600 — the digits the console line rounds away. The mass has already stabilized to eight figures by the default resolution; the radius keeps drifting, but only in the fourth decimal of a kilometre. Then compare both against Beat 5's independent fine-grid reference, 7802.758706219183 m: the radius error is +1.21 m, +0.80 m, +0.78 m and +0.38 m at the four resolutions, shrinking as the grid refines, while the mass's relative error shrinks even faster, from 1.6×10−8 down to 2.1×10−10. Then note what does not change: the number of stored radial samples is 240, 479, 955 and 1908, each roughly double the last, tracking steps_per_scale almost exactly as Eq. 6 below predicts.

Then the step itself. Each radial step advances the coupled pair \(\mathbf s=[p,m]\) with \(\mathbf f(r,\mathbf s)=[dp/dr,\,dm/dr]\) from Eqs. 1 and 2 using classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta:

Eq. 5 — Classical RK4 step ALGORITHM
\[ \mathbf k_1=\mathbf f(r,\mathbf s), \quad \mathbf k_2=\mathbf f\!\left(r+\tfrac{h}{2},\mathbf s+\tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf k_1\right), \quad \mathbf k_3=\mathbf f\!\left(r+\tfrac{h}{2},\mathbf s+\tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf k_2\right), \quad \mathbf k_4=\mathbf f(r+h,\mathbf s+h\mathbf k_3), \] \[ \mathbf s_{n+1} = \mathbf s_n + \frac{h}{6}\left(\mathbf k_1+2\mathbf k_2+2\mathbf k_3+\mathbf k_4\right). \]

"Fourth-order" describes two different ratios, and it matters which one is meant. A single RK4 step has local truncation error \(O(h^5)\), so halving that one step's size shrinks its own error by a factor of about 32. Accumulated over a fixed radial distance of smooth integration — more, smaller steps covering the same span — the errors accumulate and the global error over that span is \(O(h^4)\), a factor of about 16 per halving. That \(O(h^4)\) scaling is a property of the smooth interior integration itself; it is not what the steps_per_scale sweep above or Beat 5's error table actually show. Their radius errors (+1.21, +0.80, +0.78, +0.38 m at steps_per_scale 200, 400, 800, 1600) shrink by factors of only about 1.5, 1.0 and 2.1 per doubling, and their mass errors by factors of about 4.2, 4.4 and 4.2 — well short of, and less uniform than, a clean factor of 16. The surface radius and mass are not the output of smooth interior integration alone: they also depend on the surface-location estimate of Eq. 7 below, a local linear (tangent-line) extrapolation of the last computed state, and on the linear tail-mass correction that goes with it. ("Linear" describes the extrapolation formula, not its accuracy: a tangent-line estimate of where a smooth function reaches zero is itself second-order accurate in the remaining distance, which is why Beat 5's independent reference — built the same way — converges roughly fourfold, not twofold, each time its own grid is doubled.) Near the surface, production also integrates pressure directly, and pressure's behavior there differs from the smooth interior variables RK4's formal order describes. Separating exactly how much of the observed radius and mass convergence above comes from the surface estimate, the tail-mass rule, or that near-surface behavior would need its own controlled comparison, which this page does not attempt; what the numbers above do show is that the whole solver, surface treatment included, does not reproduce RK4's textbook global order end to end. rk4_step() in physics_neutron.py implements the single step of Eq. 5; taking one step of size 2 m and two of size 1 m from the same starting state agree to a relative 1.4×10−13 in pressure and 9.2×10−13 in mass, but that is floating-point roundoff, not a measurement of either error order — a 1–2 m step is far too short next to the star's roughly 6550 m scale height for its truncation error to be visible above roundoff. If any trial evaluation of \(\mathbf f\) would need a pressure below zero, rk4_step() raises the dedicated SurfaceCrossingError rather than evaluating the equation of state at a nonphysical pressure, and the driver halves \(h\) and retries.

The step size itself is set from a characteristic length, not chosen by hand:

Eq. 6 — Scale height and nominal radial spacing DEFINITION
\[ \ell = \frac{\sqrt{p_C/G}}{\rho_C}, \qquad \Delta r = \frac{\ell}{N_{\rm scale}}. \]

where \(N_{\rm scale}\) is steps_per_scale. For the default model \(\ell\approx6550\) m, so \(\Delta r\approx16.375\) m at the default \(N_{\rm scale}=400\) — the value already used to seed Beat 3. Doubling steps_per_scale halves \(\Delta r\) and roughly doubles the number of stored samples, which is exactly the pattern in the four runs above.

Convergence test. Repeat a model with steps_per_scale = 400, 800, and 1600. Surface radius and total mass should settle down as the radial grid is refined, as in the table above.

Beat 5 · Finding the surface without crossing it

The equation of state is not defined for negative pressure, so the integration must never continue into negative pressure — but the star's true physical surface, where pressure reaches exactly zero, is not itself one of the stored radial samples. Usually the program extrapolates forward from the last positive-pressure sample: it evaluates a variable that stays linear in radius near the surface, and its local slope there, to estimate how much farther out that variable would reach zero, without needing any sample beyond the last positive one. Only rarely, when a full step overshoots past zero pressure before that extrapolation catches it, does the program fall back to interpolating between the last positive sample and the (otherwise-discarded) non-positive trial result the step produced. Either way, the appended endpoint has pressure and density set to zero by construction. See how the usual, extrapolation-based estimate is made, and how close it comes to the star's true zero-pressure radius.

from driver_neutron import compute_neutron_star

REF_R = 7802.758706219183
for n in (200, 400, 800, 1600):
    d = compute_neutron_star(1.666667, 1.26e35, 5.3802e3, steps_per_scale=n)
    print(n, 'R_err(m) =', d['surface_radius_m'] - REF_R)

Save this as surface_convergence_check.py beside the program's four modules and run it with python surface_convergence_check.py. These are the same runs as Beat 4, now compared directly against an independent fine-grid reference: a second, hand-written integrator in the test suite that evolves the regular surface variable \(q=p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\) directly, instead of pressure, and applies the same kind of local tangent-line extrapolation to estimate where \(q\) reaches zero, but at a grid far finer than the production default (steps_per_scale=8000 by default there against 400 here). Evolving \(q\) rather than \(p\) removes the systematic bias measured below. REF_R above is a fixed benchmark carried in the test suite (test_physics_neutron.REFERENCE_RADIUS_M) and checked directly against the production solver's own surface-location regression — not against fresh runs of the q-reference integrator shown here, which the test suite instead exercises independently at four other parameter combinations. Running the shown function once at steps_per_scale=8000 for this model's own parameters does not reproduce REF_R bit-for-bit, differing by about a micrometre — the same scale as the change from doubling the reference's own grid, quantified next — and the test suite now checks that specific gap directly (test_q_reference_model_matches_default_reference_constant) rather than leaving the relationship between the two to be inferred from this page. That doubling sequence, 8000→16000→32000→64000, moves the reference's own answer by about 0.99, 0.26 and 0.065 micrometres, shrinking roughly fourfold each time (consistent with the tangent-line estimate's second-order accuracy, discussed in Beat 4) before floating-point roundoff takes over at still finer grids. All of this is negligible next to the roughly 0.8 m production error it is used to measure; the point is only that REF_R is a well-converged fixed benchmark, not the exact physical surface, and its own trailing digits carry less certainty than their count suggests.

Three tasks, in order. First, the errors: +1.21 m, +0.80 m, +0.78 m and +0.38 m at steps_per_scale 200, 400, 800 and 1600, all positive, meaning the production solver's estimated surface is consistently a little further out than the reference — confirming it is an estimate with a small, quantifiable bias, not the exact zero-pressure location. Then compare the size of these errors with the star itself: even the coarsest, 1.21 m, is about \(1.6\times10^{-4}\) of the 7.804 km radius, and the error shrinks as the grid refines, as any consistent estimate should. Then ask why extrapolating pressure directly would do worse: near the surface, \(p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\), not \(p\) itself, is the quantity that falls linearly in \(r\) to leading order, so extrapolating \(p\) linearly instead would introduce a larger systematic bias, which is exactly what the independent reference above is built to expose and measure.

Then the rule. On the last nominal step before the surface, the loop tests whether the regular variable's linear extrapolation reaches zero within the current step:

Eq. 7 — Surface location by extrapolating the regular variable ALGORITHM
\[ q = p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}, \qquad \Delta r_{\rm surface} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\left(-\frac{p}{dp/dr}\right). \]

If \(0<\Delta r_{\rm surface}\le\Delta r\), the loop stops without taking a full RK4 step: it appends \(r+\Delta r_{\rm surface}\) with pressure and density set to exactly zero, and estimates the small remaining enclosed mass by integrating the leading polytropic density falloff over that last interval, \(m+\,dm/dr\cdot(-p/(dp/dr))\). If a full RK4 step is taken instead and its result comes out at or below zero pressure — which the local extrapolation above usually, but not always, catches first — the driver falls back to a linear interpolation between the last positive and first non-positive pressure samples, the same idea used by the trajectory-landing interpolation in CannonTrajectory, just applied to pressure instead of height.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 2 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 6 · Compactness, causality, and Buchdahl's bound

Every completed model is checked against two independent limits: a mathematical bound on how compact any static fluid star can be, and a physical requirement that nothing travel faster than light inside the model equation of state. They do not have to fail together. Push the central pressure up and watch each one separately.

python main.py --pC 1e34 --output_type mass
python main.py --pC 1e35 --output_type mass
python main.py --pC 1e36 --output_type mass
python main.py --pC 1e37 --output_type mass

Look at the printed compactness, causality check, and Buchdahl check in each of the four runs.

Three tasks, in order. First, the compactness column: 0.203957, 0.349845, 0.462172 and 0.466203 at central pressures of 1034, 1035, 1036 and 1037 Pa — rising with central pressure but nowhere near Buchdahl's bound of 8/9≈0.8889, so all four print Buchdahl check: satisfied. Then the causality column: the central sound speed \(v_s/c\) for the same four runs is 0.357557, 0.566689, 0.898142 and 1.423460, so the first three print causality check: satisfied and the last, at \(p_C=10^{37}\) Pa, prints causality check: NOT satisfied — the model's sound speed has exceeded the speed of light, even though its Buchdahl check still passes. Then draw the conclusion these two diagnostics are built to make visible: compactness is a purely geometric bound on \(2GM/(Rc^2)\) that a star satisfies or does not regardless of what is inside it, while causality is a statement about the assumed equation of state itself, and this simple polytrope breaks the second limit well before it comes anywhere near the first. A failed causality check means the toy equation of state has been pushed into an unphysical regime; it is not a claim that real neutron-star matter transmits signals faster than light.

Then the geometric bound. Under Buchdahl's assumptions — a static, spherically symmetric, isotropic perfect fluid whose density does not increase outward — the compactness of any equilibrium configuration is bounded independently of the equation of state:

Eq. 8 — Buchdahl's bound DERIVED
\[ \frac{2GM}{Rc^2} \le \frac{8}{9}. \]

The program reports \(2GM/(Rc^2)\) for every completed model and compares it with 8/9; it does not attempt to enforce or prove the bound, only to report whether a given model happens to respect it. A polytropic sequence approaching but never reaching this value would be an interesting numerical exploration, not evidence for or against the theorem, which follows from its own analytic assumptions rather than from any particular program's output. The central sound-speed diagnostic behind the causality check, \(v_s^2/c^2=\gamma p/(\rho c^2)\), is evaluated once at the center for every model and is not itself a numbered equation on this page, since it is a direct consequence of Eq. 3.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 5 and Experiment 7 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 7 · A sequence of stars, and where the model stops

One star is a single point. A sequence of stars, built by varying only the central pressure, is where the model earns its keep — and where it also shows where a single polytrope runs out of road, from below as well as from above.

python main.py --pC 1e34 --output_type mass
python main.py --pC 1e35 --output_type mass
python main.py --pC 1e36 --output_type mass
python main.py --pC 1e28 --output_type mass
python main.py --pC 1e27 --output_type mass
python main.py --max_steps 100
python main.py --gamma 1

Look at the total mass printed by each of the first five runs, and at what the last two print instead of a summary.

Three tasks, in order. First, the turnover: total mass is 0.818445, 0.963016 and 0.849747 solar masses at \(p_C=10^{34}\), \(10^{35}\) and \(10^{36}\) Pa (a slightly finer sample than Beat 6's own runs, since a controlled central-pressure sweep is exactly Experiment 4). Mass rises and then falls as central pressure increases, so somewhere between \(10^{35}\) and \(10^{36}\) Pa this one-parameter family has a maximum — the same turning point behind the usual instability argument, which the program does not itself test dynamically. Then the low-pressure end, the puzzle of Investigation 20.1: at \(p_C=10^{28}\) Pa the total mass is 0.020979 solar masses, and at \(p_C=10^{27}\) Pa it falls to 0.010529 solar masses, below the approximate 0.02-solar-mass threshold the textbook flags as implausibly low-density for a real neutron star. The numerical result is not wrong; it simply extrapolates one high-density polytrope into a regime where the crust, neutron-drip physics, and changing composition that a real low-density neutron star has are entirely absent from this single power law. Then the last two commands: --max_steps 100 prints Neutron: The stellar surface was not reached within 100 radial steps. Increase max_steps or reconsider the model parameters. and exits with status 1 rather than returning a truncated profile, and --gamma 1 is rejected exactly as it was in Beat 2, before any integration begins.

Then the rest of the list, which no beat can test because the program has no setting for it. The equation of state is a single polytrope, not a modern piecewise or tabulated nuclear-matter model, and cannot represent a crust, neutron drip, or composition change with radius. The star is static, spherically symmetric, isotropic and non-rotating; there is no rotation, magnetic field, finite temperature, tidal deformability, or baryonic-versus-gravitational mass bookkeeping. The program computes equilibrium configurations only: it can display a model on either side of the mass maximum found above, but it does not evolve an unstable star, model a collapse, or compute quasi-normal modes. And even a model that finishes and prints a summary, like the acausal \(p_C=10^{37}\) Pa run of Beat 6, is not automatically trustworthy: it ran to completion, but its own causality check said so, which is exactly why that diagnostic is printed on every run rather than only on ones that fail to finish.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 4 and Experiment 8 in Suggested Experiments.

Algorithm

This section collects the rules of the program in one place. The physics equations, Eqs. 1–3, are introduced in Beats 0–2; the numerical rules, Eqs. 4–7, in Beats 3–5.

One run, in order

  1. Validate the settings: \(\gamma\) must be finite and greater than 1, \(p_C\) and \(K\) must be finite and positive, and steps_per_scale and max_steps must be integers in their documented ranges.
  2. Compute the central density from \(p_C\) with Eq. 3, then the scale height and nominal step with Eq. 6.
  3. Seed the state at \(r_0=\Delta r\) with the central expansion, Eq. 4.
  4. While fewer than max_steps radial steps have been taken, test whether the surface falls within the current step (Eq. 7); if not, advance with one RK4 step (Eq. 5), halving the step and retrying if a trial stage would need negative pressure.
  5. When the surface is located, append the surface point with pressure and density set to zero and stop; print the model summary and checkpoint table (or plot the requested profile from main.py).

Initialisation

The equation-of-state parameters and central pressure are validated first. The central density, scale height, and nominal step follow from them, and the first stored point is the regular central-expansion state at \(r_0=\Delta r\), not the coordinate singularity at \(r=0\).

Integration loop

On each pass through the loop, the solver:

  1. evaluates the pressure derivative at the current state and tests whether the surface, extrapolated with Eq. 7, falls within the next nominal step;
  2. if it does, appends the extrapolated surface point and stops;
  3. otherwise takes one RK4 step (Eq. 5), shortening the trial step whenever an intermediate stage would require negative pressure, and appends the new state.

After every accepted step the solver also checks that the new compactness remains below 1, raising RuntimeError immediately if it does not, since a compactness of 1 or more means the TOV denominator of Eq. 2 has reached zero.

Termination

Normal termination locates the surface with Eq. 7 or, failing that, by linear interpolation between the last positive and first non-positive pressure samples (Beat 5). If max_steps is exhausted first, the function raises RuntimeError instead of silently returning an incomplete profile.

Guarded arithmetic throughout. Every stage of the pipeline — the equation-of-state inversion, the central expansion, each RK4 stage, and the compactness check — validates its inputs and outputs explicitly and converts an overflow, an underflow, or a non-finite result into a ValueError or RuntimeError with a message naming what went wrong, rather than letting a raw floating-point exception or a silent nan escape to the caller.

Code Modules

physics_neutron.py
Physics & Integrators

Defines the equation of state (eos_density()), the TOV and mass derivatives (structure_derivatives()), the regular central expansion (central_state()), and the RK4 stepper (rk4_step()), all with guarded arithmetic.

driver_neutron.py
Simulation Driver

Validates inputs, sets the scale height and nominal step, seeds the central state, marches outward with dynamically growing arrays, locates the surface, checks compactness and causality, and returns the full profiles plus a compact model summary.

compute_neutron_star(...) -> dict of profiles and summary fields
plot_neutron.py
Visualisation & Summary

Plots pressure, density, or enclosed mass against radius in km, with an optional logarithmic y-axis for pressure or density, and prints the global model summary and the five-row checkpoint table with print_model_summary().

main.py
Command-Line Entry Point

Defines and validates the student-facing command-line options, passes the five simulation settings to compute_neutron_star(), and converts a caught ValueError, RuntimeError, or OverflowError into a concise Neutron:-prefixed exit message.

Version and Build ID. python main.py --version prints the model version and the Build ID without running a simulation. The Build ID is a short hash of the four program modules above and of nothing else. This Help file, the Release Notes, the sample outputs and the test suite are not part of it, so editing any of those does not change it, and editing one of the four modules does. It appears at the top of this page and on the first line of every run's printed summary.

Quick Start

Requirements

Python 3.10 or later, with numpy and matplotlib:

pip install numpy matplotlib

Running the Program

Clone or download the repository, then run the program from its folder:

cd GFTGU-Programs/Neutron
python main.py
python main.py --pC 1e35 --output_type density --log_y
python main.py --help
python main.py --version

Each run prints the two-part summary described under Printed Summary, and then opens the plot window. The options and their defaults are listed under Parameters.

python main.py

Changing the central pressure and equation of state:

Refining the radial resolution:

python main.py --steps_per_scale 1600 --output_type mass

Combining any or all settings:

Output

Every run computes the full radius, pressure, density, and enclosed gravitational mass profiles; --output_type selects which one is plotted.

  • a single profile line against radius in km, with pressure in Pa, density in kg/m³, or enclosed mass in solar masses;
  • an optional logarithmic y-axis for pressure or density, with the exact zero-valued surface point masked out explicitly rather than left to Matplotlib, since zero cannot be shown on a log axis;
  • the mass profile always on a linear axis, regardless of --log_y;
  • grid lines and axis labels naming the plotted quantity and its units.

compute_neutron_star() returns a dictionary whose radius, pressure, density and mass lists all have the same length, the number of stored radial samples, including the central starting point and the located surface. plot_neutron(data, output_type, log_y=False) displays one of the three profiles from that dictionary.

The output-selector interface. output_type accepts "Pressure", "Density", or "Mass"; capitalization and surrounding spaces are ignored, so " DENSITY " and "density" select the same profile. Anything else, or a non-string value, raises a ValueError whose message names the three accepted selectors. log_y must be an actual Python bool; 0, 1, and the string "yes" are all rejected rather than silently treated as booleans.

Suggested Experiments

Suggested order: the experiments below are arranged roughly from introductory parameter exploration to more demanding sequence-building and cross-program comparisons. Several require more than one run; record the printed results of each command before comparing them.
Experiment 1 · Introductory
Profile shapes: pressure, density, and mass

Goes with Beat 0.

Run the default model with --output_type set in turn to pressure, density, and mass. Describe how each profile changes with radius, and explain in words why enclosed mass must increase outward while pressure and density decrease.

Check: all three runs share the same surface radius, 7.804 km, and total mass, 0.962591 Msun (Beat 0); only the plotted curve changes.

Experiment 2 · Introductory–Intermediate
Numerical convergence

Goes with Beat 4 and Beat 5.

Repeat the default model with --steps_per_scale = 200, 400, 800, and 1600. Compare surface radius, total mass, and compactness. Which reported quantity is most sensitive to radial refinement, and which has essentially already converged at the default resolution?

Check: surface radius is 7.803970, 7.803563, 7.803543 and 7.803139 km, and total mass is 0.96259066, 0.96259065, 0.96259064 and 0.96259064 Msun, at the four resolutions (Beat 4). Mass converges far faster than radius.

Experiment 3 · Intermediate
Comparison with Newtonian hydrostatics

Goes with Beat 1.

Run Star separately and match its central density and pressure into Neutron. Star obtains its central density from temperature and composition inputs and derives its own polytropic constant, so the two programs do not take an identical set of equation-of-state parameters; after running Star, use its central density \(\rho_C\) to set \(K=p_C/\rho_C^\gamma\) in Neutron. Compare mass, radius, and pressure and density profiles between the two programs. Relativistic differences should grow as compactness increases.

Check: the weak-field agreement demonstrated numerically in Beat 1 is the limiting case in which the two programs must agree closely; the default Neutron model, at compactness 0.3643, is far from that limit.

Experiment 4 · Intermediate
Mass–central-pressure sequence and the maximum mass

Goes with Beat 7.

Run a logarithmically spaced series of --pC values and record data["total_mass_solar"] and data["surface_radius_km"] for each. Plot mass and radius against central pressure and locate the mass maximum. The program computes equilibria only; it does not dynamically evolve the unstable branch past the turnover. Can you choose a toy polytropic equation of state whose maximum mass exceeds two solar masses? Three? Explain why such a result is not, by itself, evidence for a realistic neutron-star equation of state.

Check: the default equation of state gives 0.818445, 0.963016 and 0.849747 Msun at \(p_C=10^{34}\), \(10^{35}\) and \(10^{36}\) Pa, so the maximum lies between the last two (Beat 7).

Experiment 5 · Intermediate
Buchdahl compactness along a sequence

Goes with Beat 6.

Plot data["compactness"] for the same sequence of models used in Experiment 4. Every configuration must remain at or below 8/9 (Eq. 8). Investigate how close the chosen sequence comes to the bound, and separately track data["causality_satisfied"] across the same sequence: does the model become acausal before or after it approaches the Buchdahl bound?

Check: for the default equation of state, compactness reaches only 0.4662 by \(p_C=10^{37}\) Pa, well short of 8/9, while causality has already failed at that same central pressure (Beat 6). For this polytrope, causality is the binding limit, not Buchdahl's bound.

Experiment 6 · Intermediate–Advanced
Controlled equation-of-state stiffness comparison

Goes with Beat 2.

Because \(K\)'s dimensions depend on \(\gamma\) (Beat 2), comparing exponents at a fixed numerical \(K\) is not a controlled comparison. Instead choose a common central pressure and a target central density, and for each \(\gamma>1\) compute \(K=p_C/\rho_C^\gamma\) before running: 

python main.py --gamma 1.5 --K 7.374987e6 --pC 1.26e35 --output_type mass
python main.py --gamma 1.666667 --K 5.3802e3 --pC 1.26e35 --output_type mass
python main.py --gamma 2 --K 2.863463e-3 --pC 1.26e35 --output_type mass

The three K values above already target a common \(\rho_C\approx6.63345\times10^{18}\) kg/m³ at the shared \(p_C=1.26\times10^{35}\) Pa. Compare the resulting mass and radius, and describe how a stiffer equation of state (larger \(\gamma\)) changes the star at fixed central density.

Check: the middle command, \(\gamma=1.666667\), reproduces the default model of Beat 0 exactly: surface radius 7.804 km, total mass 0.962591 Msun.

Experiment 7 · Advanced
Limits of a single polytrope at high density

Goes with Beat 6.

Explore a wide central-pressure range with python main.py --pC 1e35 --output_type density --log_y and python main.py --pC 1e37 --output_type density --log_y. For each model, evaluate \(v_s^2/c^2=\gamma p/(\rho c^2)\), especially at the center, and note where the printed causality check fails. Compare the form of this equation of state with a modern piecewise polytrope or tabulated nuclear equation of state, and explain why a realistic self-bound quark-matter model generally needs an offset energy density that cannot be represented merely by choosing \(\gamma=1\) here (which this solver refuses outright; Beat 2).

Check: central sound speed rises from \(v_s/c=0.566689\) at \(p_C=10^{35}\) Pa to \(v_s/c=1.423460\) at \(p_C=10^{37}\) Pa, crossing 1 somewhere between the two (Beat 6).

Experiment 8 · Advanced · Schutz suggestion
The minimum-mass puzzle of Investigation 20.1

Goes with Beat 7.

Reduce --pC below 1e28 until the computed mass falls below 0.02 solar masses, the approximate lower bound Investigation 20.1 discusses for a physically credible neutron star. Does the numerical result invalidate that discussion? Examine how this single high-density polytrope extrapolates into the low-density regime, and identify the crust, neutron-drip, composition-change, or other low-density physics the simple equation of state leaves out entirely. Distinguish a mathematical equilibrium of the toy equation of state from a physically credible low-mass neutron star.

Check: --pC 1e28 prints 0.020979 Msun, just above the threshold, and --pC 1e27 prints 0.010529 Msun, below it (Beat 7).

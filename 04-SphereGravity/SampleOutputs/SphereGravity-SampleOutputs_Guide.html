SphereGravity

Representative Sample Outputs

Version 1.2.0  |  Build 35c1d2c0dd16  |  Generated 5 September 2026

Purpose. This compact, self-contained guide presents three plots generated from the supplied current source. The examples show the raw field of a numerically tiled thin spherical shell and the discretisation error at two angular resolutions. No external image files are required.

SphereGravity numerically tests Newton’s shell theorem. In dimensionless units the thin shell has radius R = 1, density scale 1, and G = 1. The default thickness-to-radius mass-scale parameter is epsilon=0.001. The calculation samples 1,000 radii from 0 through 4.995; the ideal shell surface at r = 1 is deliberately not evaluated.

Figure 1 — Raw acceleration profile

radius, accel = run_spheregravity(
    nDiv=100, outputType="acceleration", epsilon=0.001)
plot_spheregravity(radius, accel, outputType="acceleration")

With 100 divisions in each angular coordinate, the numerical shell contains 10,000 conceptual tiles. The red dashed line marks the shell boundary.

What it shows

  • Inside the hollow shell, positive and negative tile contributions nearly cancel, as Newton’s theorem predicts.
  • Outside the shell, the computed inward acceleration decreases approximately as 1/r². The narrow region close to the idealized surface is the hardest part for a finite angular grid to resolve.
  • The gap at r = 1 is intentional: the zero stored at that array position is only a compatibility placeholder and is omitted from the drawn curve.

Computed shell mass: 0.012566887  |  g(0.5): -9.194e-07  |  g(2): 0.003141880  |  M/2²: 0.003141722

Figure 2 — Default relative difference, 100 divisions

radius, difference = run_spheregravity(
    nDiv=100, outputType="relative difference", epsilon=0.001)
plot_spheregravity(radius, difference,
                   outputType="relative difference")

This is the program’s supplied main.py configuration. Outside the shell the curve is (gnum − M/r²)/(M/r²); inside, where the exact field is zero, the residual is normalized by M.

What it shows

  • The largest errors occur at the sampled radii immediately beside the shell: +0.152 at r = 0.995 and -0.151 at r = 1.005.
  • Away from the surface the agreement is much better. At r = 2, the exterior relative difference is only 5.030e-05.

Interior residual at r=0.5: -7.316e-05  |  Exterior difference at r=2: 5.030e-05  |  Shell-mass relative error: 4.112e-05

Figure 3 — Higher-resolution convergence, 1,000 divisions

radius, difference = run_spheregravity(
    nDiv=1000, outputType="relative difference", epsilon=0.001)
plot_spheregravity(radius, difference,
                   outputType="relative difference")

Increasing the angular resolution by a factor of ten gives one million conceptual shell tiles and greatly reduces the numerical residuals. Notice that the vertical scale is much tighter than in Figure 2.

What it shows

  • At r = 0.5 the normalized interior residual falls from -7.316e-05 to -7.311e-07.
  • At r = 2 the exterior relative difference falls from 5.030e-05 to 5.026e-07, almost exactly the hundredfold improvement expected from approximate 1/ndiv² convergence.
  • Even at high resolution the nearest points to the discontinuous thin-shell surface remain the least accurate: -0.0094 and +0.0095 on the two sides.

Computed shell mass: 0.012566376  |  Continuum 4π ε: 0.012566371  |  Shell-mass relative error: 4.112e-07

Notes for evaluation

  • Textbook method retained: all three displayed results use the default transparent midpoint-loop implementation, which mirrors the numerical-integration discussion rather than hiding the shell sum inside vectorized array operations.
  • Optional optimization: the physics module also supplies an interchangeable bounded-NumPy implementation for the advanced vectorization experiment. It was not used to produce these figures.
  • Meaning of epsilon: changing epsilon rescales the shell mass and raw acceleration. It is a mass-scale factor, not a geometrical thickening of the ideal shell.
  • Surface limitation: the field on an infinitely thin shell is discontinuous and the point-mass tile approximation is singular there, so the program skips exactly r = 1.
  • Verification: all 65 supplied tests passed from the canonical tests directory before these figures were generated.

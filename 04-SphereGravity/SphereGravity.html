What This Program Does

SphereGravity computes the gravitational acceleration produced by a thin spherical shell of matter at a series of radial distances from the center, through the interior, and out to about five shell radii. The calculation is a numerical approximation: conceptually, the shell is divided into ndiv × ndiv small tiles. By azimuthal symmetry, the code sums over ndiv latitude rings and multiplies each ring's contribution by ndiv identical tiles.

The program can display either the raw acceleration profile or the relative difference between the numerical result and the exact Newtonian prediction. The latter mode makes it easy to see how rapidly the computation converges to the analytical answer as the number of tiles is increased.

Physical Background

One of Newton's most beautiful results in the Principia is the shell theorem: a uniform spherical shell of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\) exerts exactly the same gravitational force on any external test mass as if all of \(M\) were concentrated at the shell's center. Furthermore, a test mass placed anywhere inside the hollow shell experiences no net gravitational force at all.

These results have profound consequences throughout physics and astronomy. They allow us to treat the Earth, Sun, and any other spherically symmetric body as point masses when computing exterior gravitational fields. The theorem is also important in Newtonian cosmology: for a spherically symmetric mass distribution, matter in shells outside a chosen radius makes no net contribution to the gravitational acceleration at that radius.

Newton proved the theorem analytically. Here we verify it numerically: because any spherically symmetric body can be decomposed into a nested set of thin shells, proving the result for a single thin shell suffices for the general case, as Schutz explains in Investigation 4.3.

Newton's Shell Theorem

For a thin spherical shell of total mass \(M\) and radius \(R\), the gravitational acceleration \(g\) at radial distance \(r\) from the center is:

Eq. 1 — Shell theorem (exact Newtonian result)
\[ g(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{GM}{r^2} & r > R \quad (\text{outside the shell}) \\[10pt] 0 & r < R \quad (\text{inside the hollow shell}) \end{cases} \]

The program uses dimensionless units throughout: the shell radius is set to \(R = 1\), the volume-density scale is \(\rho = 1\), and \(G = 1\). The parameter \(\varepsilon = 0.001\) supplies a nominal thickness factor when assigning mass to each surface tile. Geometrically, however, all tile masses lie on the radius-\(1\) surface, so the calculation is a thin-shell model; changing \(\varepsilon\) changes the mass scale, not the shell geometry.

Governing Equations

Shell geometry and tile masses

The shell is parameterised by latitude angle \(\theta\) (measured from the equator, ranging from \(-\pi/2\) at the south pole to \(+\pi/2\) at the north pole) and azimuthal angle \(\varphi\). The shell is divided into \(n_\text{div}\) equal steps in each coordinate. The angular step sizes are:

Eq. 2 — Angular step sizes
\[ \Delta\varphi = \frac{2\pi}{n_\text{div}}, \qquad \Delta\theta = \frac{\Delta\varphi}{2} \]

Each tile at latitude \(\theta\) has a surface area element \(\Delta A = \cos\theta\, \Delta\theta\,\Delta\varphi\) (in units where \(R = 1\)). The mass of each tile is:

Eq. 3 — Tile mass
\[ \delta m = \varepsilon\,\rho\,R^2\,\cos\theta\;\Delta\theta\;\Delta\varphi \;\longrightarrow\; \varepsilon\,\cos\theta\;\Delta\theta\;\Delta\varphi \quad (R = \rho = 1) \]

All \(n_\text{div}\) tiles in a ring of constant latitude are identical, so the ring's contribution to the total mass is \(n_\text{div}\,\delta m\). In the continuum limit the shell mass is \(M = 4\pi\varepsilon\). The program uses the finite midpoint sum produced by the same latitude tiling, which approaches this value as nDiv increases.

Distance from tile to observer

By the azimuthal symmetry of the problem the observation point is placed at position \(r\) along the shell's symmetry axis. The distance from a tile at latitude \(\theta\) to the observer at radius \(r\) is:

Eq. 4 — Distance from tile to observer
\[ d(\theta,r) = \sqrt{1 + r^2 + 2r\sin\theta} \]

Axial component of acceleration

The gravitational acceleration toward the center of the shell contributed by a single ring of tiles is:

Eq. 5 — Ring contribution to radial acceleration
\[ \delta g = n_\text{div}\;\frac{G\,\delta m\;(r + \sin\theta)}{d(\theta,r)^3} \]

where the factor \((r + \sin\theta)\) is the projection of the tile-to-observer vector onto the symmetry axis. The plotted acceleration is the signed radial component in the program's convention, so small numerical residuals inside the shell may have either sign. The total acceleration at radius \(r\) is:

Eq. 6 — Total numerical acceleration
\[ g_\text{num}(r) = \sum_{k=0}^{n_\text{div}-1} \delta g_k \]

Relative difference

When the relative difference output mode is selected, the program reports:

Eq. 7 — Relative difference from Newton's theorem
\[ \delta(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{g_\text{num}(r) - g_\text{Newton}(r)}{g_\text{Newton}(r)} & r > 1 \quad\bigl(g_\text{Newton} = M/r^2\bigr)\\[10pt] \dfrac{g_\text{num}(r)}{M} & r \leq 1 \quad \text{(inside: Newton predicts zero)} \end{cases} \]

Outside the shell the Newtonian acceleration is \(g_\text{Newton} = M/r^2\) (with \(G=1\)). Inside the shell Newton predicts exactly zero, so the raw numerical acceleration is normalised by \(M\) (the Newtonian acceleration just outside the surface) to produce a dimensionless measure of the residual error.

Tiling Algorithm

The computation follows a straightforward nested-loop scheme described in Investigation 4.3 of Gravity from the Ground Up:

  1. Initialise geometry. Compute the angular step sizes \(\Delta\varphi\) and \(\Delta\theta\), and set the initial latitude \(\theta_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\theta - 90°\) (centre of the first tile, just above the south pole).
  2. Compute total shell mass. Loop over all \(n_\text{div}\) latitude rings. For each ring accumulate \(\Delta M_k = n_\text{div}\;\delta m_k\). This gives the normalisation constant \(M\) used in relative-difference mode.
  3. Outer loop: radial positions. Step through 1 000 observation radii \(r_j = 0.005\,j\) for \(j = 0, 1, \ldots, 999\), giving \(r \in [0, 4.995]\). Restricting the plot to about five shell radii leaves enough horizontal space to see the behaviour throughout the interior \(r < 1\), while still showing the exterior \(1/r^2\) falloff.
  4. Skip the shell surface. The step at \(j = 200\) (i.e., \(r = 1.0\)) falls exactly on the idealized thin shell. The field has a discontinuity across the surface, so this point is not evaluated. Its plotted value is set to zero only as a placeholder; this is not a claim that the physical surface acceleration is zero.
  5. Inner loop: tile summation. For each \(r_j\), loop over all \(n_\text{div}\) latitude rings. Compute \(d_k = \sqrt{1 + r_j^2 + 2\,r_j\,\sin\theta_k}\) and add the ring's contribution (Eq. 5) to the running sum \(g_\text{num}(r_j)\).
  6. Post-process for output mode. If relative difference is selected, convert each acceleration value using Eq. 7. Otherwise output the raw acceleration array.
Computational cost: The inner loop executes \(n_\text{div}\) times for each of the 1 000 radii, so the total work scales as \(\mathcal{O}(1000 \times n_\text{div})\). The mass-summation loop adds another \(\mathcal{O}(n_\text{div})\). For \(n_\text{div} = 10\,000\) this is \(10^7\) floating-point operations — fast on modern hardware but noticeable.

Accuracy and Convergence

The key parameter controlling angular resolution is nDiv. As it increases, the latitude rings become narrower and the numerical sum approaches the exact shell-theorem result. Away from the shell surface, the midpoint-style angular discretisation converges approximately as \(n_\text{div}^{-2}\).

nDiv Relative error at \(r=1.1\) Expected trend
100≈ 2.5 × 10−3baseline
1 000≈ 2.5 × 10−5about 100× smaller
10 000≈ 2.5 × 10−7about 100× smaller again

It is important to compare the error at a fixed radius. Very close to the shell, the contribution from nearby tiles becomes sharply concentrated and is much harder to resolve with a coarse angular grid. With the present fine radial sampling, the first point outside the shell is \(r=1.005\), where the error can be much larger than it is at \(r=1.1\). This near-surface behaviour is itself a useful numerical effect to investigate.

Module Overview

The calculation is split among four Python files so that the physics, plotting, and user settings are easy to find:

Physics

physics_spheregravity.py

Encodes the shell-tiling algorithm, shell-mass calculation, gravitational summation, and the conversion to relative-difference output. The formulas described in Eqs. 2–7 are implemented here.

compute_shell_mass(nDiv, epsilon=0.001) → float
requires a positive integer nDiv and positive finite epsilon
compute_acceleration_profile(nDiv, outputType='acceleration', epsilon=0.001) → (ndarray, ndarray)
Driver

driver_spheregravity.py

Calls the physics calculation and returns the (radius, acceleration) arrays used by main.py and the plotting routine.

run_spheregravity(nDiv=100, outputType='acceleration', epsilon=0.001) → (ndarray, ndarray)
Plot

plot_spheregravity.py

Accepts the (radius, acceleration) arrays from the driver and renders a matplotlib figure. Axis labels and title adapt automatically to the selected output mode.

plot_spheregravity(radius, acceleration, outputType='acceleration') → None
Entry Point

main.py

The only file the user needs to edit. Set nDiv and outputType, then run python main.py. Calls run_spheregravity() and passes the result to plot_spheregravity().

nDiv = 100
outputType = 'relative difference'

Parameters

The two normal user settings, nDiv and outputType, are set in main.py. The driver and physics functions also accept epsilon as an advanced optional keyword argument; it remains fixed at 0.001 in the normal workflow.

Parameter Default Type Description
nDiv 100 int Number of angular divisions per coordinate. The shell is divided into ndiv latitude rings, each representing ndiv identical azimuthal tiles. Larger values give higher angular resolution at the cost of longer computation time. The program does not impose an arbitrary upper limit, so extremely large values may take a long time to run.
outputType 'relative difference' (main.py) OutputType (Literal['acceleration', 'relative difference']) Selects what the plot displays. Either 'acceleration' (raw \(g(r)\)) or 'relative difference' (fractional error from Newton's theorem per Eq. 7).
epsilon 0.001 float Mass-scale factor corresponding to nominal shell thickness divided by radius. Because every tile mass is placed on the same spherical surface, changing \(\varepsilon\) scales the raw acceleration and total mass but does not make the modeled shell geometrically thicker. It is not a normal main.py setting; to change it, pass epsilon to run_spheregravity() or call compute_acceleration_profile() directly.

Output Modes

acceleration

Plots the computed gravitational acceleration \(g_\text{num}(r)\) versus radius \(r\). Outside the shell (\(r > 1\)) you will see the characteristic \(1/r^2\) falloff; inside (\(r < 1\)) the acceleration is essentially zero. The shell radius \(R = 1\) is the boundary between the two regions.

relative difference

Plots the fractional deviation between the numerical result and Newton's exact prediction (Eq. 7). This mode is more informative for convergence studies: as nDiv increases the curve should drop closer and closer to zero at sampled points away from the skipped shell surface. Inside the shell, where Newton predicts \(g = 0\), the residual acceleration normalised by \(M\) is plotted instead.

Quick Start

What to edit

For ordinary use, edit only these two settings near the top of main.py: 

nDiv = 100
outputType = 'relative difference'

Running the program

cd SphereGravity
python main.py

With the default settings (nDiv = 100, outputType = 'relative difference') the program computes the acceleration profile over 1 000 radii from \(r=0\) to \(r=4.995\) and opens a matplotlib window. Away from the shell surface the numerical result is already close to Newton's theorem—for example, the relative error at \(r=1.1\) is about \(2.5\times10^{-3}\) (0.25%). Much closer to \(r=1\), the finite angular tiling is harder to resolve and the error is larger.

Switching output mode

# In main.py — show raw acceleration instead of relative difference
outputType = 'acceleration'

Running programmatically

from driver_spheregravity import run_spheregravity
from plot_spheregravity   import plot_spheregravity

radius, accel = run_spheregravity(nDiv=1000, outputType='relative difference', epsilon=0.001)
plot_spheregravity(radius, accel, outputType='relative difference')

Suggested Experiments

The experiments below are arranged in approximate order of difficulty. The first four require only changes to nDiv and outputType; the last two make more direct use of the numerical machinery or ask for a substantial programming extension.

1 · Introductory — Verify the Shell Theorem Visually

Run with outputType = 'acceleration'. Confirm the two parts of Newton's shell theorem: the acceleration is essentially zero for \(r<1\), while outside the shell it falls approximately as \(1/r^2\). Compare, for example, the acceleration near \(r=2\) with that near \(r=4\); the latter should be about one quarter as large. The plotted point exactly at \(r=1\) is only a zero placeholder because the ideal thin-shell surface itself is not evaluated.

nDiv=100 · outputType='acceleration'

2 · Introductory–Intermediate — Reproduce Schutz's Convergence Test

Switch to outputType = 'relative difference' and increase the angular resolution by factors of ten: nDiv = 100, 1000, 10000. Compare the curves and observe that the numerical disagreement with Newton's theorem becomes rapidly smaller. For a quantitative comparison, read the value at the same exterior radius, such as \(r=1.1\), on each run rather than comparing different points on the three curves.

nDiv=100 / 1000 / 10000 · outputType='relative difference'

3 · Intermediate — Measure the Convergence Rate

At the fixed exterior radius \(r=1.1\), compare the relative differences for nDiv = 100, 1000, 10000. They should be approximately \(2.5\times10^{-3}\), \(2.5\times10^{-5}\), and \(2.5\times10^{-7}\). Each tenfold increase in nDiv therefore reduces the error by roughly a factor of 100, consistent with approximate \(n_\text{div}^{-2}\) convergence away from the shell. Explain why a statement about convergence must compare errors at the same radius.

compare at r=1.1 · look for error ∝ nDiv⁻²

4 · Intermediate — Why the Shell Surface Is Numerically Difficult

Keep outputType = 'relative difference' and examine exterior points progressively closer to the shell, such as \(r=1.1,\ 1.05,\ 1.01,\ 1.005\). At fixed nDiv, the error grows as \(r\) approaches 1 because the contribution from nearby tiles becomes increasingly concentrated in angle and is poorly resolved by a coarse grid. Repeat with a larger nDiv and see how much of the near-surface discrepancy disappears.

nDiv=100, then 1000 · compare r=1.1, 1.05, 1.01, 1.005

5 · Intermediate–Advanced — Interior Cancellation and Shell-Mass Convergence

First compare the residual interior acceleration at a fixed point such as \(r=0.5\) or \(r=0.9\) as nDiv increases; it should approach zero even though the individual tile contributions are not zero. Then, in a short Python session, call compute_shell_mass(nDiv) for the same values of nDiv and compare the result with the continuum value \(4\pi\varepsilon\), with the default \(\varepsilon=0.001\). These two tests show separately how the numerical tiling converges in total mass and in the much more delicate cancellation of the gravitational field.

r=0.5 or 0.9 · compute_shell_mass(100, 1000, 10000)

6 · Advanced Programming Extension — Replace the Sphere by an Ellipsoidal Homoeoid

Make a separate experimental copy of the program, replace the spherical geometry by an axially symmetric ellipsoidal homoeoid (an oblate or prolate ellipsoidal shell), and calculate the field at several interior points, including points off the symmetry axis. (By a homoeoidal shell, we mean the thin-shell limit of material between similar, concentric, similarly oriented ellipsoids of uniform volume density.) Verify that the interior field tends toward zero as the tiling is refined, while the exterior field is direction-dependent and therefore cannot be represented by a point mass at the center.

advanced · geometry, tile masses, and off-axis field components must change

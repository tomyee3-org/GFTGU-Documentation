Program Description

Random2 simulates how randomly wandering particles travel from their starting point as a function of the number of steps taken. The simulation is motivated by one of astrophysics' most striking transport phenomena: the extraordinarily slow journey of a photon from the core of the Sun to its surface.

A photon produced by nuclear fusion at the Sun's center does not travel outward in a straight line. Instead, it is absorbed and re-emitted by the dense solar plasma billions of times, changing direction randomly at each interaction. The net outward displacement after \(N\) such steps is not proportional to \(N\) — as it would be for directed motion — but rather to \(\sqrt{N}\). This square-root scaling is the hallmark of diffusion, and it means that a photon requires roughly 10,000 to 170,000 years to traverse the 700,000 km from core to surface, even though light itself crosses that distance in about 2.3 seconds if unobstructed.

Two Modes of Operation

In the "scaled_distance" mode of operation, this program performs a systematic suite of experiments. Starting with a maximum walk length of maxSteps, it halves the number of steps repeatedly, running nTrials independent random walks at each step-count to obtain a statistically reliable average. The result is a table — and a log-log plot — of average scaled net displacement versus number of steps. On log-log axes a diffusive process appears as a straight line with slope exactly \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), confirming the \(\langle d \rangle \propto \sqrt{N}\) scaling law.

Each step in a single walk has two components drawn independently and uniformly from the interval \([-1, +1]\) — see Physics & Equations for the full derivation. This mimics an isotropic scattering event where direction is equally likely in any direction within the plane. Because the step lengths vary (a step drawn near a corner of the unit square is longer than one drawn near an edge), the raw displacement is normalized by the average step length before averaging across trials, producing a dimensionless scaled distance that cleanly reveals the diffusive scaling.

The "walk2d" mode of operation adds a directly visual way of seeing the same physics. Instead of generating a log-log plot of scaled distance versus step count that provides a quantitative view of the statistics, this second mode provides an intuitive view of how a handful of individual photons might follow a meandering path from the center of a circular "star" outward towards the surface before eventually escaping.

These two separate displays are built on the same 2D step-generation physics (see Physics & Equations). The scaled-distance experiment and the star visualization can be run independently; neither depends on the other.

Astrophysical context: The random walk is the simplest model of radiative diffusion in stellar interiors. More realistic models account for the frequency-dependent mean free path of photons and the temperature gradient driving a net outward energy flux, but the \(\sqrt{N}\) scaling persists and governs the overall timescale of energy transport.

Physics & Equations

The Random Step

At each step \(i\), two displacements are drawn independently and uniformly from \([-1, +1]\):

(1) \[ \Delta x_i = 2 U_i^{(x)} - 1, \quad \Delta y_i = 2 U_i^{(y)} - 1, \qquad U \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1). \]
Why 2D: Random2 uses a 2D walk throughout, for both display modes, since the star visualization is inherently planar, and the \(\sqrt{N}\) diffusion law (see Diffusion Scaling Law below) is dimension-independent, so nothing about the underlying physics demonstration is lost by working in a plane.

Individual Step Length

The Euclidean length of step \(i\) is:

(2) \[ \ell_i = \sqrt{\Delta x_i^2 + \Delta y_i^2}. \]

For components uniform on \([-1,1]\), the expected 2D step length has the closed form

(3) \[ \langle \ell \rangle = \frac{\sqrt{2} + \ln(1+\sqrt{2})}{3} \approx 0.7652, \]

confirmed numerically by direct simulation (20 million samples agree with this value to five significant figures). Individual step lengths range from \(0\) to \(\sqrt{2}\).

Accumulated Position and Net Displacement

After \(N\) steps the walker's position is:

(4) \[ X = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta x_i, \quad Y = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta y_i. \]
(5) \[ D = \sqrt{X^2 + Y^2}. \]

Average Step Length and Scaled Distance

(6) \[ \bar{\ell} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell_i, \qquad d = \frac{D}{\bar{\ell}}. \]

Diffusion Scaling Law

As in the original program, the expected scaled distance grows as the square root of the number of steps, appearing as a line of slope \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) on log-log axes:

(7) \[ \langle d \rangle \propto \sqrt{N}, \qquad \log \langle d \rangle = \tfrac{1}{2}\log N + \mathrm{const}. \]

Algorithm Overview

The scaled-distance experiment uses the following loop structure:

  • Outer loop — experiment suite: Index \(j\) runs from \(n_{\mathrm{walks}}-1\) down to \(0\); the step count starts at maxSteps and is integer-halved after each experiment, exiting early if it drops to \(\le 1\).
  • Middle loop — trial averaging: nTrials independent walks are performed per step count and their scaled distances averaged.
  • Inner loop — single walk: steps through \(n_{\mathrm{steps}}\) iterations, accumulating position and total step length.

As before, filling lengths[j]/avg_dist[j] from high \(j\) to low leaves both arrays in ascending step-count order on return, with no sorting needed before plotting.

2D Star Visualization

This display draws n_walks independent 2D walks starting at the origin ("the star's center"), each continuing until it crosses a circular boundary ("the surface") of a given or default radius — at which point a straight ray is drawn onward from the crossing point, in the walk's final direction of travel, representing the photon escaping into space.

Default Radius

The default radius is a small multiple of a walk's characteristic (RMS) diffusion distance for a nominal step count:

(8) \[ R = f \sqrt{N_{\mathrm{ref}} \, \lambda}, \qquad f = 2,\ \ N_{\mathrm{ref}} = 2000, \ \ \lambda = 1 \]

where \(f\) is radius_factor, \(N_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is reference_steps, and \(\lambda\) is mean_free_path.

reference_steps sizes the star, not the walk: it only feeds Eq. 8 above. Each walk keeps going — however many steps that takes — until it actually reaches the boundary; it typically takes several thousand steps to do so, well beyond reference_steps itself. That's expected, not a malfunction.

Exact Boundary Crossing

Rather than simply stopping a walk at the first step that lands outside the circle (which would leave a visible, unphysical jump past the boundary), the exact crossing point is found by solving for where the straight segment from the last inside point \(\mathbf{p}_0\) to the first outside point \(\mathbf{p}_1\) meets the circle \(|\mathbf{p}(t)| = R\), with \(\mathbf{p}(t) = \mathbf{p}_0 + t(\mathbf{p}_1 - \mathbf{p}_0)\):

(9) \[ a t^2 + b t + c = 0, \quad a = |\mathbf{p}_1-\mathbf{p}_0|^2,\ \, b = 2\mathbf{p}_0\cdot(\mathbf{p}_1-\mathbf{p}_0),\ \, c = |\mathbf{p}_0|^2 - R^2, \]

taking the root \(t \in (0,1]\). The escaping ray then continues from this exact point in the direction of the walk's final step, extended an additional ray_length_factor \(\times\, R\) beyond it.

Number of Walks

n_walks defaults to 4. With more walks drawn simultaneously, paths overlap heavily near the center and individual character is lost; with four, each walk's full, often dramatically convoluted shape stays legible — including cases where a walk wanders out toward one side of the star, nearly reaching the surface, then backtracks across almost the entire interior before finally escaping somewhere else altogether.

Module Structure

Random2 follows the standard GFtGU physics/driver/plot/main pattern.

random2_physics.py

2D step generation, the default-radius formula (Eq. 8), and the exact circle-crossing geometry (Eq. 9). No plotting, no driver-loop logic.

generate_2d_step() → (dx, dy) default_radius(n_steps, mfp, f) → radius circle_crossing_fraction(p0, p1, r) → t in (0, 1] or None

random2_driver.py

Both experiments live here: run_scaled_distance_experiment (the scaled-distance suite) and run_walk2d (the star visualization, running each walk until it escapes).

run_scaled_distance_experiment( max_steps, n_trials) → (lengths, avg_dist) run_walk2d( reference_steps=2000, n_walks=4, ...) → Walk2DResult

random2_plot.py

plot_scaled_distance reproduces the original log-log figure. plot_walk2d draws the star and its walks.

plot_scaled_distance(lengths, avg_dist) plot_walk2d(result, corner="upper right")
main.py: chooses between display = "scaled_distance" or "walk2d". All editable parameters for whichever mode is selected are set at the top of the function, following the same single-entry-point convention as every other GFtGU program.

All parameters are set at the top of main.py, split by which display is selected.

Program Output

Scaled-distance mode produces a log-log plot of average scaled distance versus number of steps, expected to lie close to a line of slope \(\tfrac{1}{2}\).

2D star-visualization mode produces a single square figure showing the star as a filled circle, each walk in its own color, and an escaping ray wherever a walk reached the boundary. A small annotation box (placed in the corner given by corner, upper right by default) reports the radius, the reference step count, and how many of the walks escaped.

Suggested Experiments

Verify the √N Law

Run the scaled-distance mode with defaults and confirm the log-log slope is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\).

display = "scaled_distance"

Escape-Time Distribution

Record how many steps each walk actually takes to escape at the default radius. Histogram the results across many runs — the tail is considerably longer than the median.

reference_steps = 2000 radius_factor = 2.0

Radius Factor vs. Escape Time

Increase radius_factor from 0.5 up to 2.0 or higher and watch how much longer a walk typically takes to escape as the star grows.

radius_factor = 0.5 # then 1.0, 1.5, 2.0

Number of Walks vs. Legibility

Compare n_walks = 4 against n_walks = 10 or more. At what point do individual convoluted paths become impossible to follow?

n_walks = 4 # then 6, 10

A Custom Radius

Override the default entirely with an explicit radius, and observe how a small radius produces short, direct-looking rays while a large one produces long, tangled interior paths before escape.

radius = 40.0 # override Eq. 8

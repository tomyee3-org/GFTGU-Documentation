Purpose. These six figures give a compact visual survey of CannonTrajectory, one drawn from each Beat of the Help tutorial that has a command producing its own trajectory plot. The program models a projectile near Earth's surface using constant standard gravity, a flat ground plane, and no air resistance. Read alongside the Help, each figure and its printed summary illustrate the point that Beat makes; two Beats (5 and 6) look closely at the same printed numbers without a new plot of their own, and are not pictured here.

1. Beat 0 · One launch, two motions

Figure: CannonTrajectory-Beat0-Default.png

# The default launch: 100 m/s at 45 degrees
python main.py

# Console summary:
CannonTrajectory 1.3.1 (build 983dee8256ef) — 146 trajectory samples
Range (interpolated ground crossing): 1019.7 m
Maximum height (parabolic interpolation): 254.93 m
Flight time (interpolated ground crossing): 14.421 s

The default launch: one symmetric arch, highest halfway along, with the launch point marked. Equal physical scales on the two axes preserve the true parabolic shape.

What it shows

  • Gravity acts only vertically, so the sideways and vertical motions are independent; together they trace a parabola.
  • The improved-Euler method (the default) reproduces the exact sampled position at every stored point for this constant-acceleration problem.
  • The stored trajectory deliberately includes the first sample below ground; the printed range and flight time interpolate the actual crossing (Beat 5).

Headline values

  • 146 stored points, 0.1 s apart; printed range 1019.7 m, maximum height 254.93 m, flight time 14.421 s.
  • Exact (analytic) values: range 1019.72 m, height 254.929 m, time 14.4210 s (Beat 2).
  • The last stored point lies at x = 1025.305 m, h = −5.619 m, below and beyond the interpolated landing point.

2. Beat 1 · The angle splits the speed

Figure: CannonTrajectory-Beat1-Angle60.png

python main.py --angle_deg 60

# Console summary:
CannonTrajectory 1.3.1 (build 983dee8256ef) — 178 trajectory samples
Range (interpolated ground crossing): 883.09 m
Maximum height (parabolic interpolation): 382.39 m
Flight time (interpolated ground crossing): 17.662 s

The same 100 m/s launch at 60° instead of 45°: a taller, longer-lasting arch that still lands almost exactly where the complementary 30° launch does.

What it shows

  • The launch angle only decides how the fixed launch speed is shared between the sideways and vertical directions.
  • 30° and 60° are complementary: their analytic ranges are equal, although the arches themselves look very different.
  • 45° gives the greatest range of any angle at a fixed speed; steeper or shallower angles trade range for height or the reverse.

Headline values

  • 60°: printed range 883.09 m, height 382.39 m, time 17.662 s, from 178 samples.
  • 30° (same analytic range as 60°): printed range 883.1 m, height 127.46 m, time 10.197 s.
  • 0° (fired along the ground): 2 samples; range, height and flight time all print as 0.
  • 90° (straight up): 205 samples; height 509.86 m, time 20.394 s; range prints as 1.2488e−13 m, a floating-point artifact rather than a physical result.

3. Beat 2 · The exact solution

Figure: CannonTrajectory-Beat2-Speed200.png

python main.py --speed 200

# Console summary:
CannonTrajectory 1.3.1 (build 983dee8256ef) — 290 trajectory samples
Range (interpolated ground crossing): 4078.9 m
Maximum height (parabolic interpolation): 1019.7 m
Flight time (interpolated ground crossing): 28.842 s

Doubling the launch speed to 200 m/s at the default 45° angle: the same shape, scaled up by the square of the speed in range and height.

What it shows

  • With sideways speed constant and vertical speed changing at a steady rate, both motions can be solved exactly on paper (Eqs. 4 and 5).
  • Range and maximum height grow as the square of the launch speed; flight time grows only as the speed itself.
  • The printed numbers can be checked against the closed-form formulas, which is what makes the program's numerical results verifiable at all.

Headline values

  • 200 m/s: printed range 4078.9 m, height 1019.7 m, time 28.842 s, from 290 samples — about four times the default range and height, twice the default time.
  • 50 m/s (the other command shown in Beat 2): printed range 254.92 m, height 63.732 m, time 7.2104 s — about a quarter of the default range and height, half the time.
  • Default (100 m/s) exact values for comparison: R = 1019.72 m, H = 254.929 m, T = 14.4210 s.

4. Beat 3 · Stepping forward: Euler's method

Figure: CannonTrajectory-Beat3-EulerCoarse.png

python main.py --method euler --dt 0.5

# Console summary:
CannonTrajectory 1.3.1 (build 983dee8256ef) — 31 trajectory samples
Range (interpolated ground crossing): 1054.9 m
Maximum height (parabolic interpolation): 272.91 m
Flight time (interpolated ground crossing): 14.919 s

Forward Euler at a deliberately coarse 0.5 s timestep: the ball is placed too high throughout the flight because each step uses the vertical velocity from the step's start.

What it shows

  • Forward Euler's error has one sign throughout: the ball goes too high, stays up too long, and lands too far away.
  • The stored samples lie exactly on a parabola, but the wrong one — the exact trajectory of a launch with a small extra upward speed of ½gΔt.
  • Halving the timestep halves the error, which is what “first-order accurate” means.

Headline values

  • Δt = 0.5 s: printed range 1054.9 m (35.2 m, 3.5% too long), height 272.91 m (18.0 m too high), time 14.919 s (0.50 s too long), from 31 samples.
  • Repeating with Δt = 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.02 s gives printed ranges of 1033.8, 1026.8, 1023.2, 1021.1 m, with range errors of 14.1, 7.06, 3.53, and 1.41 m.
  • Each range error divided by its Δt is close to 70.7 m/s, the sideways launch speed u₀.

5. Beat 4 · A better step: improved Euler

Figure: CannonTrajectory-Beat4-ImprovedCoarse.png

python main.py --dt 1

# Console summary:
CannonTrajectory 1.3.1 (build 983dee8256ef) — 16 trajectory samples
Range (interpolated ground crossing): 1018.5 m
Maximum height (parabolic interpolation): 254.93 m
Flight time (interpolated ground crossing): 14.404 s

Improved Euler (Heun's method) at the same coarse 1 s timestep used for forward Euler in Beat 3: visually indistinguishable from the default fine-step run, even with 16 stored points instead of 146.

What it shows

  • Improved Euler averages the derivative at the start and the (trial) end of each step, instead of using only the start.
  • For this constant-acceleration problem, every stored sample of the improved method lies exactly on the true parabola, apart from rounding error, whatever the timestep is.
  • The small remaining range and time error at Δt = 1 s is not integration error; it is the straight-line landing estimate of Beat 5.

Headline values

  • Δt = 1 s, improved: printed range 1018.5 m (1.18 m short), height 254.93 m (exact), time 14.404 s (0.017 s short), from 16 samples.
  • Δt = 1 s, forward Euler (the other command shown in Beat 4): printed range 1089.3 m (69.6 m too long), height 291.51 m (36.6 m too high), time 15.405 s (0.98 s too long).
  • The improved method's range error at Δt = 1 s is about 1/59th of forward Euler's at the same timestep.

6. Beat 7 · Where the model and the run stop

Figure: CannonTrajectory-Beat7-Speed1000.png

python main.py --speed 1000

# Console summary:
CannonTrajectory 1.3.1 (build 983dee8256ef) — 1,444 trajectory samples
Range (interpolated ground crossing): 1.0197e+05 m
Maximum height (parabolic interpolation): 25493 m
Flight time (interpolated ground crossing): 144.21 s

A 1000 m/s launch at the default 45°: about 102 km downrange, far enough that the program's flat-ground, constant-gravity assumptions are only a rough guide.

What it shows

  • The program itself enforces one kind of limit: a max_steps ceiling too low to reach the ground stops the run with a clear message rather than returning an incomplete trajectory.
  • A second kind of limit is the model, not the program: over a range of about 102 km, Earth's curvature and the changing direction of gravity become significant, which the flat-ground, constant-g model does not represent.
  • A third kind of limit is numerical: settings that would produce a non-finite intermediate value are caught and reported, rather than silently printing infinity or not-a-number.

Headline values

  • 1000 m/s: printed range 1.0197e+05 m (≈ 102 km), height 25493 m, time 144.21 s, from 1,444 samples.
  • --max_steps 145 (too low for the default launch, which needs 146 stored points): prints an error and no summary.
  • --max_steps 146 (exactly enough): runs normally, with the same result as the Beat 0 default.
  • Over 102 km, Earth's surface curves away from a flat plane by about 816 m, and the direction of gravity turns by about 0.9°.

Notes for evaluation

  • The model uses conventional standard gravity, g = 9.80665 m/s², with no air resistance, wind, Earth curvature, rotation, or variation of gravity with altitude, except as an explicit point of discussion in Beat 7.
  • All five ordinary simulation inputs are command-line options: --speed, --angle_deg, --dt, --max_steps, and --method. Run python main.py --help for descriptions and defaults.
  • Beats 5 and 6 (not pictured) study the same kind of plot already shown for Beat 4: Beat 5 explains how the printed range, height and flight time are estimated between stored samples, and Beat 6 tabulates how those estimates and the two integrators' errors shrink as the timestep is reduced.
  • plot_cannon_overlay() accepts labeled (label, xs, hs) triples and supplies the shared axes, equal physical scaling, grid, launch marker, and legend; none of the six figures above uses it, since every pictured command is a single python main.py run, but comparing two or more of these trajectories on one figure (for example, several launch angles together) is a short Python script away.
  • The program prints interpolated range, parabolically interpolated maximum height, and interpolated flight time to five significant figures. The extra displayed digits support convergence comparisons and are not a claim of real-world ballistic accuracy.

Overview

Planck2 is a black-body-radiation investigation for Chapter 10. It plots the user's choice of spectral radiance per unit wavelength \(B_\lambda\), spectral radiance per unit frequency \(B_\nu\), or spectral energy density per unit frequency \(u_\nu\), using physical wavelength or frequency on the horizontal axis. Investigations 10.1, 10.2, and 10.3 are relevant to this program.

Besides the curve, the program prints and annotates the sampled peak, the area under the dimensionless shape function, and a physical integral over wavelength or frequency, next to the exact value that the integral would have if it ran from zero to infinity. Those last two numbers are what let you check the program, and the Stefan–Boltzmann \(T^4\) law, without any other tool.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence: every black-body spectrum is one universal curve \(x^p/(e^x-1)\) in the variable \(x=h\nu/kT\), so temperature only slides and scales it, the choice of what to call the spectrum (per wavelength, per frequency, or as an energy density) changes only the exponent \(p\) and the scale, and the printed comparison with the exact area is how you tell how far to trust a numerical sum over a finite grid. Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument, a command whose printed summary or figure illustrates it, and then the equation that the printed numbers follow from.

Scope: Planck2 evaluates the Planck law of an ideal black body. It does not model a real star's absorption lines, emissivity below one, or radiation transfer, and it does not fit measured spectra. Star models stellar structure and Random2 follows radiation diffusing through an opaque interior; Planck2 shows the thermal spectrum that a given temperature produces.
What this program is, and what it does not model. Planck2 is a teaching model of the spectrum of a perfect black body at one temperature. The 5900 K default approximates the Sun's continuum, but the Sun is not an ideal black body, and the curve has no absorption lines. The numbers are computed by adding up the curve on a grid of finite size, over a finite range, with two limiting formulas at the ends of that range, so each printed integral carries a numerical error. The exact reference values it prints describe the whole range from zero to infinity; the numerical results describe the finite range. Beats 6 to 8 show what those choices do to the numbers, and how the printed comparison exposes a bad choice.
Reading this page. The equations on this page are rendered by MathJax, loaded from a public CDN, so an internet connection is needed to see them typeset. If the CDN cannot be reached, the unrendered TeX source and its delimiters remain visible—for example, \(x=hc/(\lambda kT)\)—instead of a typeset formula. This has no effect on the program itself, which needs no internet access to run.

How to read this page

Each numbered section below is one beat: a short argument in words, a command whose output illustrates it, and then the equation that the output follows from. Do them in order the first time. After that, any beat can be opened on its own.

The program's output is a consequence of its equations, so what a beat shows is the equation at work, not independent evidence that Planck's law is right. The outside checks the program offers are the exact reference values that it prints beside its numerical integrals. Beats 4 to 8 compare the numerical results with them.

Every beat uses the same program and the same printed summary. What changes from beat to beat is the command. Beat 0 runs the default spectrum, Beat 1 changes only the temperature, Beat 2 changes what the curve is a density of, Beat 3 changes what the curve measures, Beat 4 adds up the area, Beat 5 asks how that sum depends on temperature, and Beats 6 to 8 open up the numerical method: the grid, the two approximation switches, and the cases where the default settings are not enough.

Run every command from the program's folder. Each run prints a five-line summary and then opens a plot window; close the window to end the program. Each beat names the lines of the summary to look at. The second summary line, Peak wavelength or Peak frequency, gives the physical position of the peak. The plot's annotation box repeats it in shorter form and adds Peak value, which the summary does not print. Temperatures are in kelvin, wavelengths in metres, frequencies in hertz, spectral radiance per wavelength in W m−3 sr−1, spectral radiance per frequency in W m−2 sr−1 Hz−1 and spectral energy density in J m−3 Hz−1. The integrals of the radiances are in W m−2 sr−1 and that of the energy density is in J m−3.

What kind of statement is each equation? Each displayed equation in the beats carries a tag. A red LAW tag marks a law of nature that the model adopts and does not derive; Eq. (1), Planck's law, is the only one. A grey DEFINITION tag marks a quantity that is defined by a formula. A blue DERIVED tag marks a result that follows from the equations already stated. A violet ALGORITHM tag marks a rule of the numerical method, which is a choice made by the program rather than physics: Eqs. (15), (17) and (18) to (20). A green ODE tag is used by other programs for a differential equation to be integrated; Planck2 has none. The tags are there so that a law, a definition and a numerical rule are never mistaken for one another. Twenty-one equations are numbered; a few unnumbered ones appear where they are needed.

Beat 0 · One curve for a hot body

A body at 5900 K, about the temperature of the Sun's visible surface, glows across the spectrum but most strongly in one band. Ask the program for its spectral radiance per unit wavelength.

python main.py

Look at the plot, at the first line of the summary (peak at x), at Dimensionless area and its bracketed exact value, and at the last two lines, Physical integral and Exact 0..infinity bolometric value. Look also at the second line, Peak wavelength, and, in the annotation box, at Peak value.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the peak. The second summary line gives Peak wavelength = 4.917033e-07 m, which is 491.70 nm, in the green part of the spectrum, and the annotation gives Peak value = 2.9281e+13 W m−3 sr−1. The summary line says the peak is at x = 4.959505, where x is a dimensionless number that the equations below define. Then read the comparison. Dimensionless area = 122.081167 and the bracketed exact value is also 122.081167, so the sum over the grid reproduces the exact area to the printed digits. Physical integral = 2.187105e+07 and Exact 0..infinity bolometric value = 2.187106e+07 W m−2 sr−1 differ in the seventh digit. Say which of the two areas has a physical unit, and what its unit, W m−2 sr−1, says it measures: radiance added up over the whole spectrum, which is the power per unit area of the surface and per unit solid angle, and not yet the power that leaves each square metre (Beat 4). The other area is a pure number. Then look at the plot: one hump, rising steeply on the short-wavelength side and falling slowly on the long side, drawn only where the curve is above 0.3% of its peak (the option --y_frac_window, Beat 8). Before reading on, write down what you expect to happen to the hump when the temperature is lowered.

Then the law that produced that curve. A body in thermal equilibrium with radiation emits, per unit area, per unit solid angle and per unit wavelength, a spectrum that depends only on its temperature.

(1)
Planck's law, per unit wavelength LAW
\[ B_\lambda(\lambda,T)\;=\;\frac{2hc^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\;\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda kT)}-1}. \]

Here \(h\) is Planck's constant, \(c\) the speed of light and \(k\) Boltzmann's constant, all exact SI values in planck2_physics.py. The wavelength enters only through the combination \(hc/(\lambda kT)\), the energy of a photon of that wavelength measured in units of the thermal energy \(kT\). The program names it \(x\).

(2)
The dimensionless variable DEFINITION
\[ x\;=\;\frac{hc}{\lambda kT}\;=\;\frac{h\nu}{kT}. \]

The second form is the same quantity written with the frequency \(\nu=c/\lambda\). The program's horizontal grid is uniform in \(x\), not in \(\lambda\); the conversion to a physical coordinate is x_to_wavelength() or x_to_frequency() in planck2_physics.py. Substituting Eq. (2) into Eq. (1) separates everything that depends on \(T\) from the curve itself. The curve is the shape function, and the rest is a prefactor.

(3)
The shape function DEFINITION
\[ f_p(x)\;=\;\frac{x^{p}}{e^{x}-1}. \]
(4)
Planck's law in the program's form DERIVED
\[ B_\lambda\;=\;\frac{2k^{5}T^{5}}{h^{4}c^{3}}\;f_{5}(x). \]

So \(B_\lambda\) is the fixed curve \(f_5\) multiplied by a constant that grows as \(T^5\). The program computes \(f_5\) on a grid of \(x\) values (shape_function()), multiplies by that constant (prefactor()) and records the result as the array y_values of the result. Beat 1 asks what changing \(T\) does to this picture.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 1 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 1 · Temperature slides the curve

Run the default spectrum at a cooler and a hotter temperature. The middle run is the default of Beat 0, written out so that the three are side by side.

python main.py --T 3000
python main.py --T 5900
python main.py --T 10000

Look, in each run, at peak at x in the first summary line and at Peak wavelength in the second, and in each annotation box at Peak value. The horizontal axis changes from run to run; look at its numbers, not only at the shape.

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the shapes in x. The summary says x = 4.959505 in all three runs, so the same point of the same curve is the peak at every temperature. Then compare the wavelengths. Peak wavelength is 9.670165e-07 m at 3000 K, 4.917033e-07 m at 5900 K and 2.901049e-07 m at 10000 K, so the peak moves to shorter wavelengths as the body is heated: infrared at 3000 K, green at 5900 K, ultraviolet at 10000 K. Multiply each wavelength by its temperature: 9.6702×10−7 × 3000, 4.9170×10−7 × 5900 and 2.9010×10−7 × 10000 all give 2.9010×10−3 m K, the same number. Then compare the heights. Peak value is 9.9525e+11, 2.9281e+13 and 4.0957e+14 W m−3 sr−1. The ratio of the first two is 29.42, which is (5900/3000)5. Write down a rule for how the peak height depends on temperature, and check it on the third run. The sampled 2.9010×10−3 m K is 0.11% above the exact constant of Eq. (6), 2.897772×10−3 m K; Beat 6 says where that difference comes from.

Then the reason. The curve \(f_5\) does not depend on temperature, so its peak is at one fixed value of \(x\). Setting the derivative of Eq. (3) to zero gives the condition that fixes it.

(5)
Where the shape function peaks DERIVED
\[ \frac{d f_p}{dx}=0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; x \;=\; p\,\bigl(1-e^{-x}\bigr), \qquad x_{5}=4.965114,\quad x_{3}=2.821439 . \]

The equation cannot be solved in closed form, but it can be solved by repeating \(x\leftarrow p(1-e^{-x})\) (Experiment 10). The \(p=5\) root, 4.965114, is the exact peak of \(B_\lambda\). Because Eq. (2) ties \(x\) to \(\lambda T\), a fixed \(x\) at the peak is a fixed \(\lambda T\).

(6)
Wien's displacement law DERIVED
\[ \lambda_{\max}\,T \;=\; \frac{hc}{k\,x_{5}} \;=\; 2.897772\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K}. \]

Setting \(\lambda_{\max}=hc/(x_5kT)\) makes the wavelength of the peak inversely proportional to \(T\), which is what the three runs show. For the 5900 K default, Eq. (6) gives 491.15 nm and the program prints 491.70 nm. The height follows from Eq. (4): at the fixed \(x_5\), \(B_\lambda\) is proportional to \(T^5\), which is the rule to write down from the three peak values. The peak of the plotted curve and the peak of the grid are not quite the same thing, which Beat 6 examines.

Beat 2 · Per wavelength or per frequency

Radiation can be sorted by wavelength or by frequency. Ask for the same 5900 K body sorted by frequency, and then for the cold radiation that fills the universe.

python main.py --T 5900 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 2.725 --quantity frequency

Look at peak at x and at Peak frequency, and in the annotation at Peak value; compare them with Beat 0. The second run is the cosmic microwave background, whose temperature is 2.725 K.

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the peaks in x. At 5900 K the frequency curve peaks at x = 2.809720, not at the 4.959505 of the wavelength curve in Beat 0. Then convert. Peak frequency = 3.454159e+14 Hz. The wavelength that goes with that frequency is c/ν = 2.9979×108/3.4542×1014 = 8.679×10−7 m, or 867.9 nm, in the near infrared, and not the 491.70 nm of Beat 0. The same light has two different “peaks”. Say why this is not a contradiction. Then read the second run. Peak frequency = 1.595353e+11 Hz, which is 159.54 GHz, in the microwave band, and Peak value = 3.8366e-18. That is the frequency at which the sky's background radiation is brightest.

Then the reason. A spectral density is power per unit of something. Per unit wavelength and per unit frequency are different somethings, and the same band of light has a different width in each.

(7)
Same power, different variable DEFINITION
\[ B_\nu\,\lvert d\nu\rvert \;=\; B_\lambda\,\lvert d\lambda\rvert, \qquad \Bigl\lvert\frac{d\lambda}{d\nu}\Bigr\rvert=\frac{c}{\nu^{2}} . \]
(8)
Planck's law per unit frequency DERIVED
\[ B_\nu \;=\; \frac{2h\nu^{3}}{c^{2}}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/kT}-1} \;=\; \frac{2k^{3}T^{3}}{h^{2}c^{2}}\;f_{3}(x). \]

Eq. (7) says that the power in a band is the same whichever variable is used to label it. Combining it with Eq. (1) and the relation \(\lvert d\lambda/d\nu\rvert=c/\nu^2\) removes two powers of \(\nu\) from the denominator, so the wavelength law with \(x^5\) becomes the frequency law with \(x^3\). The exponent in Eq. (3) is \(p=5\) for the wavelength quantity and \(p=3\) for the two frequency quantities, which is SHAPE_EXPONENT in planck2_physics.py. The peak of \(f_3\) is at \(x_3=2.821439\) by Eq. (5), and the physical frequency follows from Eq. (2).

(9)
Where the frequency curve peaks DERIVED
\[ \nu_{\max} \;=\; \frac{x_{3}\,k\,T}{h} \;=\; 5.8789\times10^{10}\ \text{Hz K}^{-1}\times T . \]

For 2.725 K this gives 160.20 GHz and for 5900 K it gives 3.4686×1014 Hz; the program's sampled values are a little lower, 159.54 GHz and 3.4542×1014 Hz, because its peak is a grid point (Beat 6). The two peaks satisfy \(\lambda_{\max}\nu_{\max}=c\,x_3/x_5=0.568\,c\), not \(c\), so \(c/\nu_{\max}\) is not \(\lambda_{\max}\). Beat 3 changes what is being measured and keeps the frequency.

Beat 3 · Radiance and energy density

The program has a third quantity, the energy stored in the radiation field. Run it beside the frequency radiance of Beat 2, and then at the temperature of the cosmic microwave background.

python main.py --T 5900 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 5900 --quantity energy_density
python main.py --T 2.725 --quantity energy_density

Look at peak at x, at Dimensionless area, at Physical integral and its units, at Peak frequency, and in the annotation box at Peak value.

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the shapes. The first two runs both print x = 2.809720 and Dimensionless area = 6.493935, and both summaries give Peak frequency = 3.454159e+14 Hz: the curves have the same shape and the same peak frequency. Then compare the scales. Peak value is 3.8940e-08 for the radiance and 1.6323e-15 for the energy density. Divide the second by the first: 4.192×10−8. Then look at the integrals. The radiance run prints 2.187105e+07 W m−2 sr−1 and the energy-density run prints 9.167666e-01 J m−3; their ratio is the same 4.192×10−8. Find a number built from \(\pi\) and \(c\) that equals it. The third run gives the energy density of the cosmic microwave background, 4.171735e-14 J m−3, the energy in each cubic metre of empty space that the radiation fills.

Then the relation. Radiance is a brightness in one direction, per unit solid angle; energy density is the energy per unit volume, summed over all directions.

(10)
Energy density from radiance DERIVED
\[ u_\nu \;=\; \frac{4\pi}{c}\,B_\nu . \]
(11)
Planck's law for energy density DERIVED
\[ u_\nu \;=\; \frac{8\pi k^{3}T^{3}}{h^{2}c^{3}}\;f_{3}(x). \]

For radiation that is the same in every direction, the energy in a volume is the power crossing it per unit area and solid angle, integrated over the full \(4\pi\) steradians of direction and divided by the speed \(c\) at which the radiation moves. That gives the factor \(4\pi/c=4.1917\times10^{-8}\) s m−1 sr, the number from the two ratios above. Eq. (11) is Eq. (8) multiplied by it, so the energy density has the same shape function \(f_3\), the same peak, and a different prefactor and unit. The prefactors are FREQUENCY_PREFACTOR_SCALE and ENERGY_DENSITY_PREFACTOR_SCALE in planck2_physics.py. The two frequency quantities differ only in scale, so the plotted curves look identical apart from the labels on the vertical axis. Beat 4 adds up the area under these curves.

Beat 4 · Adding up the spectrum

The Stefan–Boltzmann law is about what a black body radiates, and it comes from adding up the area under the spectrum. The program adds up two different areas, and it is easy to take the wrong one for the physical result. Run the wavelength curve and the frequency curve, and compare their printed integrals.

python main.py
python main.py --quantity frequency

Look, in each run, at Dimensionless area and the exact value in brackets, and at Physical integral and Exact 0..infinity bolometric value. Ignore the plots this time.

Three tasks, in order. First, compare the two dimensionless areas: 122.081167 for the wavelength curve and 6.493935 for the frequency curve, with brackets 122.081167 and 6.493939. They are the areas of \(f_5\) and \(f_3\) of Eq. (3) over the grid, and they are very different numbers. Then compare the two physical integrals. Both runs print 2.187105e+07 W m−2 sr−1, against 2.187106e+07 for the exact value. So the two curves, which look so different, have the same integrated radiance; the 122.08 is not a radiance or any other physical quantity. Then find the small difference. The dimensionless area of the frequency curve is 6.493935 against 6.493939, low by 4.5×10−6, or 7×10−7 of the total. The physical integral of the wavelength curve is low by the same fraction, 6.9×10−7, although its dimensionless area is not the same number as that of the frequency curve. Say why the physical integral of the wavelength curve follows the area of the other curve.

Then the areas. Their exact values, for the whole range from zero to infinity, are known.

(12)
Areas of the two shape functions DERIVED
\[ \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{3}}{e^{x}-1}\,dx=\frac{\pi^{4}}{15}=6.493939, \qquad \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{5}}{e^{x}-1}\,dx=\frac{8\pi^{6}}{63}=122.081167 . \]

The physical integral has to include the change of variable from \(x\) to wavelength or frequency, the coordinate Jacobian of coordinate_jacobian(). From Eq. (2), \(\lvert d\lambda\rvert=(hc/kT)\,x^{-2}\,dx\) and \(d\nu=(kT/h)\,dx\). Putting these into Eq. (4) and Eq. (8) gives the same integrand for both radiances.

(13)
Power in a band, in terms of x DERIVED
\[ B_\lambda\,\lvert d\lambda\rvert \;=\; B_\nu\,d\nu \;=\; \frac{2k^{4}T^{4}}{h^{3}c^{2}}\;f_{3}(x)\,dx . \]

The factor \(x^{-2}\) turns \(f_5\) into \(f_3\), which is why the wavelength curve's power follows the \(p=3\) area and not its own \(p=5\) area of 122.08. Integrating Eq. (13) with the first result of Eq. (12) gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law for the radiance, and Eq. (10) gives it for the energy density. The two radiance integrals are radiance added up over the spectrum, in W m−2 sr−1. The power that a black-body surface emits per square metre into the outward hemisphere is the flux, or exitance, \(M=\pi\) times that integral, in W m−2, which is the familiar \(\sigma T^4\); the factor \(\pi\) comes from adding the radiance over the hemisphere with the cosine of the angle to the surface normal. The program prints the radiance integral and not \(M\). The energy-density integral is stored energy per unit volume, in J m−3, and is not a power.

(14)
The Stefan–Boltzmann law DERIVED
\[ \int_{0}^{\infty}B_\lambda\,d\lambda=\int_{0}^{\infty}B_\nu\,d\nu=\frac{\sigma T^{4}}{\pi}, \qquad \int_{0}^{\infty}u_\nu\,d\nu=\frac{4\sigma}{c}\,T^{4}, \qquad M=\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}B_\nu\,d\nu=\sigma T^{4}, \qquad \sigma=\frac{2\pi^{5}k^{4}}{15\,h^{3}c^{2}} . \]

The last line of the summary is this exact value, computed by exact_physical_integral(). The program's own integral is a sum over the grid, which is the trapezoidal rule.

(15)
The trapezoidal rule on the grid ALGORITHM
\[ I \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tfrac12\bigl(F_{i-1}+F_i\bigr)\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta x=\frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}{N}, \qquad F=f_p(x)\ \text{or}\ y\,\Bigl\lvert\frac{d(\text{coordinate})}{dx}\Bigr\rvert . \]

The first choice of \(F\) gives Dimensionless area and the second gives Physical integral. Both are sums over \(N\) intervals, with \(N\) the option --n_steps, taken between the same \(x_{\min}\) and \(x_{\max}\). Neither is an integral to infinity, so neither can equal its exact value exactly. Beats 6 to 8 look at what that costs.

Beat 5 · Doubling T multiplies the integral by 16

The exact integrals of Eq. (14) depend on the temperature as \(T^4\). Check that the program's sum does too, with three temperatures for each of the frequency quantities. Each run doubles the temperature of the one before.

python main.py --T 3000 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 6000 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 12000 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 3000 --quantity energy_density
python main.py --T 6000 --quantity energy_density
python main.py --T 12000 --quantity energy_density

Look at Physical integral and at Exact 0..infinity bolometric value in each run. The dimensionless area and the peak position do not change from run to run; check that.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the radiance integrals: 1.461997e+06, 2.339196e+07 and 3.742713e+08 W m−2 sr−1 at 3000, 6000 and 12000 K. Divide each by the one before it: both ratios are 16.00, which is 24, and the ratio of the last to the first is 256.0. Then read the energy densities: 6.128240e-02, 9.805184e-01 and 1.568829e+01 J m−3, with the same ratios. Then divide the first radiance integral by 30004: 1.461997×106/8.1×1013 = 1.8049×10−8 W m−2 sr−1 K−4. Compare that with σ/π = 5.670374×10−8/π, which is the same number. Do the same for the energy density and 4σ/c = 7.5657×10−16 J m−3 K−4. Say what the ratio of the two constants, 4.19×10−8, is, and where it appeared in Beat 3.

Then the reason. Eq. (13) shows that once the integral is written in \(x\), the temperature appears only in a prefactor. The dimensionless part is the same number at every temperature, which is why the area and the peak did not change.

(16)
The temperature dependence of the power DERIVED
\[ I(T)=C\,T^{4}, \qquad \frac{I(T_2)}{I(T_1)}=\Bigl(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\Bigr)^{4}, \qquad C=\frac{\sigma}{\pi}\ \text{(radiance)}\ \text{or}\ \frac{4\sigma}{c}\ \text{(energy density)} . \]

The prefactor grows as \(T^4\), as Eq. (13) shows, and the quantity it multiplies does not depend on \(T\). So doubling the temperature multiplies the radiance integral by 16, and with it the emitted flux \(M=\pi I\) of Eq. (14), and a body ten times hotter radiates ten thousand times as much from each square metre. That is Stefan's law. The energy density of the last three runs is stored energy per unit volume and follows the same law. In the program this is a direct check on Physical integral: divide by T**4 and the temperature drops out. It checks the scaling and not the accuracy. The program uses the same dimensionless grid and switches at every temperature, so whatever error the sum has multiplies both integrals by the same factor, and the ratio comes out as 16 to floating-point precision even for a badly inaccurate sum. In Beat 7's run with the switches at 5 and 6 the integral is 6.6 times too large and the ratio is still 16. The accuracy of an integral is tested by the comparison with the exact reference line, which is what Beats 6 to 8 examine. The constant \(C\) of Eq. (16) belongs to the exact integral; the computed sum has a constant that differs from it by the grid, range and switch errors of those beats.

Beat 6 · The sampled grid

So far every run used the default grid of 2000 equal steps. The grid decides how well the sum follows the curve, and how well the peak is found. Change only --n_steps.

python main.py --n_steps 10
python main.py --n_steps 100
python main.py --n_steps 1000
python main.py --n_steps 2000
python main.py --n_steps 20000

Look at peak at x, Dimensionless area and Physical integral in each run, and compare them with the exact values 122.081167 and 2.187106e+07 that are printed with them. The 2000-step run is the default of Beat 0.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the sums. With 10 steps the dimensionless area is 45.259090 and the physical integral 1.521580e+06, about 7% of the true value: the grid step is 10 units of x, and the whole hump of the curve lies between the first two of its eleven grid points. With 100 steps the area is 122.083459 and the integral 2.185156e+07, low by 9×10−4; with 1000 steps the area is 122.081167 and the integral 2.187101e+07, low by 2.7×10−6; with 2000 the integral is 2.187105e+07, low by 6.9×10−7. Going from 1000 to 2000 steps divides the error by 3.9, close to 4. Then read the peak. The printed x is 10.009000, 5.009500, 5.009500, 4.959505 and 4.964504, against the exact 4.965114. The peak does not improve steadily: the 1000-step grid, ten times finer than the 100-step one, gives exactly the same peak, and the 2000-step grid happens to land closer than the 1000-step grid. Then read the 20000-step integral, 2.187107e+07, which is not below the exact value but slightly above it, by 8.7×10−8. Ten times more steps did not make the error ten times, or a hundred times, smaller. The next beat says where the remaining error comes from.

Then the grid itself. The program divides the range of \(x\) into \(N\) equal intervals, so the peak it reports is one of the grid points.

(17)
The grid, and the sampled peak ALGORITHM
\[ x_i=x_{\min}+i\,\Delta x,\quad i=0,\dots,N, \qquad \Delta x=\frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}{N}, \qquad \bigl\lvert x_{\rm peak}-x_{\rm exact}\bigr\rvert<\Delta x\quad\text{(only if the exact peak lies in the range and the switches leave the curve unchanged there)} . \]

The last bound holds only when the exact peak of the curve lies inside the sampled range and the two switches of Beat 7 leave the curve unchanged near it. Beat 8 shows a range that cuts the peak off: the sampled peak is then the end point of the range, 0.965 from the true peak on a step of 0.002. Beat 7 shows switches that reshape the curve near the peak: the sampled peak is then wherever the altered curve is largest.

With the default limits 0.01 and 100, \(\Delta x=(100-0.01)/N\), which is 9.999, 0.9999, 0.09999, 0.049995 and 0.0049995 for the five runs. The sampled peak is the first grid point with the largest value, with no interpolation, so it can be off by as much as one step (by less than half a step once the grid is fine enough for the curve to look symmetric about its peak), and it can only take values that the grid contains. That is why 100 and 1000 steps agree: the point \(x=5.0095\) is on both grids (\(0.01+5\times0.9999\) and \(0.01+50\times0.09999\)), and is the closest one to the peak on both. The 10-step grid has no point near the peak at all, and the largest of its eleven samples is at 10.009. The error of the trapezoidal sum of Eq. (15) falls as \(\Delta x^{2}\) once the step is small enough, which is the factor of about 4 from 1000 to 2000 steps, and it does not follow that rule at 100 steps, where the step is too large. The 0.11% error in Beat 1's \(\lambda T\) is this sampling: \(x=4.959505\) instead of 4.965114 makes the wavelength 0.11% too large. The limit on the number of steps is MAX_STEPS in planck2_driver.py, and one million steps is allowed.

The reason the error at 20000 steps is not smaller is not the grid. Beat 7 shows that the approximation switches and the finite range of \(x\) leave an error of about 7×10−8 that no grid can remove.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 7 in Suggested Experiments.

Beat 7 · The approximation switches

The program does not evaluate \(x^p/(e^x-1)\) directly across the whole range. For very small \(x\), \(e^x-1\) is nearly \(x\); for very large \(x\), \(e^x\) is so big that the −1 does not matter, and the exponential would overflow. Two options set where the program switches: --x_low and --x_high. The first command below turns both switches off. The second and third move them inwards, and the fourth moves them to the middle of the peak.

python main.py --x_low 0.01 --x_high 100
python main.py --x_low 1 --x_high 3
python main.py --quantity frequency --x_low 1 --x_high 3
python main.py --x_low 5 --x_high 6

Look at peak at x, Dimensionless area and its bracketed exact value, and Physical integral and the exact value below it. Compare each run with the default of Beat 0. In the annotation box of the last run, look at Peak value.

Three tasks, in order. First, read the run with no approximations. Dimensionless area = 122.081167 and Physical integral = 2.187105e+07 are the printed values of the default run, so at the default thresholds 0.05 and 20 the approximations cost nothing that is visible in seven digits. Then read the run with the thresholds at 1 and 3. The area is 121.281452, low by 0.66%, and the physical integral is 2.201527e+07, high by 0.66%. The frequency version of the same run has the area 6.536758, against 6.493939, high by 0.66%, and the same physical integral 2.201527e+07 as the wavelength version. Say why the physical integrals of the two quantities are exactly equal, when the two areas are not. Then read the last run, in which the thresholds sit on the two sides of the peak. The area is 689.653453, 5.6 times its exact value, and the physical integral is 1.444356e+08, 6.6 times its exact value. The printed peak, x = 4.959505, is the same as in Beat 0. Say why that is not evidence that the run is right. In the annotation, Peak value = 8.3555e+14 is 29 times the 2.9281e+13 of the default run.

Then what the program evaluates. It works with the logarithm of the shape function, which does not overflow, and replaces the difficult part by a simple formula at each end.

(18)
The shape function, evaluated as a logarithm ALGORITHM
\[ \ln f_p(x)=p\ln x-\ln\!\bigl(e^{x}-1\bigr), \qquad f_p=\exp\bigl(\ln f_p\bigr). \]
(19)
The Rayleigh–Jeans switch, for \(x<x_{\rm low}\) ALGORITHM
\[ e^{x}-1\approx x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_p\approx x^{\,p-1}, \qquad \frac{f_{\rm approx}}{f_p}=\frac{e^{x}-1}{x}\approx 1+\frac x2 . \]
(20)
The Wien switch, for \(x>x_{\rm high}\) ALGORITHM
\[ e^{x}-1\approx e^{x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_p\approx x^{\,p}e^{-x}, \qquad \frac{f_{\rm approx}}{f_p}=1-e^{-x}. \]

Between the thresholds the program uses expm1(x), which computes \(e^x-1\) accurately even for small \(x\). The Rayleigh–Jeans form always over-estimates the curve, by the fraction \(x/2\) at small \(x\); the Wien form always under-estimates it, by \(e^{-x}\). At the defaults these errors are 2.5% at \(x=0.05\), where the curve is very small, and \(2\times10^{-9}\) at \(x=20\), where it is smaller still, which is why the first run prints the same numbers as the default. Moving the switches to 1 and 3 puts errors of 72% and 5% at the switch points and gives the 0.66% of the second run. With the switches at 5 and 6 the Rayleigh–Jeans form is used all the way up to the peak, where it is wrong by a factor of 29; the sampled maximum then sits at the last grid point before \(x=5\), which happens to be the default peak's grid point, 4.959505, for the wrong reason. The two printed reference values are what catch this. Both physical integrals also account for the leftover of Beat 6. At the defaults the small-\(x\) form over-estimates the integral by 1.2×10−7 of its value, and the range starting at \(x_{\min}=0.01\) leaves out 5×10−8, so about +7×10−8 is left however fine the grid.

The physical integrals of the wavelength and frequency quantities are equal, in every one of these runs, because both are the integral of Eq. (13), the same function \(f_3\) of \(x\), evaluated on the same grid with the same switches. The wavelength area is the sum of \(f_5\) and the frequency area the sum of \(f_3\), which are different sums. The rule that the thresholds must satisfy, \(x_{\min}\le x_{\rm low}<x_{\rm high}\le x_{\max}\), is checked by the program before it computes anything. It is in PlanckDomain.validate() in planck2_physics.py.

Experiments that go with this beat: Experiment 8 in Suggested Experiments.

Then how much is left out. For the exact shape integrated continuously, restricting the range removes two tails. The printed sum also has grid and switch errors (Beats 6 and 7), which come on top of this.

(21)
What a finite range leaves out DERIVED
\[ \int_{x_{\min}}^{x_{\max}}f_p\,dx \;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}f_p\,dx \;-\;\underbrace{\int_{0}^{x_{\min}}f_p\,dx}_{\approx\,x_{\min}^{\,p}/p} \;-\;\underbrace{\int_{x_{\max}}^{\infty}f_p\,dx}_{\approx\,e^{-x_{\max}}\left(x_{\max}^{p}+p\,x_{\max}^{p-1}+\cdots+p!\right)} . \]

For \(x_{\max}=10\) the upper term is 8.05 for \(p=5\), which is 6.6% of 122.08, and 0.062 for \(p=3\), which is 0.96% of 6.494. The wavelength area is a sum of \(f_5\) and loses the first. The wavelength physical integral is the sum of \(f_3\) of Eq. (13) and loses the second, which is why the two are low by different amounts. For \(x_{\min}=1\) the lower term is the reverse. The integral of \(f_3\) from 0 to 1 is 0.2248, 3.5% of 6.494, while the integral of \(f_5\) from 0 to 1 is 0.1284, 0.1% of 122.08. The rule of thumb is that low \(x\) matters for the power and high \(x\) matters for the area of \(f_5\). The printed line Exact 0..infinity bolometric value is the number that shows how much a chosen range has left out. The peak line has no such check: it is the largest grid point in the range, and at \(x_{\max}=4\) that is an end point.

The input checks catch impossible ranges before any spectrum is computed: non-finite or non-positive numbers, ranges that do not have \(x_{\min}<x_{\max}\), thresholds outside the range, an --n_steps outside 1 to 1,000,000, and a --y_frac_window outside 0 to 1. A range and resolution that cannot produce a representable positive step, or values outside the floating-point range, are rejected by the driver with a message that begins Planck2 input/model error.

Algorithm

This section collects in one place the rules of the numerical method that Beats 4 to 8 introduced one at a time. The trapezoidal rule is Eq. (15) in Beat 4, the grid and the peak are Eq. (17) in Beat 6, and the switches are Eqs. (18) to (20) in Beat 7.

One run

  1. Validate the temperature, the quantity, the domain and the resolution.
  2. Divide \([x_{\min},x_{\max}]\) into exactly n_steps intervals, giving n_steps + 1 grid points, Eq. (17). The last point is exactly x_max.
  3. At each grid point evaluate \(\ln f_p\) with the Rayleigh–Jeans, expm1 or Wien formula that applies there, Eqs. (18) to (20), and exponentiate.
  4. Multiply by the prefactor of the selected quantity, Eqs. (4), (8) and (11), to obtain \(B_\lambda\), \(B_\nu\) or \(u_\nu\); convert \(x\) to the physical wavelength or frequency, Eq. (2).
  5. Record the first grid point with the largest shape-function value as the sampled peak.
  6. Apply the trapezoidal rule, Eq. (15), between neighbouring grid points only, accumulating both the dimensionless area and the physical integral, the latter with the coordinate Jacobian of Eq. (13).
  7. Report the exact references of Eqs. (12) and (14) beside the sums.

Plot windowing

A uniform grid in \(x\) maps non-uniformly into wavelength or frequency, so the plotter sorts the points by physical coordinate. By default it shows only the region where the curve exceeds the fraction --y_frac_window, 0.003, of its peak, with a 5% margin, so the hump fills the plot. Integration and the peak search always use the full \(x\)-range. Set --y_frac_window 0 to display the whole computed coordinate range, which then ends exactly at its first and last points.

Printed Summary

Every run prints these five lines before it opens the plot. The numbers are those of the default run of Beat 0.

Planck2 1.3.0 (build c7f04ac72375) — peak at x = 4.959505
Peak wavelength = 4.917033e-07 m
Dimensionless area = 122.081167 (0..infinity exact value: 122.081167)
Physical integral = 2.187105e+07 W m^-2 sr^-1
Exact 0..infinity bolometric value = 2.187106e+07 W m^-2 sr^-1
  • peak at x: the grid point with the largest shape-function value, within one grid step of the exact peak, Eq. (17), provided that peak lies inside the range and the switches leave the curve unchanged there. It is the edge of the range if the true peak lies outside it (Beat 8), and it can sit at the last grid point below --x_low if a switch reshapes the curve (Beat 7).
  • Peak wavelength or Peak frequency: the physical coordinate of that grid point, in metres or hertz according to the quantity, converted from \(x\) by Eq. (2). It is what Wien's law of Beats 1 and 2 is about.
  • Dimensionless area: the trapezoidal sum of \(f_p\) over the grid, Eq. (15), with the exact 0 to infinity area of Eq. (12) in brackets. It is the area under the shape function and is not a radiance or a power (Beat 4).
  • Physical integral: the trapezoidal sum of the plotted spectrum over its physical coordinate, using the Jacobian of Eq. (13). Its units follow the quantity: W m−2 sr−1 for the two radiances, which is radiance added up over the spectrum and not the emitted flux \(M=\pi\) times it, and J m−3 for the energy density, which is stored energy per volume.
  • Exact 0..infinity bolometric value: the Stefan–Boltzmann reference of Eq. (14), \(\sigma T^4/\pi\) or \(4\sigma T^4/c\). The physical integral covers only the range \(x_{\min}\) to \(x_{\max}\), so the two differ by the truncation, grid and approximation errors of Beats 6 to 8. At the defaults, and in each of the three quantity modes, the relative difference is under \(10^{-6}\).

The plot's annotation box has six lines: Peak at x, Peak λ (or Peak ν, the physical coordinate of the peak), Peak value, ∫ f(x) dx (the dimensionless area), Physical integral and Exact 0..∞ physical. Printed digits in the sampled peak are not an uncertainty estimate; peak-location accuracy is limited by the grid step, Eq. (17).

Suggested Experiments

The experiments are ranked from introductory to advanced, and each is cited by the beat it extends. Each command is run from the program's folder.

1 · Solar Black-Body Approximation — Introductory

Compare the sampled wavelength peak at 5900 K with Wien's displacement law. It should be near 490 nm. A real solar spectrum also has absorption lines, and the Sun's effective temperature is nearer 5800 K, which moves the peak to slightly longer wavelength.

python main.py --T 5900 --quantity wavelength

2 · Wien's Displacement Law and Temperature — Introductory

Verify that x_peak stays nearly constant while \(\lambda_{\rm peak}T\) stays near \(2.898\times10^{-3}\,\text{m K}\). Compare frequency peaks at different temperatures to see that they are proportional to \(T\).

python main.py --T 3000 --quantity wavelength
python main.py --T 5900 --quantity wavelength
python main.py --T 10000 --quantity wavelength
python main.py --T 3000 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 10000 --quantity frequency

3 · Cosmic Microwave Background — Introductory

The 2.725 K frequency peak should be near 160 GHz. As an optional research extension, consider why the background's nearly ideal black-body spectrum supports a hot early universe.

python main.py --T 2.725 --quantity frequency

4 · Wavelength vs. Frequency Peaks — Intermediate

At the same temperature, record the \(B_\lambda\) peak wavelength, the \(B_\nu\) peak frequency, and \(c/\nu_{\max}\). The two wavelengths differ because the curves are densities with respect to different variables.

python main.py --T 5900 --quantity wavelength
python main.py --T 5900 --quantity frequency

5 · Frequency Radiance vs. Energy Density — Intermediate

Verify that x_peak and the peak frequency agree. Compare the plotted peak values and the printed physical integrals, and explain why their ratios equal \(4\pi/c\), with the corresponding change of units.

python main.py --T 5900 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 5900 --quantity energy_density

6 · Stefan–Boltzmann T⁴ Scaling — Intermediate

Record the printed physical integrals, divide by T**4, and compare with \(\sigma/\pi\) for the radiance or \(4\sigma/c\) for the energy density. Doubling \(T\) should increase the integral by a factor of 16. Multiply the radiance integral by \(\pi\) to get the emitted flux \(\sigma T^4\) in W m−2.

python main.py --T 3000 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 6000 --quantity frequency
python main.py --T 12000 --quantity frequency
# Repeat with --quantity energy_density to compare 4σ/c.

7 · Resolution and Peak Sampling — Intermediate

Compare the printed x_peak and the dimensionless area with the exact values printed beside them. For the physical integral, remember that its exact reference spans \(0<x<\infty\) while the numerical result uses the finite range. The sampled peak need not converge at the same rate as the trapezoidal area.

python main.py --n_steps 100
python main.py --n_steps 1000

8 · Rayleigh–Jeans and Wien Limits — Intermediate

9 · Truncating the Range — Intermediate to Advanced

Predict from Eq. (21) how much each printed integral will lose when the range is cut at \(x_{\max}=10\), then run it and compare. Repeat with \(x_{\min}=1\) and with \(x_{\max}=4\), and say for each which of the three quantities is hurt most and why the peak line cannot warn you.

10 · The Peak Condition by Iteration — Advanced Programming

Solve Eq. (5) by repeating \(x\leftarrow p(1-e^{-x})\) and compare the roots with the sampled peaks of a very fine grid. The script imports the driver directly, so run it from the program's folder.

import math
from planck2_driver import run_planck2

for quantity, p in (("wavelength", 5), ("frequency", 3)):
    x = float(p)
    for _ in range(50):
        x = p * (1.0 - math.exp(-x))
    sampled = run_planck2(5900.0, quantity, 100000).x_peak
